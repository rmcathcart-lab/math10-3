Volume of Prisms and Cylinders

How much fits in a box, a can or a cake pan? How much concrete for a patio? Today we count the cubes that fill a space, find volumes with one big idea: area of the base × height, change cubic units, and order material for a job.

1

What Is Volume?

The volume of an object is the amount of space it takes up. For a box, a tank or a truck, it also tells you how much fits inside. We measure volume by counting how many cubes would fill the space.

1 cm³ (cubic centimetre): a cube 1 cm on each edge, about the size of a sugar cube.
1 in³ (cubic inch): a cube 1 in on each edge, about the size of a small ice cube.
1 m³ (cubic metre): a cube 1 m on each edge, about as tall as a kitchen counter. ft³ and yd³ are used for big jobs too.

The small 3 means cubed: three lengths were multiplied. Say “cubic centimetres” for cm³.

A rectangular prism is a box shape with 6 rectangle faces. To count its cubes, count the cubes in the bottom layer, then multiply by the number of layers.

Key idea   Rectangular prism: V=l\times w\times h  (length × width × height)
Cubes in the bottom layer = l\times w = the area of the base. Number of layers = h. So V = (area of base) × height.
!
Watch out. All three lengths must be in the same unit before you multiply. Volume has a cubed unit (cm³, ft³). Area has a squared unit (cm²), and length has no small number (cm).
Think A box is 2 cm by 3 cm by 4 cm. You make it twice as tall. What happens to the volume?
It doubles: twice as many layers. 2\times3\times4=24 cm³ becomes 2\times3\times8=48 cm³.

Volume of Any Prism

A prism is a 3-D shape with two matching ends, called the bases, joined by flat rectangle sides. A prism is the same shape all the way through, like a loaf of bread: every slice is the same. A prism is named for its base: a triangular prism has triangle bases.

The idea from Section 1 works for every prism: find the area of the base, then multiply by how far the prism goes.

Key idea   Any prism: V = (area of base) × height of the prism
Step 1: Find the area of the base (the end shape) with an area formula. Triangle: A=\tfrac12bh.
Step 2: Multiply by the height of the prism (the distance from one base to the other; for a prism lying down, its length).
!
Watch out. A triangular prism has two “heights”: the height of the triangle (use it for the base area) and the length of the prism. Use each one once. And the base is the end shape, not always the bottom.

Why the ½? A triangular prism is half a box

Put the triangle inside a rectangle with the same base and height. The triangle is half the rectangle, so the prism is half the box.

Example 3

The chocolate bar box

A chocolate bar comes in a box shaped like a triangular prism. Each triangle end has a base of 6 cm and a height of 4 cm. The box is 15 cm long. Find the volume of the box.
Step 1: area of the base (triangle) =\tfrac12\times6\times4=12 cm²
Step 2: V=12\times15=180 cm³
The box has a volume of 180 cm³.
Example 4

A concrete wheelchair ramp

A concrete wheelchair ramp is a triangular prism lying on its side. The triangle (the side of the ramp) is 3.6 m along the ground and 0.3 m high. The ramp is 1.2 m wide.

(a) How much concrete is in the ramp?
The base is the triangle at the side.
Area of base =\tfrac12\times3.6\times0.3=0.54 m²
V=0.54\times1.2=0.648 m³
The ramp needs 0.648 m³ of concrete.
(b) Concrete costs $245.00 per m³. What does the concrete cost?
0.648\times\$245.00=\$158.76
The concrete costs $158.76.
3

Volume of a Cylinder

A cylinder has two matching circle bases joined by a curved side, like a can. A cylinder is the same all the way up, so the prism idea still works: area of base × height. The base is a circle, with area \pi r^2.

Key idea   Cylinder: V=\pi r^2h  (area of the circle base × height)
r is the radius (centre to edge). If you know the diameter (all the way across), r=d\div2.
Estimate with \pi\approx3 and rounded numbers to check your answer.
For r=3.7 and h=10.2: π × 3.7 x² × 10.2 ENTER shows 438.68… The x² key squares only the number just before it.
!
Watch out. r^2 means r\times r, not r\times2. And square the radius, not the diameter: halve the diameter first.
Think Can P has twice the radius of Can Q. Can R has twice the height of Can Q. Which holds more, P or R?
Can P. The radius is squared: twice the radius gives 2\times2=4 times the base area, so 4 times the volume. Twice the height only doubles it.
Example 5

A soup can and hay bales

(a) A soup can is 7.4 cm across (diameter) and 10.2 cm tall. Estimate its volume, then calculate it to the nearest tenth.
r=7.4\div2=3.7 cm
Estimate: 3\times4^2\times10=3\times16\times10=480 cm³
Calculate: V=\pi\times3.7^2\times10.2=438.68\ldots\approx438.7 cm³
This is close to the estimate, so it is reasonable.
(b) A round hay bale is a cylinder 5 ft across and 4 ft wide (its “height” when it stands on end). Find its volume to the nearest hundredth.
r=5\div2=2.5 ft
V=\pi\times2.5^2\times4=\pi\times6.25\times4
V=78.539\ldots\approx78.54 ft³
One bale has a volume of about 78.54 ft³.
(c) A farmer has 60 of these bales. What is their total volume, to the nearest cubic foot?
60\times78.539\ldots=4712.38\ldots\approx4712 ft³   The 60 bales take up about 4712 ft³.
Example 6

Marisol’s cake pans

Marisol has a cake recipe for one 9 in round pan that is 2 in deep. She only owns an 8 in by 8 in square pan, also 2 in deep.

(a) Find the volume of each pan, to the nearest hundredth. Will the batter fit in the square pan?
Round: r=9\div2=4.5 in
V=\pi\times4.5^2\times2\approx127.23 in³
Square: V=8\times8\times2=128 in³
The two pans hold almost the same amount, so the batter will fit in the square pan.
(b) A 9 in by 13 in pan, 2 in deep, holds how many round pans of batter? Round to the nearest tenth.
V=9\times13\times2=234 in³
234\div127.23\approx1.8
It holds about 1.8 round pans of batter (a little less than 2).

Will the batter fit? Pour it and see

All three pans are 2 in deep, so the pan with the bigger base holds more.

Converting Cubic Units

How many cubic inches are in a cubic foot? A foot is 12 in, so a 1 ft cube is 12 in long, 12 in wide and 12 in high. Its bottom layer has 12\times12=144 cubes, and there are 12 layers.

Key idea
Within a system (exact)
1 ft³ = 12\times12\times12=1728 in³
1 yd³ = 3\times3\times3=27 ft³
1 m³ = 100\times100\times100=1\,000\,000 cm³
Between SI and imperial (rounded)
1 m³ ≈ 1.31 yd³    1 yd³ ≈ 0.765 m³
1 m³ ≈ 35.3 ft³
Big unit → small unit: multiply.    Small unit → big unit: divide.
!
Watch out. 1 ft = 12 in, but 1 ft³ is not 12 in³. The 12 is used three times (length, width and height). In the same way, 1 m³ is not 100 cm³.

Multiply or divide? Ask which unit is bigger

A big unit holds many small units. Changing to the small unit gives more of them, so the number gets bigger: multiply.

Another way: change the lengths first

You can also change each length to the unit you want, before you find the volume. This is often easier, and it works between SI and imperial too (1 in = 2.54 cm, 1 ft = 30.48 cm, 1 yd = 0.9144 m).

Example 8

The toy chest, two ways

A toy chest is 30 in long, 18 in wide and 16 in high. Find its volume in cubic feet two ways.

(a) Find the volume in in³, then change it to ft³.
V=30\times18\times16=8640 in³
8640\div1728=5 ft³
The toy chest holds 5 ft³.
(b) Change each length to feet first (÷ 12), then find the volume.
30\div12=2.5 ft, 18\div12=1.5 ft, 16\div12=1\tfrac13 ft
V=2.5\times1.5\times1\tfrac13=5 ft³
(Calculator: 2.5\times1.5\times16\div12=5.) Both ways give 5 ft³.

Volume on the Job: Concrete, Soil and Gravel

Concrete, gravel, sand, topsoil and mulch are sold by volume: by the cubic yard or the cubic metre. On a job site, “a yard of concrete” means 1 yd³. The thickness of a slab or a layer is usually small (inches or centimetres), so change it to feet or metres first: 4\text{ in}=4\div12 ft, 10\text{ cm}=0.1 m.

Key idea   Ordering material
1Write all the lengths in the same unit (feet or metres).2Find the volume.3Change to the unit the supplier sells (ft³ ÷ 27 = yd³).4Round up to an amount you can buy.5Find the cost.
!
Watch out. Always round up when ordering. If you round down, the job comes up short, and a second small delivery costs extra.
Thickness 4 in: let the calculator do the ÷ 12. 12 × 10 × 4 ÷ 12 ENTER shows 40 (ft³).
Example 9

Tyrell’s patio

Tyrell is pouring a concrete patio 12 ft long and 10 ft wide. The slab will be 4 in thick. Concrete is sold in half cubic yards (1, 1.5, 2, …) and costs $215.00 per yd³.

(a) Find the volume of the slab in ft³.
4 in =4\div12=\tfrac13 ft
V=12\times10\times\tfrac13
V=40 ft³
(b) Change it to yd³. How much should Tyrell order?
40\div27=1.48\ldots yd³
Round up to the next half yard.
He should order 1.5 yd³.
(c) What will the concrete cost?
1.5\times\$215.00=\$322.50
The concrete will cost $322.50.
Example 10

Gravel for a farm driveway

A farm driveway is 15 m long and 3.5 m wide. It needs a layer of gravel 10 cm deep. The gravel pit sells only whole cubic yards, at $48.00 per yd³. Use 1 m³ ≈ 1.31 yd³.

(a) Find the volume of gravel in m³.
10 cm =0.1 m
V=15\times3.5\times0.1=5.25 m³
(b) How many cubic yards should be ordered, and what will they cost?
5.25\times1.31=6.8775 yd³
Round up to 7 yd³.
7\times\$48.00=\$336.00. Order 7 yd³, for $336.00.

Working backwards

If you know the volume and the base, you can find the height (or thickness, or depth). Undo the multiplication by dividing.

Key idea   V=(\text{area of base})\times h   so   h=V\div(\text{area of base})
For a box: h=V\div(l\times w)
Example 11

Mia’s shed slab

Mia has 0.9 m³ of concrete left in the truck. She uses it all to pour a slab for a shed, 3 m by 2.5 m. How thick will the slab be, in centimetres? Check your answer.
Area of base =3\times2.5=7.5 m²
h=0.9\div7.5=0.12 m =12 cm
Check: 3\times2.5\times0.12=0.9 m³ ✓   The slab will be 12 cm thick.
✓

Key ideas

Volume is the space inside, counted in cubes (cm³, in³, ft³, yd³, m³). Box: V=l\times w\times h, all lengths in the same unit.
Any prism or cylinder: V = (area of base) × height. Triangle base: \tfrac12bh. Cylinder: V=\pi r^2h, with r=d\div2. Estimate with \pi\approx3.
Cubic units: 1 ft³ = 1728 in³, 1 yd³ = 27 ft³, 1 m³ = 1 000 000 cm³. Big → small: multiply. Small → big: divide. Or change the lengths first.
On the job: same units, volume, change to the unit sold, round up, find the cost. Backwards: h=V\div(\text{area of base}).
