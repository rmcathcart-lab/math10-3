The Ice Cube Race

Two ice cubes come out of the same freezer.

One goes on a metal baking pan. One goes on a wooden cutting board. The pan and the board have sat on the counter all day.

Which ice cube melts first?

?
Vote: metal, wood, or a tie? Then touch a metal table leg and a wooden desk.
The one on the metal pan melts first, by a lot! The pan and the board are the same temperature (room temperature). Metal only feels colder because it pulls heat out of your warm hand quickly. In the same way, it pushes room heat into the ice quickly. Wood moves heat slowly. “Feels cold” is not the same as “is cold”. A thermometer tells the truth, and today we read two kinds.

Canada says 25 degrees. A US weather app says 77 degrees. Same afternoon! Today we read the two temperature scales, change from one to the other with a formula, estimate in our heads, and compare temperatures and temperature changes.

Two Temperature Scales

Temperature tells how hot or cold something is. Canada uses degrees Celsius (°C), the SI scale. The United States uses degrees Fahrenheit (°F), the imperial scale. You see Fahrenheit on US weather reports, US recipes and ovens, and on some tools and manuals made in the US.

A double thermometer shows both scales side by side. Each height on it is one temperature with two names.

Key idea   Referents (temperatures to remember)

From freezing to boiling is 100 Celsius degrees but 180 Fahrenheit degrees. So a Celsius degree is bigger:

A change of 1 Celsius degree is a change of 1.8 Fahrenheit degrees.

The scales also start in different places: water freezes at 0 on one and at 32 on the other.

Math History1742

The Celsius scale started upside down

In 1742, a Swedish astronomer named Anders Celsius made a scale with 100 degrees between freezing and boiling.

But on his scale, water boiled at 0 and froze at 100! Colder days had bigger numbers.

Celsius died in 1744. Not long after, the scale was flipped to the way we use it today. Some people give the scientist Carl Linnaeus the credit.

On the old upside-down scale, what number would a 20 °C room be?
The room is 20 degrees up from freezing. On the old scale freezing was 100 and you count down as it gets warmer: 100-20=80. The room would be 80 degrees.

Celsius to Fahrenheit

To change °C to °F, multiply by 1.8 (Fahrenheit degrees are smaller, so there are more of them), then add 32 (because water freezes at 32 °F, not 0).

Key idea   Celsius to Fahrenheit:   F=1.8\times C+32   (the same as F=\tfrac95C+32, because \tfrac95=1.8)
Step 1: Multiply the °C temperature by 1.8.    Step 2: Add 32.
1.8 × 25 + 32 ENTER shows 77, so 25 °C = 77 °F. For a temperature below zero, use the negative key (−), not the subtract key −: 1.8 × (−) 15 + 32 ENTER shows 5.

Why × 1.8, then + 32? Climb the thermometer

Start at freezing: 0 °C is 32 °F. Every Celsius degree up is 1.8 Fahrenheit degrees up. So climb 1.8 for each Celsius degree, starting from 32.

Think Without a calculator: what is 5 °C in °F? (Hint: 5 Celsius degrees is half of 10.)
10 Celsius degrees is 18 Fahrenheit degrees, so 5 is 9. Start at 32: 32+9=41. So 5 °C = 41 °F.

Amira’s hotel room

Amira’s family is staying at a hotel in Montana. The thermostat in the room is in °F.

(b) Her phone says it is -8 °C at home in Calgary. Her cousin in Montana asks what that is in °F. What should Amira tell her?
F=1.8\times(-8)+32
F=-14.4+32=17.6
It is about 17.6 °F (about 18 °F) in Calgary.
3

Fahrenheit to Celsius

To go the other way, undo the steps in reverse order: first subtract 32, then divide by 1.8.

Key idea   Fahrenheit to Celsius:   C=(F-32)\div1.8   (the same as C=\tfrac59(F-32))
Step 1: Subtract 32 from the °F temperature.    Step 2: Divide by 1.8.
Use brackets so the calculator subtracts first: ( 98.6 − 32 ) ÷ 1.8 ENTER shows 37.
!
Watch out. Without the brackets, 98.6 − 32 ÷ 1.8 ENTER divides first (order of operations) and shows 80.82…, which is wrong. Unless a question says otherwise, round temperatures to the nearest tenth of a degree.

Why brackets? The calculator’s order

The calculator always divides before it subtracts (order of operations). Brackets tell it to subtract first. A referent shows which answer makes sense.

Think Why do we subtract 32 first when we change °F to °C?
Changing °C to °F does × 1.8 first, + 32 last. To undo it, take off the last step first (− 32), then undo the first step (÷ 1.8). Like taking off your boots before your socks.

Lucas bakes from a US recipe

Lucas is baking his aunt’s cookie recipe from Arizona. It says “Bake at 375 °F”. His oven is in °C and can be set in steps of 5 degrees.

C=(375-32)\div1.8

He should set the oven to 190 °C (the closest step).

Estimating in Your Head

You will not always have a calculator, for example when a friend tells you a US weather forecast. These quick rules round 1.8 up to 2 and 32 down to 30:

Key idea   Quick estimates:
F\approx2\times C+30double, then add 30
C\approx(F-30)\div2subtract 30, then halve

The estimate is very close for ordinary weather. It gets worse for very hot or very cold temperatures. For cooking, medicine or a job, use the exact formula.

Think A friend in Texas says it is 68 °F. Estimate in °C in your head. Then check with the formula.
Estimate: (68-30)\div2=38\div2=19 °C. Exact: (68-32)\div1.8=36\div1.8=20 °C. Only 1 degree off: a nice room-temperature day.

Comparing Temperatures and Temperature Changes

To compare two temperatures, change them to the same scale first.

A temperature change is how much the temperature goes up or down. Subtract: higher − lower. With a negative temperature, count up to 0 and then past it: from -21 up to 7 is 21+7=28 degrees.

To change a temperature change to the other scale, only multiply or divide by 1.8. Do not add or subtract 32: the 32 is about where the scales start, and a change does not depend on where they start.

Key idea   Change in °F = 1.8 × change in °C     Change in °C = change in °F ÷ 1.8
-40 is the same on both scales:   1.8\times(-40)+32=-72+32=-40.

Where the two scales meet: -40

Go down 10 Celsius degrees at a time. Fahrenheit goes down 18 each time, so it catches up by 8. At -40 the two scales say the same number.

Think Is there any other temperature where °C and °F show the same number?
No. Above -40, °F is always the bigger number. Below -40, °F is always the smaller number. They only meet once.
Example 7

Same scale first

(a) On the same morning it is -25 °C in Edmonton and -15 °F in Minneapolis. Which city is colder, and by how much?
Change -15 °F to °C:
C=(-15-32)\div1.8=-47\div1.8\approx-26.1 °C
-26.1 is lower than -25, so Minneapolis is colder, by about 1.1 Celsius degrees.
(b) Food safety rules say a fridge should be at 4 °C or colder. A US-made fridge thermometer reads 38 °F. Is the fridge cold enough?
C=(38-32)\div1.8=6\div1.8\approx3.3 °C
3.3 °C is colder than 4 °C, so yes, the fridge is cold enough.

Same scale, side by side

Put both temperatures on one double thermometer. The lower mark is the colder one.

Extra example

A holiday in Florida

Kiara’s family from Lethbridge is going to Florida in March. All the signs and forecasts there are in °F.

(a) The hotel pool is 84 °F. What is that in °C, to the nearest tenth?
C=(84-32)\div1.8
=52\div1.8\approx28.9 °C
A warm pool.
(b) The forecast in Lethbridge says 32 °C next week. Her cousin in Florida asks for it in °F.
F=1.8\times32+32
=57.6+32=89.6 °F
Hot in Lethbridge too!
(c) In Florida the temperature drops 9 Fahrenheit degrees overnight. How many Celsius degrees is that?
A change: only divide by 1.8.
9\div1.8=5
It drops 5 Celsius degrees. (No 32!)
Example 8

A chinook in Calgary

A chinook is a warm, dry wind that blows down from the Rocky Mountains in winter. One morning in Calgary it is -21 °C. A chinook arrives, and by the afternoon it is 7 °C.

(a) By how many Celsius degrees did the temperature rise?
7-(-21)=7+21=28
It rose 28 Celsius degrees.
(b) By how many Fahrenheit degrees did it rise?
1.8\times28=50.4
It rose 50.4 Fahrenheit degrees.
(No +32: this is a change.)
(c) Check (b): change both temperatures to °F and subtract.
1.8\times(-21)+32=-5.8 °F
1.8\times7+32=44.6 °F
44.6-(-5.8)=50.4 ✓
!
Watch out. If you add 32 to the change, you get 50.4+32=82.4, which is far too big. Only actual temperatures (readings on a thermometer) use the +32 or -32.
✓

Key ideas

Referents: water freezes at 0 °C = 32 °F, boils at 100 °C = 212 °F; body 37 °C = 98.6 °F; room about 21 °C = 70 °F. 1 Celsius degree = 1.8 Fahrenheit degrees.
°C → °F: F=1.8\times C+32 (× 1.8, then + 32). Use (−) for a negative temperature.
°F → °C: C=(F-32)\div1.8 (− 32, then ÷ 1.8). Use brackets on the calculator. Round to the nearest tenth.
Estimate: double and add 30, or subtract 30 and halve. Compare on the same scale. A change is only × 1.8 or ÷ 1.8 (no 32). -40 °C = -40 °F.
