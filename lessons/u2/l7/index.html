Deductions and Net Pay: Reading a Pay Stub

You worked the hours, so why is your paycheque smaller than what you earned? Today we find every amount that comes off your pay, and we learn to check every line of a pay stub. It is your money!

I can…
  • find total deductions and net pay: gross pay − total deductions
  • find CPP, EI and income tax from their rates, rounded to the cent
  • find the percent of my gross pay I take home, and find a rate
  • use before-tax deductions to find my taxable income
  • complete and check a pay stub
N2 Demonstrate an understanding of income, including: wages, salary, contracts, commissions and piecework, to calculate gross pay and net pay.
1

Gross Pay, Deductions and Net Pay

Your gross pay is everything you earned in a pay period: wages, overtime, commission, bonuses. You never get all of it. Before you are paid, your employer takes some money off. Each amount taken off is a deduction.

The money that is left is your net pay, also called take-home pay. It is the amount that goes into your bank account. Each payday you also get a pay stub (or pay statement). It lists your gross pay, every deduction, and your net pay.

Key idea   Total deductions = add up every deduction   ·   Net pay = gross pay − total deductions
!
Watch out. Once deductions come off, net pay is always less than gross pay. When you plan how to spend or save, use your net pay. That is the money you actually get.
Example 1

Kai’s coffee shop pay

Kai works at a coffee shop in Cochrane. His gross pay for two weeks is $684.00. His deductions are CPP $40.70, EI $11.22, income tax $68.40, and $6.50 for his staff apron. Find his total deductions and his net pay.

Step 1: Find his total deductions.
\$40.70+\$11.22+\$68.40+\$6.50=\$126.82
Step 2: Find his net pay.
\$684.00-\$126.82=\$557.18
Kai’s deductions total $126.82, and his net pay is $557.18.
2

Required Deductions: CPP, EI and Income Tax

By law, your employer must take three deductions off your pay and send them to the government (the Canada Revenue Agency, or CRA):

CPPCanada Pension PlanMoney saved for when you retire. Your employer puts in the same amount as you.
EIEmployment InsuranceIf you lose your job (and it is not your fault), or you take time off for a new baby, EI pays you part of your wages for a while.
Income taxfederal and provincialPays for things like hospitals, schools and roads. Part goes to the federal government and part to the province.

Each one is a percent of your pay. In this lesson, every question gives you the rates to use. They are example rates.

i
Real life. Real CPP, EI and income tax amounts come from CRA payroll tables or an online payroll calculator. They follow yearly rules. For example, there is no CPP on the first part of your yearly income, there is a most you can pay each year, and income tax depends on how much you earn in the whole year. Many students with part-time jobs get some or all of their income tax back when they file a tax return in the spring.
Key idea   A deduction that is a percent: deduction = gross pay × rate (as a decimal)
CPP at 5.95%: gross pay × 0.0595.   Round each deduction to the nearest cent before you add the deductions.
!
Watch out. To change a percent to a decimal, move the decimal point 2 places left: 5.95\%=0.0595 (not 0.595), 1.64\%=0.0164, 15\%=0.15.
≈
Estimate. 1% of your pay: move the decimal point 2 places left. On $720, 1% is $7.20. CPP (about 6%) is about 6\times\$7.20\approx\$43. EI (less than 2%) is less than $14.40. If your answer is far from the estimate, check your decimal.

Estimate with 1%: count the little blocks

Cut the pay into 100 equal blocks. One block is 1% of the pay. CPP is about 6 blocks, EI is less than 2 blocks, and a 15% tax is 15 blocks.

Think Your pay stub says EI is $0.98 on a gross pay of $600. Is that possible at 1.64%?
No. 1% of $600 is $6, so 1.64% is between $6 and $12. $0.98 is much too small. Someone used 0.00164 (that is only 0.164%). The right EI is \$600\times0.0164=\$9.84.

Finding each deduction, one line at a time

Each percent deduction goes through the same machine: gross pay × rate as a decimal, then round to the cent. Fixed amounts go straight in. Then add the lines and subtract.

CPP on $1 240.00: 1240 × 0.0595 ENTER shows 73.78. EI: 1240 × 0.0164 ENTER shows 20.336, so EI is about $20.34.

Example 3

Noah’s garden centre job

Noah is 16. He works 18 hours one week at a garden centre in Calgary and earns Alberta’s student wage of $13.00 an hour (as of 2026; rates can change). His deductions are CPP 5.95%, EI 1.64% and income tax 6%.

(a) Find his gross pay, each deduction, and his net pay.
gross =18\times\$13.00=\$234.00
CPP =\$234.00\times0.0595=\$13.923\approx\$13.92
EI =\$234.00\times0.0164=\$3.8376\approx\$3.84
income tax =\$234.00\times0.06=\$14.04
total =\$13.92+\$3.84+\$14.04=\$31.80
net =\$234.00-\$31.80=\$202.20. Noah’s net pay is $202.20.
(b) Noah wants an e-scooter that costs $650.00 with tax. If he saves all his net pay, how many weeks will it take?
\$650.00\div\$202.20\approx3.2, so 3 weeks is not quite enough.
It will take Noah 4 weeks. (If he used his gross pay, he would think 3 weeks.)

Other Deductions and Percent of Gross Pay

Many jobs have other deductions. Some are a fixed dollar amount each payday and some are a percent:

Health and dental planYou and your employer share the cost.
Uniform or safety gearSometimes paid off over a few paycheques.
Charitable donationOne you choose, such as to a food bank.
Parkingor a staff social club.

Every deduction goes into the total deductions.

To compare pay, it helps to know what percent of your gross pay you keep. This is the same “part ÷ whole × 100” you used in Unit 1.

Key idea   Percent of gross pay
take-home percent =\dfrac{\text{net pay}}{\text{gross pay}}\times100deductions percent =\dfrac{\text{total deductions}}{\text{gross pay}}\times100
Finding a rate: rate =\dfrac{\text{deduction}}{\text{pay it was taken from}}\times100    The two percents add to 100%.
Think Job A pays $18.00 an hour and you keep 80%. Job B pays $20.00 an hour and you keep 70%. Which job puts more in your bank account for each hour?
Job A: \$18.00\times0.80=\$14.40 an hour. Job B: \$20.00\times0.70=\$14.00 an hour. Job A, even though its wage is lower.
Example 4

Sofia’s delivery van

Sofia drives a delivery van in Lethbridge. Her gross pay this week is $960.00. Her deductions are CPP 5.95%, EI 1.64%, income tax 16%, a health plan of $14.25, $20.00 toward her safety boots, and a $5.00 donation to the food bank. Find her net pay.

CPP =\$960.00\times0.0595=\$57.12  ·  EI =\$960.00\times0.0164=\$15.744\approx\$15.74  ·  income tax =\$960.00\times0.16=\$153.60
total =\$57.12+\$15.74+\$153.60+\$14.25+\$20.00+\$5.00=\$265.71
net =\$960.00-\$265.71=\$694.29. Sofia’s net pay is $694.29.
Example 5

What percent do I keep? What rate is that?

(a) Use Example 4. What percent of her gross pay does Sofia take home? What percent goes to deductions? Round to the nearest tenth.
take-home: \$694.29\div\$960.00=0.7232
0.7232\times100\approx72.3\%
deductions: 100\%-72.3\%=27.7\%
(check: \$265.71\div\$960.00\times100\approx27.7\%)
Sofia takes home about 72.3% of her gross pay.
(b) Marcus’s pay stub shows income tax of $97.20 on gross pay of $810.00. What income tax rate is he paying?
rate =\$97.20\div\$810.00=0.12
0.12\times100=12\%
Check: \$810.00\times0.12=\$97.20 ✓
Marcus pays income tax at 12%.
4

Before-Tax Deductions and Taxable Income

Some deductions come off before income tax is worked out. These are before-tax deductions: union dues (a fee for belonging to a union), a company pension plan, and RRSP savings (Registered Retirement Savings Plan) taken off at work. You pay income tax only on what is left. That amount is your taxable income.

Key idea   Taxable income = gross pay − before-tax deductions   ·   Income tax = taxable income × tax rate
CPP and EI are still worked out on the gross pay. If there are no before-tax deductions, taxable income = gross pay.
  1. Step 1 Find each before-tax deduction.
  2. Step 2 Taxable income = gross pay − before-tax deductions.
  3. Step 3 CPP and EI on the gross pay; income tax on the taxable income.
  4. Step 4 Add all the deductions.
  5. Step 5 Net pay = gross pay − total deductions.
!
Watch out. Before-tax deductions are still deductions. They come out of your pay, so count them in the total deductions.
Example 6

Ethan, carpentry apprentice

Ethan is a first-year carpentry apprentice in Edmonton. His gross pay for two weeks is $2 080.00. His before-tax deductions are union dues of 1.5% of gross pay and $50.00 into his RRSP. His other deductions are CPP 5.95%, EI 1.64%, and income tax 18% of his taxable income.

(a) Find his taxable income and his net pay.
Step 1: union dues =\$2\,080.00\times0.015=\$31.20; RRSP = $50.00
Step 2: taxable income =\$2\,080.00-\$31.20-\$50.00=\$1\,998.80
Step 3: CPP =\$2\,080.00\times0.0595=\$123.76; EI =\$2\,080.00\times0.0164=\$34.112\approx\$34.11
income tax =\$1\,998.80\times0.18=\$359.784\approx\$359.78
Step 4: total =\$31.20+\$50.00+\$123.76+\$34.11+\$359.78=\$598.85
Step 5: net =\$2\,080.00-\$598.85=\$1\,481.15
Ethan’s taxable income is $1 998.80 and his net pay is $1 481.15.
(b) How much less income tax does Ethan pay because of his before-tax deductions?
Tax on all of his gross pay: \$2\,080.00\times0.18=\$374.40
\$374.40-\$359.78=\$14.62
He pays $14.62 less income tax.

Why round each deduction before you add?

A pay stub only shows whole cents. The total on the stub has to match the lines on the stub, so add the rounded lines.

Think Ethan joins a second union and his dues go up. Does his income tax go up, go down, or stay the same?
It goes down (a little). Union dues are before-tax, so his taxable income gets smaller, and 18% of a smaller amount is smaller. His net pay still goes down, because the dues cost more than the tax he saves.
Math History1917

The “temporary” tax

Canada had no income tax until 1917. To help pay for the First World War, Finance Minister Sir Thomas White brought in the Income War Tax Act, meant to be a short-term war tax.

The rate was 4% of the income above a tax-free amount ($1 500 a year for an unmarried person). The law was 11 pages long. The war ended, but the tax stayed. Today the law is over 3 000 pages.

In 1917, an unmarried worker earned $2 000. The tax was 4% of the income above $1 500. How much tax was that?
\$2\,000-\$1\,500=\$500 (like taxable income!)
\$500\times0.04=\$20 for the whole year.

Reading and Checking a Pay Stub

A pay stub shows the pay period (the dates you worked), your hours and rate, your earnings (each kind of pay and the gross pay), each deduction, the total deductions, and the net pay. Many stubs also have a year-to-date (YTD) column: the total so far this year.

Mistakes happen on pay stubs, and it is your money, so check yours every payday:

  1. 1 Check the hours against your own record, and hours × rate for each earnings line.
  2. 2 Add the earnings to check the gross pay.
  3. 3 Estimate, then check each deduction.
  4. 4 Add the deductions, and check that net pay = gross pay − total deductions.

The year-to-date (YTD) column

The YTD column is a running total: each payday, the new amount is added on. New YTD = old YTD + this pay.

Before Example 7’s pay, Lucas’s YTD gross pay was $31 240.80 and his YTD CPP was $1 858.83. What do the YTD lines show after this pay?
YTD gross =\$31\,240.80+\$897.60=\$32\,138.40
YTD CPP =\$1\,858.83+\$53.41=\$1\,912.24
Think In late December, a full-time worker’s stub shows CPP $0.00. Is that a mistake?
Not always. There is a most you can pay into CPP each year. Once the YTD CPP reaches it, CPP stops until January. (EI works the same way.)

Check every line: ✓ or ✗?

Go down the stub with the checklist. Estimate each deduction first: a wrong decimal shows up right away.

Key ideas

Net pay = gross pay − total deductions. Add up every deduction. Plan with your net pay: it is the money you actually get.
A percent deduction = gross pay × rate as a decimal (5.95% = 0.0595). Round each one to the cent before you add. Estimate with 1% to catch a wrong decimal.
Percent of gross: net ÷ gross × 100 (take-home) and deductions ÷ gross × 100; they add to 100%. A rate = deduction ÷ the pay it came from × 100.
Before-tax deductions (union dues, pension, RRSP) make the taxable income smaller: tax is on the taxable income, CPP and EI on the gross. Check every line of your pay stub.
