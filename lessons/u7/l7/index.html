The Hiker Up and Down

Kai hikes up a mountain trail, from 6 a.m. to 6 p.m. He rests whenever he likes.

The next day he hikes back down the same trail, also from 6 a.m. to 6 p.m.

Is there a spot on the trail that he passes at the same time on both days?

A Yes, always.
B No, never.
C Only if his speed is the same both days.
D Only by luck: it depends on his rests.
A: yes, always! Imagine both days happen on the same day. One Kai starts at the bottom at 6 a.m. and walks up. Another Kai starts at the top at 6 a.m. and walks down. They are on the same trail, coming toward each other, so they must meet. Where they meet, both see the same time on their watch. That spot works for the real Kai on both days. We can’t say where it is, only that it must be there. Today we look up and down one line too: the line of sight from the top and from the bottom.

Angles of Elevation and Depression

Look up at the top of a flagpole, or down from a cliff at a canoe. The angle you look up or down tells you a lot. Today we measure that angle from a level line, make and read a clinometer, and use sin, cos and tan to find heights and distances you can’t measure with a tape.

I can…
  • tell an angle of elevation from an angle of depression, both measured from the horizontal
  • explain why the angle of depression from the top equals the angle of elevation from the bottom
  • read a clinometer: the angle is the difference between the reading and 90°
  • find a height with a trig ratio and add the eye height
  • find a distance from above by moving the angle of depression to the bottom
G4 Demonstrate an understanding of primary trigonometric ratios (sine, cosine, tangent) by: applying similarity to right triangles; generalizing patterns from similar right triangles; applying the primary trigonometric ratios; solving problems.
Today: applying the primary trigonometric ratios to angles of elevation and depression, and solving problems.
1

Looking Up and Looking Down

When you look at something, the straight line from your eye to the object is your line of sight. The horizontal is a level line (flat, like the floor or a calm lake) going out from your eye.

  • The angle of elevation is the angle between the horizontal and your line of sight when you look up.
  • The angle of depression is the angle between the horizontal and your line of sight when you look down.
Key idea   Angle of elevation: from the horizontal up to the line of sight.    Angle of depression: from the horizontal down to the line of sight.
Both are always measured from the horizontal (level) line, never from a wall or a pole.
Example 1

Elevation, depression, or neither?

Is the marked angle \theta an angle of elevation, an angle of depression, or neither? Explain.

(a) a drone

elevation

(b) a lifeguard

depression

(c) a rooftop

neither

(d) a lighthouse

elevation
(a) Looking up, from the horizontal: elevation. (b) Looking down, from the horizontal: depression. (d) Looking up from the boat: elevation. (c) \theta is measured from the vertical (straight down the wall), not from the horizontal, so it is neither. The angle of depression is 90^\circ-\theta.
2

The Angle of Elevation Equals the Angle of Depression

Jada stands on top of a cliff and looks down at a boat. Someone in the boat looks up at her. Both people use the same line of sight. The horizontal at the top and the horizontal at the bottom (the water) are parallel, and the line of sight is a transversal. So the two angles are alternate interior angles (Unit 5, the Z shape), and they are equal.

Key idea   Angle of depression = angle of elevation
The angle of depression from the top equals the angle of elevation from the bottom. (They are alternate interior angles between two parallel horizontal lines.)
!
Watch out. If an angle is measured from the vertical (a wall, a cliff face, a pole), it is not the angle of elevation or depression. Subtract it from 90°.

This is very useful: an angle of depression is at the top, outside the triangle. The equal angle of elevation is at the bottom, inside the right triangle, where you can use it.

Another way to see it: turn the top corner half a turn

Think A student says the angle of depression from the cliff is 34°, so the angle of elevation from the boat must be 90^\circ-34^\circ=56^\circ. What is wrong?
The two angles are equal, not complementary: the angle of elevation is also 34°. 56^\circ is the angle between the line of sight and the vertical cliff face.
Example 2

The same angle, from the other end

(a) From the top of a grain elevator, Mia sees her truck at an angle of depression of 38°. What is the angle of elevation from the truck to Mia?  38°
38°. The angle of elevation and the angle of depression along the same line of sight are equal (alternate interior angles).
(b) A drone pilot looks up at her drone at an angle of elevation of 41°. What is the angle of depression from the drone to the pilot?  41°
41°, for the same reason: one line of sight, two equal angles.
(c) Ken looks down from the top of a cliff at a canoe. His line of sight makes an angle of 64° with the cliff face (the vertical). Find the angle of depression.  26°
The vertical and the horizontal make 90°, so the angle of depression is 90^\circ-64^\circ=26^\circ.
3

Making and Reading a Clinometer

A clinometer is a tool that measures an angle of elevation or depression. Surveyors and foresters use electronic ones, but you can make one in a few minutes.

Make your own:

  1. 1. Tape a drinking straw along the straight edge of a protractor.
  2. 2. Tie a string through the small hole at the centre.
  3. 3. Tie a weight (a washer or a few paper clips) to the other end, so the string hangs below the curved edge.

Use it: Hold the protractor with the curved side down. Look through the straw at the top of the object. Let the string hang straight down, then pinch it against the protractor and read the number where it crosses the scale.

When the straw is level, the string crosses at 90°. Tilt the straw up by 25° and the string crosses at 65° on one scale and 115° on the other. Either way, it is 25° away from 90°.

Key idea   Reading a clinometer: angle of elevation (or depression) = the difference between the reading and 90°
reading less than 90°: angle =90^\circ- reading reading more than 90°: angle = reading -\,90^\circ
Think Why does the string cross at 90^\circ-\theta when the straw tilts up by \theta?
When the straw is level, the 90° mark points straight down, right on the string. Tilting the straw turns the whole protractor by \theta, so the 90° mark swings \theta away from the string. The string still hangs straight down, so it now crosses the scale \theta away from 90: at 90-\theta on one scale and 90+\theta on the other.
Example 3

Read the clinometer

(a), (b) Read each clinometer. Then find the angle of elevation.
(a) Reading 65 (or 115)   Angle 25°
90^\circ-65^\circ=25^\circ (or 115^\circ-90^\circ=25^\circ)
(b) Reading 50 (or 130)   Angle 40°
90^\circ-50^\circ=40^\circ (or 130^\circ-90^\circ=40^\circ)
(c) Find the angle of elevation for each clinometer reading.
Reading72°58°113°90°134°
Angle of elevation18°32°23°0°44°
90-72=18; 90-58=32; 113-90=23; 90-90=0 (looking straight ahead, level); 134-90=44.

4

Finding a Height: Don’t Forget Your Eyes

Now put the angle into a right triangle and use the trig ratios from Lessons 4 to 6.

↺
Quick refresher. SOH CAH TOA: \sin\theta=\dfrac{\text{opp}}{\text{hyp}}, \cos\theta=\dfrac{\text{adj}}{\text{hyp}}, \tan\theta=\dfrac{\text{opp}}{\text{adj}}. Label opp and adj from the angle you are using. If the unknown is on top, multiply. If it is on the bottom, divide.
Key idea   Solving an elevation or depression problem
  1. Step 1: Sketch it. Draw the horizontal, the line of sight, and mark the angle.
  2. Step 2: Find the right triangle. For an angle of depression, use the equal angle of elevation at the bottom.
  3. Step 3: Label opp, adj, hyp from that angle. Mark the side you know and the side you want (x).
  4. Step 4: Choose the ratio that uses those two sides. Write it, substitute, solve.
  5. Step 5: If the angle was measured from your eyes (or hand), add that height. Check, then write a sentence.

An angle of elevation measured by a person starts at their eyes, not at the ground. The triangle only reaches up from eye level, so the tan or sin answer is the height above your eyes.

Your TI-30XIIS must be in DEG (degree) mode: look for DEG on the screen. If it shows RAD or GRAD, press DRG, move to DEG with the arrow keys, and press ENTER. To find 24\times\tan31^\circ: 24 × tan 31 ) ENTER shows 14.42065486.
Example 4

Noor’s flagpole

Noor stands 24 m from the base of the flagpole at her school. Her eyes are 1.6 m above the ground. The angle of elevation to the top of the pole is 31°. How tall is the flagpole, to the nearest tenth of a metre?
The 31° angle is at Noor’s eyes. From it, h (above her eyes) is opp and 24 m is adj: use tan.
\tan31^\circ=\dfrac{h}{24},  h=24\times\tan31^\circ\approx14.42 m (h is on top, so multiply)
Add her eye height: 14.42+1.6=16.02\approx16.0 m. Check: 31° is less than 45°, so h should be less than 24 m ✓.
The flagpole is about 16.0 m tall.
45°
Check with 45°. At 45°, the height above your eyes equals your distance from the object (the triangle has two equal legs). If the angle is less than 45°, the height above your eyes is less than the distance. If the angle is more than 45°, it is more.

Watch the angle grow: when does the height catch up to the distance?

Example 5

Liam’s kite

Liam flies a kite on 60 m of string. He holds the string 1.2 m above the ground. The string makes an angle of elevation of 48°. Assume the string is straight. Round to the nearest tenth.

(a) How high is the kite above the ground?
From 48°: the string is hyp (60 m), the height above his hand is opp. Opp and hyp: sin.
\sin48^\circ=\dfrac{x}{60},  x=60\times\sin48^\circ\approx44.59 m
Add his hand height: 44.59+1.2=45.79. The kite is about 45.8 m above the ground.
(b) How far is Liam from the spot on the ground right under the kite?
Adj and hyp: cos. \cos48^\circ=\dfrac{d}{60},  d=60\times\cos48^\circ\approx40.1 m. Liam is about 40.1 m from that spot. (No hand height here: it is a distance along the ground.) Check: both legs are shorter than the 60 m string ✓.
(a) 60 × sin 48 ) ENTER shows 44.58868953.  (b) 60 × cos 48 ) ENTER shows 40.14783638.

Drag the clinometer to tilt it until the line of sight hits the top of the tree. Read the clinometer, then find the height. Tap Hide the reading and let the class read it first. New tree gives a new one.

Outside (optional): work with a partner. Pick something tall at school (a wall, a light pole, a tree, the gym). Measure your distance from its base with a tape or by pacing, and measure your eye height. Sight the top through the straw, read the clinometer, and find the height.

5

Looking Down: Distances from Above

For an angle of depression, the angle is at the top, outside the triangle. Move it to the bottom: the angle of elevation there is equal. Then the height is opp, and the distance along the ground is adj.

!
Watch out. When the distance you want is adj and you know opp, \tan\theta=\dfrac{\text{opp}}{x} puts x on the bottom, so you divide: x=\dfrac{\text{opp}}{\tan\theta}.
On the calculator: 45 ÷ tan 17 ) ENTER shows 147.1883678.

Multiply or divide? Check which answer makes sense

Think A boat is seen from a cliff at an angle of depression of 60°. Without a calculator, is the boat closer to the cliff than the cliff is tall, or farther?
Closer. At the boat the angle of elevation is 60°, more than 45°, so the height (opp) is more than the distance (adj).
Example 6

Ava’s canoe

From a lookout on a cliff, Ava is 45 m above a lake. She sees a canoe at an angle of depression of 17°. How far is the canoe from the base of the cliff, to the nearest tenth of a metre? Sketch it first.
The angle of elevation from the canoe is also 17° (alternate interior angles). From that angle: 45 m is opp, x is adj. Use tan.
\tan17^\circ=\dfrac{45}{x},  x=\dfrac{45}{\tan17^\circ}\approx147.2 m (x is on the bottom, so divide)
Check: 17° is much less than 45°, so x should be much more than 45 m ✓. The canoe is about 147.2 m from the base of the cliff.
Example 7

Landing in Calgary

A plane is 1 800 m above the runway when it starts its descent to the Calgary airport. It comes down in a straight line at an angle of depression of 4°. Round to the nearest tenth of a kilometre.

(a) What is the horizontal distance from the plane to the spot where it touches down? Sketch it first.
At the touchdown spot the angle of elevation is 4°. 1 800 m is opp, x is adj: tan.
\tan4^\circ=\dfrac{1\,800}{x},  x=\dfrac{1\,800}{\tan4^\circ}\approx25\,741 m \approx25.7 km
(b) How far does the plane fly along its path to touch down?
The path d is hyp. Opp and hyp: sin.
\sin4^\circ=\dfrac{1\,800}{d},  d=\dfrac{1\,800}{\sin4^\circ}\approx25\,804 m \approx25.8 km
Check: the path (hyp) is the longest side, a little longer than x ✓.
(a) 1800 ÷ tan 4 ) ENTER shows 25741.19926.  (b) 1800 ÷ sin 4 ) ENTER shows 25804.05665. Divide by 1 000 for kilometres.
Extra example

The Calgary Tower

Sam stands 51 m from the base of the Calgary Tower. His eyes are 1.5 m above the ground. The angle of elevation to the top is 75°. How tall is the tower, to the nearest tenth of a metre?
Opp = height above his eyes, adj = 51 m: tan. h=51\times\tan75^\circ\approx190.33 m. Add his eye height: 190.33+1.5=191.83\approx191.8 m. Check: 75° is more than 45°, so h is more than 51 m ✓. (The real tower is about 191 m tall.)
✓

Key ideas

Measure from the horizontal: elevation is up from the level line to the line of sight; depression is down. An angle from the vertical is neither: use 90^\circ- it.
Depression = elevation: one line of sight, two parallel horizontals, alternate interior angles (the Z). Move the angle to the bottom, inside the triangle.
Clinometer: angle = the difference between the reading and 90° (90- reading, or reading -\,90).
Solve: sketch, find the right triangle, label opp/adj/hyp, choose sin, cos or tan. Unknown on top: multiply; on the bottom: divide. Add the eye height for a height.
