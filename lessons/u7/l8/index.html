Finding Angles with Inverse Trig Ratios

So far we started with an angle and found a side. Today we go the other way. When you know two sides of a right triangle, the inverse keys on your calculator turn their ratio back into the angle: how steep a ramp is, whether a ladder is safe, the angle of a roof.

I can…
  • use the inverse keys \sin^{-1}, \cos^{-1} and \tan^{-1} (in DEG mode) to turn a ratio into an angle
  • type the whole fraction inside the bracket, and round the angle to the nearest degree
  • find an acute angle of a right triangle from any two sides, choosing sin, cos or tan
  • find the other acute angle with 90^\circ-\theta, and check an angle a second way
  • decide if an angle is reasonable, and solve problems about ladders, ramps and roofs
G4 Demonstrate an understanding of primary trigonometric ratios (sine, cosine, tangent) by: applying similarity to right triangles; generalizing patterns from similar right triangles; applying the primary trigonometric ratios; solving problems.
Today: using the inverse trig ratios to find angles, and solving problems with them.
1

Undoing a Trig Ratio

In Lessons 4 to 7 you started with an angle and used sin, cos or tan to get a ratio. For example, \tan35^\circ\approx0.7002.

Now we go the other way: we know the ratio and want the angle. To go backwards, we use an inverse trig ratio. “Inverse” means “undo”, the way subtracting undoes adding and the square root undoes squaring.

Key idea   Inverse trig ratios (read \sin^{-1} as “inverse sine”)
If \sin\theta=0.5,then \theta=\sin^{-1}(0.5)=30^\circ.“Which angle has a sine of 0.5?”
If \cos\theta=0.2,then \theta=\cos^{-1}(0.2)\approx78^\circ.“Which angle has a cosine of 0.2?”
If \tan\theta=0.7002,then \theta=\tan^{-1}(0.7002)\approx35^\circ.“Which angle has a tangent of 0.7002?”

The inverse keys on your calculator

First check that the screen shows DEG (degree mode). If it shows RAD or GRAD, press DRG, choose DEG with the arrow keys and press ENTER.
The inverse keys are the second functions of the trig keys: 2ndsin gives \sin^{-1}, 2ndcos gives \cos^{-1}, and 2ndtan gives \tan^{-1}.
Example: 2ndtan 0.7002 ) ENTER. The screen shows tan⁻¹(0.7002) and the answer 34.99…, so the angle is about 35°.
!
Watch out. The -1 is not a power. \tan^{-1}(0.7002) does not mean 1\div0.7002. It is the angle whose tangent is 0.7002. Round angles to the nearest degree unless the question says otherwise.

The −1 is not a power: two different buttons

Think Without a calculator: which is bigger, \tan^{-1}(0.8) or \tan^{-1}(1.2)? How do you know?
\tan^{-1}(1.2). A bigger tangent means a steeper slope, so a bigger angle. (\tan^{-1}(1)=45^\circ, so one is a bit under 45° and the other a bit over: about 39° and 50°.)

When the ratio is a fraction

In a triangle the ratio usually starts as a fraction, like \sin A=\frac58. Type the whole fraction inside the bracket. Do not round the fraction to a short decimal first: that can change the angle.

\sin A=\frac58:   2ndsin 5 ÷ 8 ) ENTER shows 38.68…, so \angle A\approx39^\circ.

Think Why can a tangent be more than 1 (like 1.7321), but a sine or cosine never can?
sin and cos divide by the hypotenuse, the longest side, so they are always less than 1. tan is opp ÷ adj, and opp can be longer than adj (any angle over 45°).

Finding an Angle From Two Sides

To find an acute angle \theta in a right triangle, you need two sides. Label them from \theta’s point of view, as in Lessons 4 to 6: the hypotenuse (hyp) is across from the right angle and is the longest side; the opposite side (opp) is across from \theta; the adjacent side (adj) touches \theta and is not the hypotenuse.

Key idea   Finding an angle
  1. Step 1: Label the sides opp, adj and hyp from the angle you want.
  2. Step 2: Circle the two sides you know. Choose the ratio that uses them (SOH CAH TOA):
    opp and hyp → \sin\theta=\dfrac{\text{opp}}{\text{hyp}}adj and hyp → \cos\theta=\dfrac{\text{adj}}{\text{hyp}}opp and adj → \tan\theta=\dfrac{\text{opp}}{\text{adj}}
  3. Step 3: Write the ratio and put in the two lengths.
  4. Step 4: Use the inverse key: \theta=\sin^{-1}(\ldots), \cos^{-1}(\ldots) or \tan^{-1}(\ldots). Round to the nearest degree.
Example 3

Label, choose, find θ

Label the two given sides opp, adj or hyp from \theta. Then find \theta to the nearest degree.
(a)

\tan\theta=\frac59,  \theta=\tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac59\right)

(b)

\sin\theta=\frac7{12},  \theta=\sin^{-1}\!\left(\frac7{12}\right)
\theta=35.68\ldots\approx36^\circ
(c)

\cos\theta=\frac{8.5}{11},  \theta=\cos^{-1}\!\left(\frac{8.5}{11}\right)

Part (c): 2ndcos 8.5 ÷ 11 ) ENTER shows 39.40…
Think In part (a), could you use sin instead of tan?
Not yet: sin needs the hyp, and we don’t know it. You could find it first with the Pythagorean theorem (\sqrt{9^2+5^2}\approx10.30), then \sin^{-1}\!\left(\frac5{10.30}\right)\approx29^\circ, the same angle. Using the two sides you were given is quicker and safer.

3

The Other Acute Angle, and Checking

The three angles of any triangle add to 180°. In a right triangle one angle is 90°, so the two acute angles add to 90°.

Key idea   Other acute angle =90^\circ-\theta   (for example, if one acute angle is 28°, the other is 90^\circ-28^\circ=62^\circ)

You can check an angle by finding it a second way: with a different ratio, or from the other angle’s point of view. Both ways should give the same answer.

!
Watch out. When you move to the other acute angle, opp and adj swap. The side that was opposite the first angle is adjacent to the second one. The hypotenuse never changes.
Example 4

Both acute angles, checked

In \triangle ABC, \angle C=90^\circ. The sides are a=8 cm, b=15 cm and c=17 cm.

(a) Find \angle A with the tangent ratio.
From A: opp =a=8, adj =b=15.
\tan A=\frac8{15},  \angle A=\tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac8{15}\right)=28.07\ldots\approx28^\circ
(b) Check \angle A with the sine ratio.
opp =8, hyp =17. \sin A=\frac8{17},  \angle A=\sin^{-1}\!\left(\frac8{17}\right)=28.07\ldots\approx28^\circ ✓
(c) Find \angle B by subtracting.
\angle B=90^\circ-28.07^\circ=61.93^\circ\approx62^\circ
(d) Check \angle B with a ratio from B’s point of view.
From B: opp =b=15, adj =a=8 (they swapped).
\angle B=\tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{15}8\right)=61.92\ldots\approx62^\circ ✓
Example 5

Find both angles

In \triangle PQR, \angle R=90^\circ, the hypotenuse PQ=14.5 m and QR=6.2 m. Find \angle P and \angle Q to the nearest degree. Check \angle Q with a ratio.
From P: QR=6.2 is opp and PQ=14.5 is hyp, so use sin.
\sin P=\frac{6.2}{14.5},  \angle P=\sin^{-1}\!\left(\frac{6.2}{14.5}\right)=25.31\ldots\approx25^\circ
\angle Q=90^\circ-25.31^\circ=64.69^\circ\approx65^\circ
Check: from Q, QR=6.2 is adj. \angle Q=\cos^{-1}\!\left(\frac{6.2}{14.5}\right)=64.68\ldots\approx65^\circ ✓
Extra example

Both acute angles

In \triangle DEF, \angle F=90^\circ, the legs are d=9 cm and e=12 cm, and the hypotenuse is f=15 cm.

(a) Find \angle D.
From D: opp 9, adj 12. \tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac9{12}\right)=36.86\ldots\approx37^\circ
(b) Find \angle E by subtracting.
90^\circ-36.87^\circ=53.13^\circ\approx53^\circ
(c) Check \angle E with the sine ratio.
From E: opp 12, hyp 15. \sin^{-1}\!\left(\frac{12}{15}\right)=53.13\ldots\approx53^\circ ✓

Why do the two acute angles always add to 90°?

Think Jonah says a right triangle has angles of 90°, 50° and 50°. Can he be right?
No. The two acute angles must add to 90°, but 50^\circ+50^\circ=100^\circ. All three would add to 190°, not 180°. If one acute angle is 50°, the other is 90^\circ-50^\circ=40^\circ.
4

Is the Angle Reasonable?

A calculator does exactly what you type, so a small slip gives a wrong angle. Before you write your answer, compare it with these benchmarks.

Key idea   Quick checks for an angle \theta in a right triangle
  • If the two legs are equal, \theta=45^\circ. If opp is shorter than adj, \theta is less than 45°. If opp is longer, \theta is more than 45°.
  • If opp is half the hypotenuse, \theta=30^\circ (\sin30^\circ=0.5). If adj is half the hypotenuse, \theta=60^\circ.
  • The bigger acute angle is across from the longer leg.
  • An ERROR with \sin^{-1} or \cos^{-1} means the number was more than 1. The hypotenuse is the longest side, so it always goes on the bottom.
  • A tiny answer like 0.5 (for an angle that looks like about 30°) means the calculator is in RAD mode.
Think Liv’s angle looks like about 60°, but her calculator shows 1.047. What happened?
Her calculator is in RAD mode (60° is about 1.047 radians). Press DRG, choose DEG, and do it again.

Angles on the Job: Ladders, Ramps and Roofs

Many jobs need an angle that you cannot measure easily, but you can measure two lengths. Sketch a right triangle, mark the right angle, write the lengths you know, mark the angle you want, then follow the four steps.

Key idea   Safety guidelines (approximate; always follow the rules for your job site)
  • Ladders: a ladder should make an angle of about 75° with the ground. (Rule of thumb: 1 m out from the wall for every 4 m of ladder.)
  • Wheelchair ramps: a slope of 1 in 12 (1 m up for every 12 m along the ground), which is about 4.8°, is the steepest usually allowed.
  • Roof pitch: a “5/12 pitch” means the roof rises 5 inches for every 12 inches across.
Example 7

A ladder and a ramp

(a) A painter leans a 6 m ladder against a wall. The foot of the ladder is 1.5 m from the wall. What angle does the ladder make with the ground? Is it safe?
From \theta: the ground distance 1.5 m is adj, the ladder 6 m is hyp. Use cos.
\cos\theta=\frac{1.5}6,  \theta=\cos^{-1}\!\left(\frac{1.5}6\right)=75.52\ldots\approx76^\circ
This is very close to 75°, so the ladder is set up safely.
(b) A wheelchair ramp at a community hall is 8 m long (along the ramp surface). It rises 0.6 m. Find the angle between the ramp and the ground, to the nearest tenth of a degree. Does it meet the guideline?
From \theta: the rise 0.6 m is opp, the ramp 8 m is hyp. Use sin.
\sin\theta=\frac{0.6}8,  \theta=\sin^{-1}\!\left(\frac{0.6}8\right)=4.30\ldots\approx4.3^\circ
4.3° is less than about 4.8°, so yes, the ramp meets the guideline.
Example 8

A roof and a drone

(a) A garage roof has a 5/12 pitch. Find the angle \theta between the roof and the horizontal. Then find angle x at the peak, between the rafter and the vertical centre post.
The pitch triangle has opp =5 and adj =12. Use tan.
\theta=\tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac5{12}\right)=22.61\ldots\approx23^\circ
The post, the rafter and the horizontal make a right triangle, so x=90^\circ-22.62^\circ=67.38^\circ\approx67^\circ.
(b) A drone is hovering 90 m above a field. A car is parked 140 m away, measured along the ground. Find the angle of depression from the drone to the car.
The angle of depression at the drone equals the angle of elevation at the car (alternate interior angles). From the car: opp =90, adj =140.
\theta=\tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{90}{140}\right)=32.73\ldots\approx33^\circ. The angle of depression is about 33°.
Part (b): 2ndtan 90 ÷ 140 ) ENTER shows 32.73…

Inverse = undo. \sin^{-1}, \cos^{-1}, \tan^{-1} turn a ratio back into an angle. Keys: 2nd sin / cos / tan, in DEG mode. The -1 is not a power.
