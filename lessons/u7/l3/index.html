The Pythagorean Theorem: Finding a Leg, and Squaring a Corner

Last lesson we knew the two legs and found the hypotenuse. Today we turn the formula around to find a missing leg: how high a ladder reaches, how high a kite flies. Then we use the theorem as a test: is a corner really 90°? That is how carpenters square a deck with just a tape measure.

I can…
  • find a missing leg of a right triangle: a^2=c^2-b^2 (big square minus small square)
  • decide whether the missing side is a leg or the hypotenuse, even when the letters and the picture are different
  • solve real problems with ladders, kites and two triangles that share a side
  • test whether a triangle is a right triangle by comparing a^2+b^2 with c^2
  • use the 3-4-5 method and equal diagonals to check that a corner is square
G2 Demonstrate an understanding of the Pythagorean theorem by: identifying situations that involve right triangles; verifying the formula; applying the formula; solving problems.

Finding a Missing Leg

In a right triangle, the two sides that make the right angle are the legs (a and b). The side across from the right angle is the hypotenuse (c). It is always the longest side. The Pythagorean theorem says a^2+b^2=c^2.

In Lesson 2 you knew both legs and found the hypotenuse. Now you know the hypotenuse and one leg, and you want the other leg.

The squares on the sides of this right triangle have areas 9, 16 and 25.

The two small squares together make the big one: 9+16=25.

So if you take one small square away from the big one, the other small square is left:

25-16=9   and   25-9=16

To find a leg, subtract: (hypotenuse)2 − (the other leg)2.

Rearranging the formula. Start with a^2+b^2=c^2. To get a^2 by itself, subtract b^2 from both sides: a^2=c^2-b^2.

Key idea
Missing hypotenuse: c^2=a^2+b^2 (add the squares)
Missing leg: a^2=c^2-b^2 or b^2=c^2-a^2 (subtract the squares)
  1. Step 1. Find the hypotenuse: it is across from the right angle.
  2. Step 2. Write the formula for the side you want.
  3. Step 3. Substitute and square.
  4. Step 4. Subtract (or add).
  5. Step 5. Take the square root.
  6. Step 6. Check: the hypotenuse is the longest side.
!
Watch out. When you find a leg, always do big square minus small square (hypotenuse2 first). A leg is always shorter than the hypotenuse. If your leg is longer than the hypotenuse, you added when you should have subtracted.
On the TI-30XIIS, \sqrt{\ } is the second job of the x² key. To find \sqrt{10.6^2-5.6^2} in one go, press 2ndx² 10.6 x² − 5.6 x² ) ENTER. The screen shows 9.

Leg or hypotenuse? Six steps

Think Can a leg ever be longer than the hypotenuse? Use the squares to explain.
No. The square on the hypotenuse is made of both small squares together, so it is bigger than either one. A bigger square has a longer side, so the hypotenuse is always the longest side.

Ladders, Kites and Other Real Problems

Many real problems hide a right triangle: a wall and the ground, a pole and its rope, a screen and its diagonal. Draw a sketch, mark the right angle, and label what you know. Then ask: is the missing side a leg or the hypotenuse?

Ladder safety. A common safety rule for a straight or extension ladder is the 4-to-1 rule: for every 4 m of height up the wall, move the foot of the ladder 1 m out from the wall. So the distance out should be about the height ÷ 4. (This makes an angle of about 75° with the ground.)
Think Jordan has a 6 m ladder. Can it reach a window 6 m up the wall? Explain.
No. The ladder is the hypotenuse, so it is always longer than the height it reaches. Its foot has to be out from the wall, so it reaches less than 6 m. (With the foot 1.5 m out: \sqrt{6^2-1.5^2}=\sqrt{33.75}\approx5.8 m.)
Example 3

Jordan’s ladder

Jordan leans a 7.3 m extension ladder against the wall of a house in Red Deer. The foot of the ladder is 1.8 m from the wall.

(a) How high up the wall does the ladder reach, to the nearest tenth of a metre?
The ladder is across from the right angle (wall meets ground), so the ladder is the hypotenuse. The height h is a leg.
h^2=7.3^2-1.8^2=53.29-3.24=50.05
h=\sqrt{50.05}=7.074\ldots
The ladder reaches about 7.1 m up the wall.
(b) Does Jordan’s ladder follow the 4-to-1 rule? Explain.
By the rule, the foot should be about 7.1\div4\approx1.8 m from the wall. It is 1.8 m out, so yes, the ladder follows the rule.
All in one: 2ndx² 7.3 x² − 1.8 x² ) ENTER gives 7.074…

Two triangles that share a side

Some diagrams have two right triangles that share a side. Find the shared side from the first triangle, then use it in the second.

Keep the full value. Keep the full calculator value of the shared side (or its square) for the next step. Round only at the very end. Rounding in the middle can change your final answer.
Example 5

Find x, then y

Find x and y, to the nearest tenth of a centimetre.
Left triangle: 9.5 cm is the hypotenuse, x is a leg.
x^2=9.5^2-4.1^2=90.25-16.81=73.44, so x=\sqrt{73.44}=8.569\ldots\approx8.6 cm
Right triangle: the legs are x and 6.3 cm, and y is the hypotenuse. Use x^2=73.44 (the full value).
y^2=73.44+6.3^2=73.44+39.69=113.13, so y=\sqrt{113.13}=10.636\ldots\approx10.6 cm
(If you used the rounded 8.6: 8.6^2+6.3^2=113.65 and y\approx10.7, which is a little off.)

More real problems

Draw a sketch for each one. Leg or hypotenuse? Answer to the nearest tenth.

(a) A 12 ft ladder leans on a wall with its foot 3 ft out. How high does it reach? Does it follow the 4-to-1 rule?
The ladder is the hypotenuse. h^2=12^2-3^2=144-9=135, h=\sqrt{135}=11.618\ldots\approx11.6 ft. Rule: 11.6\div4\approx2.9 ft out. 3 ft is close, so yes.
(b) A wire 15 m long runs from the top of a 12 m flagpole to a stake in the ground. How far is the stake from the bottom of the pole?
The wire is the hypotenuse. d^2=15^2-12^2=225-144=81, d=\sqrt{81}=9 m.
(c) A ramp is 6.1 m long and rises 0.5 m. How far along the ground does it go?
The ramp is the hypotenuse. g^2=6.1^2-0.5^2=37.21-0.25=36.96, g=\sqrt{36.96}=6.079\ldots\approx6.1 m. (Almost the same as the ramp: a gentle ramp is nearly flat.)

Is It a Right Triangle?

The Pythagorean theorem works only for right triangles. So we can use it as a test. Square the two shorter sides and add them. Compare the total with the square of the longest side.

Key idea   Right triangle test. Let c be the longest side, and a and b the two shorter sides.
If a^2+b^2=c^2:the triangle is a right triangle. The right angle is across from c.
If a^2+b^2>c^2:not a right triangle. The biggest angle is less than 90° (the corner is too closed).
If a^2+b^2<c^2:not a right triangle. The biggest angle is more than 90° (the corner is too open).
!
Watch out. The longest side must be c, even when it is not written last. For sides 17, 8 and 15, test 8^2+15^2 against 17^2.

What goes wrong when c is not the longest side

Think Why can’t a^2+b^2=c^2 work if c is not the longest side?
The square on c is supposed to hold both of the other squares. If another side is longer than c, its square alone is already bigger than c^2, so the two sides can never balance.

Example 7

Harjit’s shelf bracket

In shop class, Harjit welds a steel shelf bracket. The part on the wall is 30 cm long, the part under the shelf is 40 cm long, and the brace between their ends is 49 cm long.

(a) Is the corner between the wall and the shelf a right angle? Show how you know.
30^2+40^2=900+1\,600=2\,500 and 49^2=2\,401
2\,500\ne2\,401, so it is not a right triangle. The corner is not 90°.
(a^2+b^2 is bigger, so the corner is less than 90°: the shelf would tilt down.)
(b) How long should the brace be to make a right angle?
c=\sqrt{2\,500}=50 cm. The brace should be 50 cm long.
Extra example

More right-triangle tests

(a) 16 cm, 30 cm, 34 cm
16^2+30^2=256+900=1\,156 and 34^2=1\,156. Equal: yes, a right triangle.
(b) 6 m, 8 m, 11 m
6^2+8^2=36+64=100 and 11^2=121. 100 is less: no, the biggest angle is more than 90°.
(c) 6.5 ft, 2.5 ft, 6 ft
Longest: 6.5. 2.5^2+6^2=6.25+36=42.25 and 6.5^2=42.25. Equal: yes.
Think A triangle has three equal sides, 5 cm each. Without any math, can it be a right triangle?
No. In a right triangle the hypotenuse is always longer than each leg. With three equal sides, no side is the longest. (Check: 5^2+5^2=50, but 5^2=25.)

Squaring a Corner: the 3-4-5 Method

A square corner is a 90° corner. Builders need square corners for decks, sheds, foundations, fences and garden beds. A carpenter’s square is too small for a big job, but a tape measure and the Pythagorean theorem work for any size.

The 3-4-5 method
  1. Step 1. Measure 3 units along one side from the corner and make a mark.
  2. Step 2. Measure 4 units along the other side and make a mark.
  3. Step 3. Measure the diagonal between the two marks. If it is exactly 5 units, the corner is square, because 3^2+4^2=9+16=25=5^2.
If the diagonal is too long, the corner is more than 90°: push the sides closer together. If it is too short, the corner is less than 90°: open the sides up.
Key idea   Multiples of 3-4-5 also work: multiply all three numbers by the same number.
× 2: 6-8-10× 3: 9-12-15× 4: 12-16-20× 0.5: 1.5-2-2.5(any unit: ft, m, …)
Use the biggest triangle that fits the job: a small measuring error matters less on a big triangle.

Builders have used this idea for thousands of years. Today framers, landscapers and concrete crews still use it, along with laser tools.

Why a bigger triangle gives a better check

Think Does the 3-4-5 method work in metres too? In inches?
Yes. 3^2+4^2=5^2 is true for any unit, as long as all three measurements use the same unit: 3 m, 4 m, 5 m or 3 in, 4 in, 5 in.
Example 8

Mateo’s deck

Mateo is building a deck frame in his backyard in Airdrie. He measures 6 ft along one rim board from the corner and 8 ft along the other, and marks both spots. The diagonal between the marks is 10 ft 2 in.

(a) What should the diagonal be? Why?
6^2+8^2=36+64=100=10^2
For a square corner the diagonal should be exactly 10 ft (6-8-10 is 3-4-5 times 2).
(b) Is the corner square? What should Mateo do?
No. 10 ft 2 in is too long, so the corner is more than 90°.
Mateo should push the boards a little closer together until the diagonal is 10 ft.

Checking a rectangle with its diagonals

A rectangle has two diagonals (corner to opposite corner), and in a rectangle they are equal. If one diagonal is longer than the other, the corners are not square: the shape has been pushed over.

Example 9

Noor’s garden bed

Noor builds a frame for a raised garden bed. It should be a rectangle 1.2 m wide and 2.4 m long.

(a) How long should each diagonal be, to the nearest centimetre?
The diagonal is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with legs 1.2 m and 2.4 m.
d^2=1.2^2+2.4^2=1.44+5.76=7.2
d=\sqrt{7.2}=2.683\ldots\approx2.68 m
(b) Noor measures the two diagonals: 2.71 m and 2.65 m. Is her frame a rectangle with square corners? What should she do?
No. In a rectangle the diagonals are equal, and these are not. She should push the two corners at the ends of the longer diagonal (2.71 m) toward each other until both diagonals measure about 2.68 m.
2ndx² 1.2 x² + 2.4 x² ) ENTER gives 2.683…
Extra example

Square it up

(a) A fence crew uses a 3-4-5 triangle times 1.5. What are its three sides, in metres?
3\times1.5=4.5, 4\times1.5=6, 5\times1.5=7.5: 4.5 m, 6 m, 7.5 m
(b) Marks at 9 ft and 12 ft. The diagonal reads 14 ft 9 in. Square? What should you do?
It should be 15 ft (9-12-15). 14 ft 9 in is too short: the corner is less than 90°. Open the sides up.
(c) A shed floor should be 8 ft by 10 ft. How long should each diagonal be, to the nearest tenth of a foot?
d^2=8^2+10^2=64+100=164, d=\sqrt{164}=12.806\ldots\approx12.8 ft

Key ideas

Missing leg: subtract. a^2=c^2-b^2, big square minus small square, then \sqrt{\ }. Missing hypotenuse: add. A leg is always shorter than the hypotenuse.
Real problems: sketch, mark the right angle, ask “leg or hypotenuse?”. Read the question again at the end (hand height!). With two triangles, keep the full value of the shared side.
Right triangle test: c = the longest side. a^2+b^2=c^2: right. Bigger: the angle is less than 90°. Smaller: more than 90°.
Square corners: 3-4-5 (or 6-8-10, 9-12-15…). Diagonal too long: push in. Too short: open up. A rectangle’s two diagonals are equal.
