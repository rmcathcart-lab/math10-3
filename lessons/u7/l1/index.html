Squares and Perfect Squares

To square a number, multiply it by itself. We write a small 2 up high: 6^2 is read “6 squared” and means 6\times6=36. The small 2 is called an exponent.

Why “squared”? A square that is 6 units long on each side is covered by 6\times6=36 small squares. So the area of a square is its side length squared.

A perfect square is what you get when you square a whole number: 1^2=1,\ 2^2=4,\ 3^2=9,\ 4^2=16,\ \dots So 1, 4, 9 and 16 are perfect squares.

Key idea   Squaring: n^2=n\times n   6^2=6\times6=36   Area of a square =(\text{side})^2
!
Watch out. 6^2 means 6\times6=36. It does not mean 6\times2=12. The exponent tells you how many 6s to multiply.

Square Roots

Finding a square root is the opposite of squaring. It “undoes” the square. The square root of 49 is 7, because 7^2=49. We write \sqrt{49}=7. The sign \sqrt{\ } is the square root sign.

If you know the area of a square, its side length is the square root of the area.

square:   side 7 → area 7^2=49
square root:   area 49 → side \sqrt{49}=7
Key idea   \sqrt{49}=7 because 7^2=49.   Side of a square =\sqrt{\text{area}}
A perfect square has a whole-number square root (\sqrt{81}=9). Any other whole number has a square root that is a decimal that never ends (\sqrt{40}=6.324\,555\dots). Round it as the question asks, often to the nearest tenth.
Calculator. The \sqrt{\ } key is above x². Press 2ndx². The screen shows √(. Type the number, then ) and ENTER. For \sqrt{40}: 2ndx² 40 ) ENTER shows 6.32455532.
!
Watch out. A square root is not half the number: \sqrt{64} is 8, not 32. Check any square root by squaring it: 8^2=64 ✓, but 32^2=1\,024.

Estimating a Square Root

Before you use a calculator, estimate. Then you can tell if the calculator answer makes sense. The trick: find the two perfect squares that the number sits between.

Key idea   To estimate \sqrt{50}:
  1. Step 1. Find the perfect squares just below and just above the number: 49<50<64.
  2. Step 2. Take their square roots: \sqrt{49}=7 and \sqrt{64}=8. So \sqrt{50} is between 7 and 8.
  3. Step 3. Which perfect square is the number closer to? 50 is much closer to 49, so \sqrt{50} is a little more than 7.

Triangles: The Angle Sum, and Naming Triangles

A triangle has 3 sides, 3 corners and 3 angles. Each corner is a vertex (plural: vertices) and is named with a capital letter. The triangle with vertices A, B and C is written \triangle ABC.

If you tear the three corners off any paper triangle and put them side by side, they always make a straight line. A straight line is 180^\circ (Unit 5).

Key idea   The angles in a triangle add up to 180^\circ:   \angle A+\angle B+\angle C=180^\circ
Missing angle =180^\circ- (the two angles you know).
In a right triangle, the right angle uses 90^\circ, so the other two angles add up to 90^\circ (they are complementary).

Naming triangles

Triangles get names from their sides and from their angles. Small tick marks show sides that are the same length.

Key idea
By sides
By angles
Equilateral: all 3 sides equal. All 3 angles are 60^\circ.
Acute: all 3 angles are less than 90^\circ.
Isosceles: 2 sides equal. The 2 angles across from the equal sides are also equal.
Right: one angle is exactly 90^\circ (marked with a small square).
Scalene: no sides equal, and no angles equal.
Obtuse: one angle is more than 90^\circ.
!
Watch out. Every triangle has at least two acute angles. The biggest angle decides the name: if it is 90^\circ the triangle is right, if it is more than 90^\circ it is obtuse. A triangle can never have two right angles or two obtuse angles (they would already use up 180^\circ or more). A triangle gets one name of each kind, like a right isosceles triangle.

The Parts of a Right Triangle

A right triangle has one right angle (90^\circ), shown by a small square in the corner. Its sides have special names:

  • The two sides that make the right angle are the legs.
  • The side across from (opposite) the right angle is the hypotenuse (say “hy-POT-en-use”). It is always the longest side.
Key idea   Naming the sides of \triangle ABC
  1. 1. With the small letter of the vertex across from it: side a is across from \angle A, side b across from \angle B, side c across from \angle C.
  2. 2. With its two end points: side a is also side BC.
In this unit the right angle is usually at C. Then the hypotenuse is c (side AB), and the legs are a and b.
i
Tip. To find the hypotenuse, put your finger on the little square and slide straight across the triangle. The side you land on is the hypotenuse. A triangle can be turned any way, so the hypotenuse is not always the slanted side or the bottom side.

Example 10

The ladder

A ladder leans against the wall of a house. The ground is level and the wall goes straight up.
(a) Where is the right angle? Mark it on the picture.
Where the wall meets the ground: the wall goes straight up from level ground, so that corner is 90^\circ.
(b) Which part is the hypotenuse? Which parts are the legs?
The ladder is across from the right angle, so it is the hypotenuse. The legs are the wall (from the ground to the top of the ladder) and the ground (from the wall to the foot of the ladder).
(c) The ladder makes a 75^\circ angle with the ground. What angle does it make with the wall?
Right triangle, so the two other angles add to 90^\circ: 90^\circ-75^\circ=15^\circ. The ladder makes a 15° angle with the wall.

Triangles at work

(a) A wheelchair ramp meets the level ground at 5^\circ. A support post stands straight up under the high end. What angle does the ramp make with the post?
The post and the ground make a right angle: 90^\circ-5^\circ=85^\circ.
