Two Doors, Two Robots

You are stuck in an escape room with two doors. One door leads out. The other leads to a broom closet.

A robot stands at each door. One robot always tells the truth. The other robot always lies. You don’t know which robot is which.

You may ask ONE robot ONE question. What should you ask?

A“Are you the robot that tells the truth?”
B“Which door leads out?”
C“Which door would the other robot say leads out?”
D“Is 2 + 2 = 4?”
Ask C, then go through the OTHER door! If you ask the truth-teller, he truthfully tells you the liar’s wrong answer. If you ask the liar, he lies about the truth-teller’s right answer. Either way you hear the wrong door. A is useless (both robots say “yes”). B gives an answer you can’t trust. D tells you who the liar is, but then your one question is used up. Today: picking the right question first, just like picking the right tool for a triangle.

Solving Right Triangles: Choosing the Right Ratio

You now have four tools for right triangles. Today the hard part is not the math: it is choosing the right tool. We learn a 4-step routine, find every side and angle of a triangle, work with two triangles at once, and check that our answers make sense.

I can…
  • choose Pythagoras, sine, cosine, tangent, an inverse key or 90° − the angle from what I know and what I want
  • solve a right triangle: find all of its missing sides and angles
  • find the hidden right triangle in a word problem, and solve problems with two triangles that share a side
  • tell whether an answer is reasonable without solving again

1

Which Tool Do I Use? A Four-Step Routine

You now have four tools for right triangles: the Pythagorean theorem, the three trigonometric ratios (sine, cosine and tangent), their inverse keys for angles, and the fact that the two acute angles add to 90°. The hard part is often not the math. It is choosing the right tool. This routine works every time.

Key idea · Solving a right triangle: the 4-step routine
  1. Sketch and label. Mark the right angle and the angle you are using (\theta). Label the sides opp, adj and hyp from \theta. Put a ? on what you want.
  2. Choose the tool. Look at what you know and what you want:
    You knowYou wantUse
    two sidesthe third sidePythagoras: a^2+b^2=c^2
    one acute anglethe other acute angle90^\circ- the angle you know
    an acute angle and one sideanother sidethe ratio with the side you know and the side you want
    two sidesan acute anglethe ratio with those two sides, then 2nd and the ratio key
  3. Solve. Write the equation, substitute, solve. Unknown on top: multiply. Unknown on the bottom: divide.
  4. Check. Is the answer reasonable? (See Section 5.)
SOH \sin\theta=\dfrac{\text{opp}}{\text{hyp}}CAH \cos\theta=\dfrac{\text{adj}}{\text{hyp}}TOA \tan\theta=\dfrac{\text{opp}}{\text{adj}}
✂
Leave one out. To choose a ratio, find the side you do not know and do not want. Leave it out.   No hyp → TOA.   No opp → CAH.   No adj → SOH.
Example 1

Name the tool, then solve

For each triangle, name the tool, write the equation, then solve. Round sides to the nearest tenth and angles to the nearest degree.

Two sides known, want the third side.

(c)

Think it through

Think Kai knows two sides of a right triangle and wants an angle. He says, “Two sides, so Pythagoras.” Is he right?
No. Pythagoras only finds a side. For an angle he needs the ratio with those two sides, then the inverse key (2nd and the ratio key).
Think You know an acute angle and the hypotenuse, and you want the side opposite the angle. Which side do you leave out? Which ratio is left?
Leave out the adjacent side (not known, not wanted). The ratio without adj is SOH: \sin\theta=\dfrac{\text{opp}}{\text{hyp}}.

Solving a Whole Triangle

To solve a triangle means to find all of its missing sides and angles. A right triangle has three sides and two acute angles (the right angle is already known). If you know two sides, or one side and one acute angle, you can find everything else.

Key idea · Tips for solving a whole triangle
  • If you know an acute angle, find the other one first: it is just 90^\circ- the angle.
  • Each time, use the numbers you were given if you can, not an answer you rounded. Rounded numbers make small errors grow.
  • Keep the full calculator value until the end. Round sides to the nearest tenth and angles to the nearest degree.
  • Finish with a check: Pythagoras, or the angles adding to 90°.

An angle and the hypotenuse

Solve \triangle ABC.  \angle C=90^\circ, \angle A=38^\circ and the hypotenuse c=14.5 m.

\angle B= 52°a= 8.9 m
b= 11.4 mCheck: a^2+b^2=c^2 ✓

Side a: From \angle A, a is opp and 14.5 m is hyp. No adj → SOH.
 \sin38^\circ=\dfrac{a}{14.5} so a=14.5\sin38^\circ=8.927\ldots\approx8.9 m (unknown on top: multiply)
Side b: From \angle A, b is adj and 14.5 m is hyp. No opp → CAH. (Use the given 14.5, not the rounded 8.9.)

Check: 8.927^2+11.426^2=79.69+130.56=210.25, and 14.5^2=210.25 ✓
The hypotenuse (14.5 m) is the longest side, and the bigger angle (52°) faces the longer leg (11.4 m) ✓
\angle B=52^\circ, a\approx8.9 m, b\approx11.4 m.

Solving Triangles in Real Problems

In a word problem, the triangle is hidden in the story. Draw it first. Ground and floors are horizontal; walls, poles and towers are vertical, so they meet at a right angle. Then use the same 4 steps.

Example 4

A wheelchair ramp in Airdrie

A community hall in Airdrie needs a wheelchair ramp up to a door that is 0.6 m above the sidewalk. Canadian accessibility guidance says a ramp should rise at an angle of about 4.8° or less (that is about 1 m up for every 12 m along).

(a) How long must the ramp be?
From 4.8°: 0.6 m is opp, the ramp is hyp. No adj → SOH.
\sin4.8^\circ=\dfrac{0.6}{x} (unknown on the bottom: divide)
x=\dfrac{0.6}{\sin4.8^\circ}=7.170\ldots
The ramp must be about 7.2 m long.
(b) How much horizontal space along the sidewalk does the ramp need?
From 4.8°: 0.6 m is opp, the run is adj. No hyp → TOA.
\tan4.8^\circ=\dfrac{0.6}{y}, so y=\dfrac{0.6}{\tan4.8^\circ}=7.145\ldots
The ramp needs about 7.1 m of space.
Check: 1 m up per 12 m along: 0.6\times12=7.2 m ✓
!
Watch out. When the unknown is on the bottom, students often multiply by mistake: 0.6\times\sin4.8^\circ=0.05 m. A ramp 5 cm long can’t climb 60 cm! The hypotenuse must be the longest side.
Unknown on the bottom, so divide: 0.6 ÷ sin 4.8 ) ENTER. Estimate first: the ramp must be longer than the 7.1 m run, but only a little.
Example 5

A painter’s ladder in Lethbridge

A painter in Lethbridge leans a 24 ft extension ladder against a house. The foot of the ladder is 6 ft from the wall. A ladder is safest at about 75° to the ground.

(a) What angle does the ladder make with the ground? Is it safe?
From \theta (at the ground): 6 ft is adj, 24 ft is hyp. No opp → CAH.
\cos\theta=\dfrac{6}{24}=0.25
\theta=\cos^{-1}(0.25)=75.522\ldots\approx76^\circ
The ladder is at about 76°. That is close to 75°, so it is safe.
(b) How high up the wall does the ladder reach?
Two sides known, want the third: Pythagoras (uses only the given numbers).
h^2=24^2-6^2=576-36=540, so h=\sqrt{540}=23.237\ldots
The ladder reaches about 23.2 ft up the wall. (Check: h is a bit shorter than the 24 ft ladder ✓)
(a) 2ndcos 6 ÷ 24 ) ENTER   (b) 2ndx² 24 x² − 6 x² ) ENTER

Think it through

Think In Example 5(b), Rosa found the height with 24\sin76^\circ. She got 23.3 ft, not 23.2 ft. Why is it a little off?
She used the rounded angle 76°. The real angle is 75.522…°. With the given numbers only (Pythagoras), or the full angle, the height is 23.2 ft.
Think A flagpole’s shadow is on flat ground. Where is the right angle in the hidden triangle?
At the bottom of the pole: the pole is vertical and the ground is horizontal. The sun’s ray is the hypotenuse.

Two Right Triangles in One Problem

Some problems have two right triangles that share a side. The shared side is the key.

Key idea · Two-triangle strategy
  1. Sketch both triangles. Mark the side they share.
  2. Start in the triangle where you know enough (two facts besides the right angle).
  3. Use what you found in the first triangle (keep the full calculator value) to solve the second triangle.
  4. Add or subtract lengths if the question asks for a total or a difference.
Example 6

A drone over a soccer field

A drone is hovering 45 m above a soccer field near Red Deer. Ava and Ben stand on opposite sides of the point right under the drone. Ava sees the drone at an angle of elevation of 34°. Ben sees it at 52°. How far apart are Ava and Ben?

The triangles share the 45 m height. Each triangle has the height and an angle, so start with either one.
Ava’s triangle: From 34°: 45 m is opp, x is adj. No hyp → TOA.
 \tan34^\circ=\dfrac{45}{x}, so x=\dfrac{45}{\tan34^\circ}=66.715\ldots m
Ben’s triangle: From 52°: 45 m is opp, y is adj. No hyp → TOA.
 \tan52^\circ=\dfrac{45}{y}, so y=\dfrac{45}{\tan52^\circ}=35.157\ldots m
Total: 66.715\ldots+35.157\ldots=101.873\ldots
Ava and Ben are about 101.9 m apart.
Check: Ben’s angle is steeper, so he should be closer to the drone: 35.2 m < 66.7 m ✓
Both parts in one line, so nothing gets rounded: 45 ÷ tan 34 ) + 45 ÷ tan 52 ) ENTER
Example 7

A garage roof

A garage is 7.2 m wide and its walls are 2.7 m high. The two sides of the roof meet in the middle, and each side rises at 24°.

(a) How high does the roof rise above the walls?
The dashed line cuts the roof into two equal right triangles. Each has a base of half the width: 7.2\div2=3.6 m.
From 24°: the rise is opp, 3.6 m is adj. No hyp → TOA.
\tan24^\circ=\dfrac{r}{3.6}, so r=3.6\tan24^\circ=1.602\ldots\approx1.6 m
(b) How long is each rafter (from wall to peak)?
From 24°: 3.6 m is adj, the rafter is hyp. No opp → CAH. (Use the given 3.6, not the rise.)
\cos24^\circ=\dfrac{3.6}{R}, so R=\dfrac{3.6}{\cos24^\circ}=3.940\ldots\approx3.9 m
(c) How tall is the garage at its peak?
Total height = wall + rise
=2.7+1.602\ldots=4.302\ldots\approx4.3 m
The roof rises about 1.6 m, each rafter is about 3.9 m, and the garage is about 4.3 m tall.

Two buildings downtown

Two buildings in downtown Calgary are 30 m apart. Mo stands on the roof of the shorter building, 20 m above the street. The angle of elevation from Mo to the top of the taller building is 32°.

(a) How tall is the taller building?
Top triangle: 30 m is adj, the part above Mo is opp. No hyp → TOA.
30\tan32^\circ=18.746\ldots m
Add the 20 m building: 20+18.746\ldots=38.746\ldots\approx38.7 m
(b) What is the angle of depression from Mo to the bottom of the taller building?
Bottom triangle: 20 m is opp, 30 m is adj. No hyp → TOA.
\tan^{-1}\!\left(\dfrac{20}{30}\right)=33.690\ldots\approx34^\circ
The two triangles share the 30 m line.

Think it through

Think In Example 6, Ava and Ben stand on the same side of the drone instead. What changes in the last step?
The distance between them is the difference: 66.715\ldots-35.157\ldots=31.557\ldots\approx31.6 m. Draw the sketch first so you know whether to add or subtract.
Think Why does the dashed line in the garage make two equal right triangles?
Both sides of the roof rise at the same angle (24°) and the peak is in the middle, so each half has the same base (3.6 m), the same angle and the same height (the shared dashed line).
5

Is the Answer Reasonable?

A calculator does exactly what you type, even when it is wrong. Always ask: does this answer make sense?

Key idea · Reasonableness checks for right triangles
  • The hypotenuse is the longest side. Each leg is shorter than it.
  • The two acute angles add to 90°.
  • The bigger angle faces the longer side; the smaller angle faces the shorter side.
  • \sin\theta and \cos\theta are always less than 1 (the hyp is on the bottom). If the inverse key gives an ERROR, you probably put the hyp on top.
  • Benchmarks: at 45° the two legs are equal; at 30° the opposite side is half the hypotenuse.
  • Check the calculator is in DEG mode (\sin30^\circ=0.5).
Example 8

Spot the wrong answers

Each answer below is wrong. Explain how you can tell without solving, then find the correct answer.

(a) The legs of a right triangle are 9 m and 4 m. Jordan says the hypotenuse is 8.5 m.
The hypotenuse must be longer than both legs, and 8.5 m is shorter than 9 m.
Correct: c=\sqrt{9^2+4^2}=\sqrt{97}=9.848\ldots\approx9.8 m.
(b) In a right triangle, the side opposite \theta is 8 cm and the hypotenuse is 12 cm. Priya pressed 2ndsin 12 ÷ 8 ) ENTER and got an ERROR.
12\div8=1.5, and a sine can’t be more than 1. She put the hyp on top. It should be opp ÷ hyp.
Correct: \theta=\sin^{-1}\!\left(\dfrac{8}{12}\right)=41.810\ldots\approx42^\circ.
(c) In a right triangle with a 20° angle and a 70° angle, Leo says the leg opposite the 20° angle is 15 cm and the leg opposite the 70° angle is 5.5 cm.
The bigger angle (70°) must face the longer leg, but here it faces the shorter one. Leo mixed up the legs.
Correct: the leg opposite 20° is 5.5 cm and the leg opposite 70° is 15 cm. (Check: \tan20^\circ=0.364 and 5.5\div15=0.367 ✓)
(d) Sam stands 20 m from a flagpole and measures a 35° angle of elevation from the ground to the top. He gets a height of 20\tan35^\circ=9.5 m.
Benchmarks: at 30°, 20\tan30^\circ\approx11.5 m; at 45° the height would equal 20 m. So at 35° the height should be between 11.5 m and 20 m, and 9.5 m is too small. His calculator was in RAD mode.
Correct (DEG mode): 20\tan35^\circ=14.004\ldots\approx14.0 m.
(b) Priya’s keys give an ERROR; the right order is 2ndsin 8 ÷ 12 ) ENTER.  (d) In DEG mode, 20 × tan 35 ) ENTER gives 14.004…

Why the checks work: turn the angle

Keep the hypotenuse the same and turn the angle. Watch the two legs, \sin\theta and \cos\theta.

Think it through

Think Can \tan\theta be more than 1? When?
Yes. \tan\theta=\dfrac{\text{opp}}{\text{adj}} has no hyp. When \theta is more than 45°, the opposite leg is longer than the adjacent leg, so \tan\theta>1 (for example \tan60^\circ=1.732\ldots).
Think A ladder makes 30° with the ground and is 8 m long. Without a calculator, how high does it reach?
At 30° the opposite side is half the hypotenuse: 8\div2=4 m. (8\sin30^\circ=4 ✓) That’s a very flat, unsafe ladder!
✓

Key ideas

4 steps: sketch and label from \theta; choose the tool from what you know and want; solve (unknown on top: multiply; on the bottom: divide); check.
Tools: two sides → third side: Pythagoras. Angle → other angle: 90° −. Angle + side → side: SOH CAH TOA (leave one out). Two sides → angle: 2nd + ratio.
Solve a triangle: find all sides and angles. Use given numbers, keep full values, round at the end. Two triangles: start where you know enough; the shared side links them.
Reasonable? Hyp is the longest side; acute angles add to 90°; bigger angle faces the longer side; sin and cos are less than 1; 45° → equal legs, 30° → opp = half the hyp; DEG mode.
