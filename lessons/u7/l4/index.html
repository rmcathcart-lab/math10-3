Warm-up

The Block Staircase

You build a staircase out of cube blocks. 1 step takes 1 block. 2 steps take 1 + 2 = 3 blocks. 3 steps take 6 blocks.

How many blocks do you need for a staircase with 10 steps?

A 50 blocks
B 55 blocks
C 100 blocks
D 110 blocks
B: 55 blocks. 1 + 2 + 3 + … + 10 = 55. Here is a quick trick: build a second staircase and flip it on top of the first. Together they make a rectangle 10 blocks wide and 11 blocks tall: 10 × 11 = 110 blocks. One staircase is half of that: 110 ÷ 2 = 55. The story goes that young Carl Friedrich Gauss used this trick in school to add 1 to 100 in seconds: 100 × 101 ÷ 2 = 5 050. Every step goes 1 up and 1 across. Today: how do we measure how steep something is? We compare up ÷ across.

Similar Right Triangles and the Tangent Ratio

Right triangles with the same acute angle are similar, so opposite ÷ adjacent is always the same number for that angle. That number is the angle’s tangent. Your calculator knows it for every angle, and you can use it to find a side you can’t measure, like the height of a cell-phone tower.

1

Naming the Sides: Opposite, Adjacent and Hypotenuse

In a right triangle, the side across from the right angle is the hypotenuse (hyp). It is always the longest side. The other two sides are the legs.

Trigonometry means “triangle measurement”. It uses one acute angle (less than 90°) of a right triangle to find lengths you can’t measure directly. We often call that angle \theta. It is the Greek letter theta: just a name for an angle, the way x is a name for a number. The two legs get names that tell where they are from \theta.

Key idea   Labelling routine (do it first, every time)
  1. Step 1. Find the right angle. The side across from it is the hyp.
  2. Step 2. Find \theta. The side across from \theta is the opp (opposite side). It does not touch \theta.
  3. Step 3. The last side is the adj (adjacent side). It touches \theta and it is not the hyp. (Adjacent means “next to”.)
!
Watch out. The hyp touches \theta too, but it is never called adj. And opp and adj depend on the angle you use: if you switch to the other acute angle, opp and adj swap. The hyp never changes.

2

Similar Right Triangles: opp ÷ adj Stays the Same

In Unit 6 you learned that two triangles are similar if two pairs of angles are equal. So all right triangles with the same acute angle are similar: they share the 90° angle and \theta, and the third angle is 180^\circ-90^\circ-\theta in each one. Similar triangles have the same shape. Let’s see what that does to opp and adj.

Example 2

Three triangles with a 35° angle

Three right triangles each have a 35° angle. In your booklet they are drawn to actual size, so you can measure them with a ruler to check.

(a) Why are the three triangles similar?
Each one has a 90° angle and a 35° angle, so the third angle is 180^\circ-90^\circ-35^\circ=55^\circ in each. The angles match, so the triangles are similar.
(b) Label opp and adj from the 35° angle. Then complete the table. Round opp ÷ adj to 2 decimal places.
Triangle 1Triangle 2Triangle 3
opp (cm)1.42.13.5
adj (cm)2.03.05.0
opp ÷ adj1.4\div2.0=0.702.1\div3.0=0.703.5\div5.0=0.70
The vertical side is opp, the bottom is adj. All three ratios are 0.70.
(c) For every right triangle with a 35° angle, opp ÷ adj is about 0.70. The size of the triangle does not matter.
0.70; the size does not matter.
(d) A ramp has a 35° angle with the ground and is 10 m long along the ground (adj). Predict its height (opp).
opp ÷ adj =0.70, so opp =0.70\times10=7.0. The ramp is about 7.0 m high.

Same angle, same ratio

Grow the triangles, work out opp ÷ adj, and then change the angle.

Why it works

Triangle 2 is Triangle 1 enlarged by the scale factor k=1.5, so both legs are multiplied by 1.5: 1.4\times1.5=2.1 and 2.0\times1.5=3.0. Multiplying the top and the bottom of a ratio by the same number does not change it.

Think A right triangle with a 35° angle has adj = 40 cm. Without a calculator, about how long is opp?
opp ÷ adj is about 0.70 for 35°, so opp \approx0.70\times40=28 cm.

Each angle has its own value of opp ÷ adj, and it has a name.

Key idea   The tangent ratio. In a right triangle, for an acute angle \theta:
\tan\theta=\dfrac{\text{opp}}{\text{adj}}=\dfrac{\text{length of the side opposite }\theta}{\text{length of the side adjacent to }\theta}
For one angle, \tan\theta is always the same number, however big or small the triangle is (the triangles are similar). We say “tan \theta”; tan is short for tangent. The hyp is not used.

The tan Key on Your Calculator

You don’t have to draw and measure a triangle to find \tan\theta. Your calculator knows it for every angle.

Angles in this unit are in degrees, so the calculator must be in DEG mode: look for DEG on the screen. If it shows RAD or GRAD, press DRG, use the arrow keys to underline DEG, and press ENTER. To find \tan35^\circ, press tan 35 ) ENTER. The screen shows 0.700207538. Quick test of the mode: \tan45^\circ must be exactly 1.

Math Historyc. 860 and 1583

Tangent began as a shadow

Around the year 860, the astronomer Habash al-Hasib made the first known table of the “shadow” for each angle of the sun. Today we call that number the tangent.

Picture a stick poking straight out of a wall in the sun. Shadow ÷ stick is the tangent of the sun’s angle: one more right triangle!

The name came in 1583, from the Danish mathematician Thomas Fincke: tangens, Latin for “touching”.

A stick 1.0 m long sticks straight out of a wall. The sun is 35° up. How far down the wall is the shadow of its tip?
Shadow ÷ stick =\tan35^\circ, so the shadow is 1.0\times\tan35^\circ=0.70 m. The same 0.70 as in Example 2!

From \angle B: opp =AC=15, adj =BC=8.

(c) Which angle is bigger, \angle A or \angle B? How do you know?
\angle B. Its tangent is bigger (1.875>0.5333), and it is across from the longer leg. (\tan B is more than 1, so \angle B is more than 45°.)

4

Finding a Missing Side: Multiply or Divide

If you know an acute angle and one leg, the tangent ratio gives you the other leg. Write the ratio, put in what you know, and solve for the unknown.

Key idea   Finding a side with tan
  1. Step 1. Label opp, adj and hyp from \theta. (You need opp and adj: one you know, one you want.)
  2. Step 2. Write \tan\theta=\dfrac{\text{opp}}{\text{adj}}.   Step 3. Substitute the numbers you know.
  3. Step 4. Solve. x on the top: multiply both sides by the bottom number, so x=\text{adj}\times\tan\theta.
    x on the bottom: multiply both sides by x, then divide both sides by \tan\theta, so x=\text{opp}\div\tan\theta.
  4. Step 5. Round at the end, then check that the answer makes sense.
For 9.0\times\tan27^\circ, press 9.0 × tan 27 ) ENTER. For 6.3\div\tan40^\circ, press 6.3 ÷ tan 40 ) ENTER. The tan key opens a bracket, so close it with ) before you type anything after the angle. Don’t round \tan\theta on the way: type it all in one go.

x on the top: multiply

Example 5

Find the opposite side

Find x, to the nearest tenth.
(a)
From 27°: x is opp, 9.0 is adj.
\tan27^\circ=\dfrac{x}{9.0}
9.0\times\tan27^\circ=x  (x on top: multiply)
x=4.5857\ldots\approx4.6 cm
Check: 27^\circ<45^\circ, so opp should be shorter than adj: 4.6<9.0 ✓
(b)
From 58°: x is opp, 3.5 is adj.
\tan58^\circ=\dfrac{x}{3.5}
3.5\times\tan58^\circ=x
x=5.6011\ldots\approx5.6 m
Check: 58^\circ>45^\circ, so opp should be longer than adj: 5.6>3.5 ✓
Example 6

How tall is the tower?

A surveyor sets her angle tool on the ground, 45 m from the base of a cell-phone tower. The tool measures a 63° angle between the ground and the line to the top of the tower. How tall is the tower, to the nearest tenth of a metre?
From the 63° angle: the tower h is opp, the 45 m along the ground is adj.
\tan63^\circ=\dfrac{h}{45}
h=45\times\tan63^\circ=88.317\ldots
The tower is about 88.3 m tall. Check: 63^\circ>45^\circ, so the height should be more than 45 m ✓

x on the bottom: divide

When x is the adjacent side, it ends up on the bottom of the ratio. Two moves get it out: multiply both sides by x, then divide both sides by \tan\theta.

Example 7

Find the adjacent side

Find x, to the nearest tenth. This time x is on the bottom of the ratio.
(a)
From 40°: 6.3 is opp, x is adj.
\tan40^\circ=\dfrac{6.3}{x}
x\times\tan40^\circ=6.3  (multiply both sides by x)
x=\dfrac{6.3}{\tan40^\circ}  (divide both sides by \tan40^\circ)
x=7.508\ldots\approx7.5 cm
(b)
From 66°: 12 is opp, x is adj.
\tan66^\circ=\dfrac{12}{x}
x=\dfrac{12}{\tan66^\circ}=5.342\ldots\approx5.3 ft
Check with the other angle, 90^\circ-66^\circ=24^\circ: from 24°, x is opp and 12 is adj, so x=12\times\tan24^\circ=5.342\ldots ✓
!
Watch out. \tan40^\circ is a number (0.8391\ldots), not 40. Never write x=6.3\div40. And when x is on the bottom, the answer is not 6.3\times\tan40^\circ: that would be the answer if x were the opp side.

Example 7(b) check: use the other angle

The other acute angle is 90^\circ-66^\circ=24^\circ. From there, opp and adj swap, so x is on top and we multiply. Both ways must give the same answer.

Example 8

A wheelchair ramp

A wheelchair ramp must rise 0.48 m to reach a doorway. Accessibility guides suggest a ramp angle of about 4.8° or less.

(a) How far along the ground must the ramp reach? Round to the nearest tenth of a metre.
From 4.8°: the rise 0.48 m is opp and x (along the ground) is adj.
\tan4.8^\circ=\dfrac{0.48}{x}
x=\dfrac{0.48}{\tan4.8^\circ}=5.716\ldots
The ramp must reach about 5.7 m along the ground.
(b) Many guides say a ramp should go 12 units along for every 1 unit up (a slope of 1:12). Does your answer to (a) agree?
12\times0.48=5.76 m. That is very close to 5.7 m, so yes: an angle of 4.8° is about a 1:12 slope.
0.48 ÷ tan 4.8 ) ENTER gives 5.716167547.
5

Is the Answer Reasonable?

A calculator does what you type, even when it is wrong. Use these checks before you write your final answer.

Key idea   Quick checks for tan
  • A length is always positive. A negative answer usually means the calculator is in RAD mode.
  • The 45° check: if \theta<45^\circ, opp is shorter than adj. If \theta>45^\circ, opp is longer than adj. If \theta=45^\circ, they are equal.
  • The hyp is the longest side, so each leg must be shorter than the hyp.
  • Check with the other acute angle, 90^\circ-\theta: opp and adj swap, and you should get the same answer.
Example 9

Who is correct?

A right triangle has a 52° angle, and the side adjacent to it is 8 m. Three students found the opposite side, x.

Avax=8\div\tan52^\circ=6.3 m
Benx=8\times\tan52^\circ=-48.4 m
Colex=8\times\tan52^\circ=10.2 m
(a) Who is correct? Use a check from the box above to show that the other two answers are not reasonable.
Cole is correct: x is opp (on top), so multiply: 8\times\tan52^\circ=10.239\ldots\approx10.2 m.
Ava: 52^\circ>45^\circ, so opp must be longer than 8 m. 6.3 m is too short.
Ben: a length can’t be negative.
(b) What did Ava and Ben each do wrong?
Ava divided. That is only right when x is on the bottom (when x is the adj side).
Ben’s set-up is right, but his calculator was in RAD mode. In DEG mode he would get 10.2 m.
In DEG mode, 8 × tan 52 ) ENTER gives 10.23953306.
