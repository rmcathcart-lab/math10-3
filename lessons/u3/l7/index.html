The Mind-Reading Trick

Think of any whole number. Keep it a secret! Then:

  1. Double it.
  2. Add 10.
  3. Halve it (divide by 2).
  4. Take away the number you started with.

What number do you end with? Try it again with a different number.

?
Did everyone get the same number? Tell your neighbour what you got.
Everyone ends with 5! Halving undoes the doubling, so the +10 shrinks to +5 and you are back to your number + 5. Then taking away your number undoes your number. Only the 5 is left. Try 7: 7\to14\to24\to12\to5. Today we undo steps on purpose, to find a missing side.

Rearranging Formulas: Finding a Missing Side

You know the fence is 36 m long. You know the run is 11 m long. How wide is it? Today we use the perimeter, circumference and area formulas backwards to find a missing side, diameter or radius. Then we check by putting the answer back in.

I can…
  • substitute into a formula and undo the operations to find the missing value
  • rearrange a formula in one step to find a diameter, a radius, a side or a width
  • rearrange P=2l+2w in two steps: undo the + first, then the ×
  • check a missing side: does it make sense, and does it give back the number I started with?

Formulas, Variables and Undoing

A formula is a rule written with letters. Each letter is a variable: it stands for a number. To use a formula, you substitute: you replace each letter you know with its number. Usually you know every value but one, and you want to find the missing one.

Key idea   Formulas in this lesson
RectangleP=2l+2wl = length, w = width
SquareP=4ss = side
TriangleP=a+b+ca, b, c = the sides
CircleC=\pi d or C=2\pi rd = diameter, r = radius
Area of a rectangleA=lwlength × width

When the missing value is not by itself, you undo what the formula did to it. Each operation has an opposite that undoes it:

Key idea   Opposites:   add ⟷ subtract     multiply ⟷ divide
Balance rule: an equation is like a balance. Whatever you do to one side, do the same to the other side.
Example 1

Substitute, then undo

Substitute the numbers you know. Then undo to find the missing value.

(a) A square patio has a perimeter of 46 ft. How long is each side? Use P=4s.
46=4s
s is multiplied by 4, so divide both sides by 4.
46\div4=s
s=11.5 ft
Check: 4\times11.5=46 ✓
Each side is 11.5 ft.
(b) A rectangular rug has an area of 7.5 m². It is 3 m long. How wide is it? Use A=lw.
7.5=3\times w
Divide both sides by 3.
7.5\div3=w
w=2.5 m
Check: 3\times2.5=7.5 ✓
The rug is 2.5 m wide.
(c) Wood edging for a triangle-shaped flower bed is 10 m long. Two sides are 2.8 m and 3.5 m. How long is the third side?
P=a+b+c
10=2.8+3.5+c
10=6.3+c
Subtract 6.3 from both sides.
c=10-6.3=3.7 m
Check: 2.8+3.5+3.7=10 ✓

Think it through

Think What undoes “multiply by \pi”? What undoes “add 2l”?
Divide by \pi. Subtract 2l. Every operation is undone by its opposite.
Think In Example 1(a), why can’t you just subtract 4 from 46?
4s means 4 times s. Subtracting undoes adding, not multiplying. 46-4=42, and 4\times42=168, not 46 ✗.

One Step: Finding a Diameter, Radius or Width

Sometimes you will use the same formula many times to find the same thing. Then it is faster to rearrange the formula first. That means you write it a new way, so the thing you want to find is by itself on one side of the equal sign. When the formula only multiplies, rearranging takes one step: divide.

Key idea   One-step rearranging
C=\pi ddivide both sides by \pi:d=C\div\pi C=2\pi rdivide both sides by 2\pi:r=C\div(2\pi)(or r=d\div2) A=lwdivide both sides by l:w=A\div l(and l=A\div w) P=4sdivide both sides by 4:s=P\div4
Check: put your answer back into the first formula. You should get the number you started with.
≈
Estimate. \pi is a little more than 3, so a diameter is a little less than one third of the circumference. A circumference of 90 cm means a diameter of a bit under 30 cm.
For r=C\div(2\pi), use brackets: 113 ÷ ( 2 × π ) ENTER. Or divide twice: 113 ÷ 2 ÷ π ENTER. Typing 113 ÷ 2 × π is wrong: it multiplies by \pi.

Cover the one you want

The formula triangle holds all three ways to write C=\pi d. Cover the letter you want to find; what is left tells you what to do.

Why the brackets matter

The calculator works left to right for × and ÷. Without brackets, “÷ 2 × π” divides by 2 and then multiplies by π.

Example 2

A tree trunk and a table

(a) An arborist (a tree expert) wraps a tape measure around a spruce trunk. The circumference is 188 cm. Find the diameter of the trunk, to the nearest tenth of a centimetre.
Estimate: 188\div3\approx63, so a bit less than 63 cm.
d=C\div\pi
d=188\div\pi=59.84\ldots\approx59.8 cm
Check: \pi\times59.8=187.9\approx188 ✓
The trunk is about 59.8 cm across.
(b) A round table has 113 in of metal trim around its edge. Find the radius of the table, to the nearest tenth of an inch.
Estimate: 113\div6\approx19
r=C\div(2\pi)
r=113\div(2\times\pi)=17.98\ldots\approx18.0 in
Check: 2\times\pi\times18.0=113.1\approx113 ✓
The radius is about 18.0 in.
(a) 188 ÷ π ENTER shows 59.84…   (b) 113 ÷ ( 2 × π ) ENTER shows 17.98…

A missing side from the area: w=A\div l

A rectangle’s area is rows of squares. If you know the area and one side, ask: how many rows of that length fill the area?

Example 3

A bedroom and a patio

(a) Kai’s rectangular bedroom floor has an area of 132 ft². The room is 12 ft long. How wide is it?
w=A\div l
w=132\div12=11 ft
Check: 12\times11=132 ✓
The room is 11 ft wide.
(b) Rearrange A=lw to find the length. Then use it: a rectangular patio uses 22.5 m² of paving stones and is 3.6 m wide. How long is it?
Divide both sides by w: l=A\div w
l=22.5\div3.6=6.25 m
Check: 6.25\times3.6=22.5 ✓
The patio is 6.25 m long.

Think it through

Think A bigger spruce has a circumference of 376 cm, double the one in Example 2. What happens to its diameter?
It doubles too: 376\div\pi=119.68\ldots\approx119.7 cm, about 2\times59.8.
Think Kai’s room has the same 132 ft² but is 11 ft long instead. How wide is it now?
132\div11=12 ft. It is the same room turned sideways.

Two Steps: The Perimeter of a Rectangle

In P=2l+2w, the formula does two things to w: it multiplies by 2, then it adds 2l. To undo, go in reverse order, like taking off your shoes before your socks.

Key idea   Rearranging in two steps
Step 1 Undo the + or − first.Step 2 Then undo the × or ÷.
P=2l+2w P-2l=2wStep 1: subtract 2l from both sides. (P-2l)\div2=wStep 2: divide both sides by 2.
So w=(P-2l)\div2   and in the same way   l=(P-2w)\div2
!
Watch out. Subtract two lengths (2l), not one. And don’t forget Step 2: P-2l is the two widths together, not one width.
↔
Another way. Half of the perimeter is one length plus one width. So you can also find w=P\div2-l. For P=36 and l=11: 36\div2-11=18-11=7. Same answer.
Example 4

Ava’s dog run

Ava has 36 m of fencing for a rectangular dog run. She wants it to be 11 m long. How wide will it be?

(a) Solve by substituting first.
P=2l+2w
36=2(11)+2w
36=22+2w
36-22=2w (subtract 22 from both sides)
14=2w
w=14\div2=7 m
(b) Solve by rearranging first. Check your answer.
w=(P-2l)\div2
w=(36-2\times11)\div2
w=(36-22)\div2=14\div2=7 m
Check: 2(11)+2(7)=22+14=36 ✓
The dog run will be 7 m wide.
i
Substitute first or rearrange first? Both ways give the same answer. Substituting first is often easier for one question. Rearranging first is faster when you must do the same thing many times, like filling in a table.

See it with the fence

Lay the whole fence out straight. Take off the two lengths. What is left is the two widths together: split it in half.

Two more missing sides

(a) A rectangular lot in Airdrie has a perimeter of 84 m. It is 16.5 m wide. How long is it? Check your answer.
l=(P-2w)\div2
l=(84-2\times16.5)\div2=(84-33)\div2=51\div2=25.5 m

Check Your Answer

Always check a missing side in two ways:

Key idea
1. Does it make sense? A width is less than half the perimeter. A diameter is about a third of the circumference. A length can’t be negative. 2. Substitute back. Put your answer into the original formula. You should get the number you started with.
Example 6

Find the mistake

Each student made a mistake. Check their answer, explain the mistake, and find the correct answer.

(a) A rectangle has a perimeter of 50 cm and a length of 15 cm. Noah wrote: w=50-2(15)=20 cm.
Check: 2(15)+2(20)=70, not 50 ✗
Noah forgot Step 2. 50-30=20 is both widths.
w=(50-30)\div2=10 cm
Check: 2(15)+2(10)=50 ✓
(b) A round wading pool has a circumference of 94.2 in. Mei wrote: d=94.2\times\pi=295.9 in.
Makes sense? No. A diameter can’t be longer than the distance around.
Mei multiplied by \pi. To undo \times\pi, divide.
d=94.2\div\pi=29.98\ldots\approx30.0 in
Check: \pi\times30.0=94.25\approx94.2 ✓

Think it through

Think Could a rectangle with a perimeter of 20 m be 12 m long?
No. w=(20-24)\div2=-2 m, and a width can’t be negative. Two lengths of 12 m already use 24 m, more than all the fence.
Think Jin says a pool with a circumference of 60 ft has a diameter of about 30 ft. Does that make sense?
No. A diameter is about a third of the circumference, so about 20 ft. (60\div\pi=19.1 ft.) Jin divided by 2.
Example 7

Maya’s garden edging

Maya has $78.00 to spend on garden edging. The edging costs $3.25 per foot. She wants to put it all the way around a rectangular garden bed that is 7 ft long. How wide can the bed be?

Feet of edging she can buy = money ÷ price per foot =\$78.00\div\$3.25=24 ft. This is the perimeter.
w=(P-2l)\div2=(24-2\times7)\div2=(24-14)\div2=10\div2=5 ft
Check: 2(7)+2(5)=24 ft ✓; 24\times\$3.25=\$78.00 ✓
The garden bed can be 5 ft wide.
✓

Key ideas

Substitute what you know, then undo with the opposite: + ⟷ −, × ⟷ ÷. Whatever you do to one side, do to the other side.
One step: d=C\div\pi, r=C\div(2\pi), w=A\div l, s=P\div4. Use brackets for 2\pi on the calculator.
Two steps: undo the + first, then the ×. w=(P-2l)\div2 and l=(P-2w)\div2. Subtract two lengths.
Check: does it make sense (a diameter is about a third of C)? Put it back into the original formula: you should get the number you started with.
