Three Cuts, How Many Pieces?

You have one round pizza and a long, sharp pizza cutter.

You may make 3 straight cuts. Each cut goes all the way across. The pieces do not have to be the same size, and you can’t move or stack the pieces.

What is the greatest number of pieces you can get?

A 6 pieces
B 7 pieces
C 8 pieces
D 9 pieces
B, 7 pieces. Cutting through the centre every time gives only 6. Instead, make each new cut cross every earlier cut, at a new point (not where two cuts already meet). The 3rd cut crosses 2 cuts, so it passes through 3 pieces and splits each one: 4+3=7. Bonus: with 4 cuts, the most is 11. The pattern adds 2, then 3, then 4: 2, 4, 7, 11, …

A coffee shop floor, a shed wall, a backyard with a deck, a pond in a park: most real shapes are not one simple shape. Today we split shapes into parts we know, take away the cut-outs, count squares on a grid, and check our answers a second way.

I can…

Composite Shapes: Split and Add

A composite shape is a shape made of two or more simple shapes, such as rectangles, triangles and half circles. Most floor plans, yards and walls are composite shapes. There is no single formula for them, but you can split them into shapes you already know.

Key idea   Split and add
1Draw lines to split the shape into rectangles, triangles or circle parts. 2Find any missing side lengths. 3Find the area of each part. 4Add the areas. Write the answer in square units (m², ft², …).
A semicircle is half a circle:  A=\tfrac12\pi r^2  (the radius r is half the diameter).
!
Watch out. The parts must not overlap. Each square unit is counted only once. To find a missing side, look across the shape: the short sides on one side add up to the long side on the other side.
Think Can you split the coffee shop floor in Example 1 into three rectangles? Would the area change?
Yes, for example with both lines. The area does not change: it is still 51 m². Splitting more just means more small parts to add (and more chances for a slip).

The overlap trap

What happens if two parts overlap? Some square metres get counted twice.

Example 1

The coffee shop floor

The floor plan shows the seating area of a coffee shop. All the corners are square corners (90°).

(a) Find the missing side lengths a and b.
a=9-5=4 m    b=7-4=3 m
Hint. The top (5 m) and a together are as long as the bottom (9 m).
(b) Split the floor with a vertical (up-and-down) line into two rectangles. Find the area of the floor.
A: 5\times7=35 m²
B: 4\times4=16 m²  (B is a by 4 m)
Total: 35+16=51 m²
(c) Split it another way, with a horizontal (side-to-side) line. Do you get the same area?
C: 9\times4=36 m²
D: 5\times3=15 m²  (D is 5 m by b)
Total: 36+15=51 m². Yes, the same area.

Example 2

Siding for Mila’s shed

Mila is putting new siding on the end wall of her garden shed. The wall is a rectangle with a triangle on top.

(a) Find the area of the rectangle and of the triangle.
Rectangle: 10\times7.5=75 ft²
Triangle: \tfrac12\times10\times3.5=17.5 ft²
(b) What is the total area of the wall?
75+17.5=92.5 ft²  (or 92\tfrac12 ft²)
(c) The shed has two end walls like this. One box of siding covers 50 ft². How many boxes does Mila need for both end walls?
Both walls: 2\times92.5=185 ft²  ·  185\div50=3.7 boxes. She cannot buy part of a box, so round up: Mila needs 4 boxes.

Half a circle on top

A semicircle is half a circle, so its area is half of \pi r^2. The radius is half the width it sits on.

Example 3

An arched window

An arched window is a rectangle 36 in wide and 48 in tall, with a semicircle on top.

(a) Find the area of the rectangle.
36\times48=1\,728 in²
(b) Find the area of the semicircle.
r=36\div2=18 in
A=\tfrac12\times\pi\times18^2=508.938\ldots in²
(c) Find the total area of the window, to the nearest square inch.
1\,728+508.938\ldots=2\,236.938\ldots
The window is about 2 237 in².
(d) Window film is sold by the square foot. Change your answer to square feet, to the nearest tenth. (1 ft² = 144 in²)
2\,236.938\div144=15.534\ldots
The window is about 15.5 ft².

Whole Minus the Cut-Out

Some shapes have a hole or a piece missing: a lawn with a pool in it, a wall with a door and a window. For these, find the area of the whole shape, then subtract the area of each cut-out (the part that is not included).

Key idea   Area = area of the whole shape − area of the cut-out(s)
Many composite shapes can be done either way: split and add, or whole minus cut-out. Using both is a good way to check.
Example 4

The coffee shop again, and the Nguyens’ backyard

(a) Find the area of the coffee shop floor from Example 1 using whole minus cut-out.
Whole: 9\times7=63 m²
Cut-out corner: a\times b=4\times3=12 m²
63-12=51 m² (the same as Example 1)
(b) The Nguyens’ backyard is a rectangle 15 m by 12 m. It has a deck 6 m by 4.5 m and a round flower bed 3 m across. The rest will be sod (grass). Find the area of sod, to the nearest hundredth of a square metre.
Whole yard: 15\times12=180 m²   Deck: 6\times4.5=27 m²
Flower bed: r=3\div2=1.5 m,  \pi\times1.5^2=7.0685\ldots m²
Sod: 180-27-7.0685\ldots=145.931\ldots\approx145.93 m²
(c) Sod costs $4.80 per m². The landscaper rounds the area up to the next whole square metre. What will the sod cost (before tax)?
145.93 m² rounds up to 146 m².  146\times\$4.80=\$700.80   The sod will cost $700.80.

Three ways, one answer

The coffee shop floor three ways: two different splits, and whole minus cut-out.

Example 5

Owen paints his bedroom wall

Owen is painting one wall of his bedroom. The wall is 12 ft long and 8 ft high. It has a door 3 ft wide and 6 ft 8 in high, and a window 4 ft wide and 3 ft 6 in high. He will not paint the door or the window.

(a) Change the door height and the window height to feet.
6 ft 8 in = 6\tfrac{8}{12}=6\tfrac23 ft     3 ft 6 in = 3\tfrac12=3.5 ft
8 in is \tfrac{8}{12}=\tfrac23 of a foot: 6\tfrac23 ft.   6 in is half a foot: 3.5 ft.
(b) Find the area Owen will paint.
Wall: 12\times8=96 ft²   Door: 3\times6\tfrac23=3\times\tfrac{20}{3}=20 ft²
Window: 4\times3.5=14 ft²   Paint: 96-20-14=62 ft²
(c) Owen will paint two coats. One can of paint covers about 100 ft². How many cans does he need?
Two coats: 2\times62=124 ft².  124\div100=1.24 cans, so round up: Owen needs 2 cans.
The calculator can do 6\tfrac23 for you: 3 × 6 A b/c 2 A b/c 3 ENTER shows 20. Or use the decimal: 3 × ( 6 + 8 ÷ 12 ) ENTER.

Irregular Shapes: Counting Squares

An irregular shape has curved or uneven sides, like a pond, a lake or a spill. There is no formula for it, so we estimate its area with a grid.

Full square: completely inside the shape.
Part square: cut by the edge of the shape. Some are almost full and some are almost empty, so on average a part square is about half full. Two part squares make about one full square.
Key idea   Area ≈ (full squares + part squares ÷ 2) × area of one square
✓
Count carefully. Put a small mark in each square as you count it (a tick for full, a dot for part), so you do not count a square twice or miss one.
Example 6

The pond in the park

A park map shows a pond on a grid. Each square is 1 m by 1 m, so each square is 1 m².

(a) Complete the table.
Full squares12
Part squares20
Part squares ÷ 220\div2=10
Estimate of area12+10=22 m²
12 full, 20 part, 20\div2=10, estimate 12+10=22 m²
(b) Finish the sentence. The pond’s area is about 22 m².
about 22 m²

Why do two part squares make about one full square?

Pull the 20 part squares of the pond out of the grid and pair them up: an almost-empty one with an almost-full one.

Think Why not count every part square as a full square?
It would count a lot of grass as pond: 12+20=32 m², far more than the real 21.5 m². Counting none of them (12 m²) is far too small. Half is the fair middle.

When one square stands for more

When each square stands for more than 1 square unit, find the area of one square first, then multiply. To check an estimate, draw a rectangle around the shape (the real area is less) and a rectangle inside it (the real area is more). A good estimate is between the two.

Example 7

A slough in a field

A farmer’s map shows a slough (a prairie pond) in a field. On the map, each square stands for 10 m by 10 m.

(a) What area does one square stand for?
10\times10=100 m²
(b) Estimate the area of the slough.
Full: 16   Part: 22   22\div2=11
16+11=27 squares   27\times100=2\,700 m²
(c) On the map, draw the smallest rectangle (on grid lines) that goes all the way around the slough. Find the area it stands for.
8\times5=40 squares
40\times100=4\,000 m². The slough is less than this.
(d) Draw the biggest rectangle that fits inside the slough. Find its area. Is your estimate from (b) between the two rectangles?
5\times3=15 squares, so 1 500 m²
Yes: 1\,500<2\,700<4\,000 m².

Is My Answer Reasonable? Checking a Second Way

Area mistakes are easy to make: a missing side, an overlap, a diameter used as a radius. Always check your answer.

Key idea   Ways to check an area
Second waySplit the shape a different way, or use whole minus cut-out. You should get the same answer. Rectangle aroundThe answer must be less than the area of a rectangle that goes around the shape. UnitsArea is always in square units (m², ft², …).
Example 8

The skating oval

A town floods an outdoor skating oval. It is a rectangle 40 m long and 20 m wide, with a semicircle on each end.

(a) Estimate first. What are the length and width of a rectangle that goes around the whole oval? What is its area?
Length: 10+40+10=60 m (each end adds a radius). Width: 20 m.
60\times20=1\,200 m². The oval must be less than 1 200 m².
(b) Find the area of the oval, to the nearest square metre. Is it less than your answer to (a)?
Rectangle: 40\times20=800 m²
Two semicircles make one circle, r=20\div2=10 m:  \pi\times10^2=314.159\ldots
800+314.159\ldots\approx1\,114 m². Yes, less than 1 200 m².
(c) Jordan got 2 057 m². Use part (a) to explain how you know his answer is wrong. What mistake do you think he made?
2 057 m² is more than the 1 200 m² rectangle that goes around the oval, so it cannot be right. He used the diameter (20 m) as the radius: \pi\times20^2=1\,256.6\ldots, and 800+1\,256.6\ldots\approx2\,057.

The verdict: which answer can be right?

✓

Key ideas

Split and add. Split a composite shape into rectangles, triangles and semicircles (\tfrac12\pi r^2). Find missing sides by looking across. No overlaps.
Whole minus cut-out. Area of the whole shape, then subtract each hole: a deck, a pool, a door, a window.
Irregular shapes: area ≈ (full squares + part squares ÷ 2) × area of one square.
Check: a second way gives the same answer; the area is less than a rectangle around the shape; square units. Round up when you buy boxes, cans or sod.
