A Rope Around the Earth

A rope is wrapped tightly around the Earth at the equator. It is about 40 000 km long. Now we add just 1 metre to the rope and lift it evenly, all the way around.

How big is the gap under the rope?

A thinner than a sheet of paper
B big enough for a cat to crawl under
C taller than a person
B, about 16 cm! Most people guess A. But adding 1 m to the distance around makes the circle about 1\div\pi\approx0.32 m wider, so the rope lifts about 16 cm everywhere: a cat could crawl under it all the way around the world. The twist: it is the same 16 cm for a rope around a basketball. Today we learn the rule behind it.

Perimeter of a Polygon

A polygon is a flat shape with straight sides, like a triangle, a rectangle or a hexagon. The perimeter is the total distance around the outside of a shape. To find it, add the lengths of all the sides. Perimeter is a length, so its unit is cm, m, in or ft (no small 2).

Key idea
Perimeter P = the sum of all the side lengths
Square (side s): P=4s
Rectangle (length l, width w): P=2l+2w or P=2(l+w)
!
Watch out. Start at one corner and go around the shape. Tick each side as you add it, so you do not miss one or count one twice. All the sides must be in the same unit before you add.
⌐
Reading diagrams. A small square in a corner means a right angle (a square corner, 90°). Small tick marks on sides mean those sides are equal. Diagrams are often not drawn to scale: use the numbers, not a ruler.

Rectangles and squares: a fence around a yard

A rectangle has two lengths and two widths. Walk around it and you add l+w+l+w: that is 2l+2w, or 2(l+w) (add one length and one width, then double).

A square has 4 equal sides: 36\div4=9 cm.

Finding Missing Side Lengths

Sometimes a diagram does not show every side. When all the corners are right angles (like an L shape), use the sides you know:

  • The sides that go across at the top add up to the side across the bottom.
  • The sides that go up and down on one side add up to the side on the other.
Key idea   Missing side = the long side − the part you know.   Then add all the sides, including the ones you found.
✓
Check. When a shape is a rectangle with one corner cut out (an L shape), its perimeter is the same as the perimeter of the whole rectangle around it. The cut just moves two sides inward. (This shortcut does not work when a notch is cut into the middle of a side.)
Example 3

Rosa’s L-shaped garden

All corners are right angles.

Rosa is planting a garden shaped like an L. She wants to put a short border fence around it.

(a) Find the missing side lengths a and b.
a=9.6-4.1=5.5 m (across: top parts add to the bottom)
b=7.2-2.8=4.4 m (up and down: right parts add to the left)
(b) Find the perimeter of the garden.
P=9.6+2.8+5.5+4.4+4.1+7.2=33.6 m
Rosa needs 33.6 m of border fence.
(c) Check with the shortcut for an L shape.
Rectangle around it: 9.6 m by 7.2 m.
2(9.6+7.2)=2\times16.8=33.6 m ✓

Circumference of a Circle

A circle has no straight sides, so we need new words.

  • The centre is the point in the middle.
  • The radius (r) is the distance from the centre to the edge.
  • The diameter (d) is the distance all the way across, through the centre. It is two radii: d=2r.
  • The circumference (C) is the distance around the circle. It is the perimeter of a circle.

For every circle, the circumference is a little more than 3 times the diameter. The exact number is called pi (say “pie”), written \pi. It is about 3.14, and its decimals go on forever.

Key idea   Circumference: C=\pi d or C=2\pi r  (\pi\approx3.14)
Estimate: C\approx3\times d. For example, a circle 30 cm across goes around a bit more than 3\times30=90 cm.
Use the π key: it holds many more decimals than 3.14. π × 30 ENTER shows 94.24777961. With a radius of 7: 2 × π × 7 ENTER shows 43.98229715. (Using 3.14 gives an answer that is a tiny bit different.)
!
Watch out. If you are given the radius, double it first (or use C=2\pi r). The most common mistake is \pi\times r, which gives only half the circumference.
Think A pizza has a radius of 7 in. Sam writes C=\pi\times7\approx22.0 in. How can you tell this is wrong without a calculator?
The diameter is 14 in, so the estimate is 3\times14=42 in. 22 in is only about half of that: Sam used the radius instead of the diameter.

Why is it always about 3.14?

Wrap a string around any round object, then lay it next to the diameter. Small or huge, the string is always a bit more than 3 diameters: C\div d\approx3.14. That number is \pi.

One turn of a wheel

Each time a wheel turns once, it rolls forward a distance equal to its circumference.

Example 6

Nadia’s bike

Each time a bike wheel turns once, the bike rolls forward a distance equal to the wheel’s circumference. Nadia’s bike wheel has a diameter of 66 cm.

(a) How far does the bike roll when the wheel turns once? Round to the nearest tenth of a centimetre.
C=\pi d=\pi\times66
C=207.345\ldots
The bike rolls about 207.3 cm in one turn.
(b) How far does the bike roll in 500 turns? Give your answer in metres, to the nearest tenth.
500\times207.345\ldots=103\,672.5\ldots cm
103\,672.5\div100=1036.7 m
About 1036.7 m (just over 1 km).
(c) About how many turns does the wheel make in 1 km?
1 km = 1000 m = 100 000 cm
100\,000\div207.345\ldots\approx482.3
The wheel turns about 482 times in 1 km.
Math HistoryEgypt · about 240 BCE

A Stick and a Shadow

Eratosthenes ran the great library in Alexandria, Egypt. In the town of Syene, far to the south, the noon Sun on the longest day of the year shone straight down: sticks had no shadow.

At the same time in Alexandria, a stick cast a short shadow. Its angle showed the towns were 1/50 of the way around the Earth, about 5000 stadia apart (an old Greek unit). So the Earth was about 50\times5000=250\,000 stadia around.

Today we know the Earth is about 40 000 km around. Use C=\pi d: about how wide is the Earth?
d=C\div\pi=40\,000\div\pi\approx12\,700 km.

Shapes With Half Circles

A semicircle is half of a circle. Its curved edge is half of the circumference. Two semicircles with the same diameter make one whole circle.

Many shapes are made of a rectangle and one or two semicircles (an arched window, a running track). To find the perimeter, add only the edges on the outside. Do not add a line where two pieces join.

Key idea
Curved edge of a semicircle = \pi d\div2
Two equal semicircles = \pi d (one whole circle)
Perimeter = the straight edges on the outside + the curved edges
!
Watch out. A half circle on its own has a straight edge too (its diameter). Remember to add it. But when the half circle sits on a rectangle, that line is inside the shape, so it is not part of the perimeter.
Think A circle has a diameter of 10 m. Is half of it (a semicircle with its straight edge) half the perimeter of the circle?
No. The circle: \pi\times10\approx31.4 m. The semicircle: curved 31.4\div2=15.7 m plus the straight edge 10 m = 25.7 m. That is much more than half of 31.4 m.
Example 7

Rugs, windows and skating paths

Find the perimeter of each shape. Round to the nearest tenth.

(a) a half-circle rug
Curved: \pi\times4\div2=6.283\ldots ft
Straight: 4 ft

P\approx10.3 ft
(b) an arched window frame
The arch is half a circle with d=90 cm: \pi\times90\div2=141.37\ldots cm

P=330+141.37\approx471.4 cm
(The dashed line is inside: not added.)
(c) an outdoor skating path: two straight sides 40 m long, and a semicircle at each end
Two semicircles, d=18 m, make one circle: \pi\times18=56.548\ldots m
Straight: 40+40=80 m

Fencing, Trim and Edging: Finding the Cost

Fencing a yard, putting baseboard in a room and edging a garden are all perimeter jobs. Follow these steps.

  1. Step 1. Find the length needed. Use only the sides that need it, and subtract any gaps (doors, gates).
  2. Step 2. If it is sold by the metre or foot: cost = length × price per metre (or per foot).
  3. Step 3. If it is sold in pieces or rolls of a fixed length: divide to find how many, then round up. You cannot buy part of a piece. Cost = number of pieces × price per piece.
Key idea   Cost = length × price per unit length     Pieces needed = length needed ÷ length of one piece → always round up
!
Watch out. Round pieces up even when the decimal is small: 5.1 pieces means you must buy 6. If you buy 5, you will be short.
Think Jamal makes the gate gap 1.5 m instead of 1.2 m. How much less does the fencing cost?
0.3 m less fencing: 0.3\times\$18.75=\$5.625, about $5.63 less (before tax).
Example 8

Jamal’s dog run

Jamal is fencing a rectangular dog run, 9.5 m by 6 m. He will leave a 1.2 m space for a gate. Fencing costs $18.75 per metre, and the gate costs $129.00.

(a) Find the perimeter of the dog run.
P=2(9.5+6)=2\times15.5=31 m
(b) How many metres of fencing does Jamal need?
31-1.2=29.8 m of fencing
(c) Find the cost of the fencing.
29.8\times\$18.75=\$558.75
(d) Find the total cost of the fencing and the gate, with 5% GST.
Before tax: \$558.75+\$129.00=\$687.75
With GST: \$687.75\times1.05=722.1375\approx\$722.14
The total cost is $722.14.
Example 9

Shawna’s baseboard

Shawna is putting new baseboard around her bedroom. The room is a rectangle 11 ft 6 in by 13 ft 3 in. There is no baseboard across the 2 ft 8 in doorway. Baseboard is sold in 8 ft pieces for $13.98 each. (Pieces can be cut and joined anywhere.)

(a) Find the perimeter of the room in feet and inches.
11 ft 6 in + 13 ft 3 in = 24 ft 9 in
P=2\times 24 ft 9 in = 48 ft 18 in = 49 ft 6 in
(b) How much baseboard does Shawna need?
49 ft 6 in − 2 ft 8 in
= 48 ft 18 in − 2 ft 8 in (regroup 1 ft as 12 in)
= 46 ft 10 in
(c) How many 8 ft pieces must she buy, and what do they cost?
46 ft 10 in is about 46.83 ft. 46.83\div8\approx5.85
Round up: 6 pieces. (5 pieces = 40 ft is not enough.)
6\times\$13.98=\$83.88
(d) How much baseboard will be left over?
6 pieces = 6\times8=48 ft
48 ft − 46 ft 10 in = 47 ft 12 in − 46 ft 10 in
= 1 ft 2 in left over

Key ideas

Perimeter: add all the sides, in the same unit. Square P=4s; rectangle P=2l+2w=2(l+w). Tick each side so you don’t miss one.
Missing sides: the long side − the part you know. An L shape has the same perimeter as the rectangle around it (not true for a notch in the middle of a side).
Circumference: C=\pi d=2\pi r, about 3\times d. Given the radius? Double it first. One turn of a wheel rolls one circumference.
Half circles: curved edge \pi d\div2; add outside edges only. Cost: length × price; pieces = length ÷ piece length, round up.
