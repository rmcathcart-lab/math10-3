The Bookworm’s Trip

Three books stand on a shelf in order: Volume 1, Volume 2, Volume 3. Their spines face you, like in a library.

In each book, the pages are 2 in thick. Each cover is \tfrac18 in thick.

A bookworm starts at page 1 of Volume 1. It chews in a straight line to the last page of Volume 3.

How far does the bookworm chew?

A 6\tfrac34 in
B 6 in
C 2\tfrac12 in
D 2 in
C: only 2\tfrac12 in! Stand a book on a shelf with its spine facing you: its front cover is on the right. So page 1 of Volume 1 sits right next to Volume 2, and the last page of Volume 3 is on its left, also next to Volume 2. The worm only chews through 4 covers and the pages of Volume 2: 2+4\times\tfrac18=2+\tfrac48=2\tfrac12 in. Grab two books from the shelf and check!

Imperial Units of Length

Canada’s official system of measurement is the SI (metric) system. The imperial system is an older system. The United States still uses it, and so do many Canadian trades. The imperial units of length are the inch, the foot, the yard and the mile.

Key idea   Imperial units of length
UnitSymbolHow it relatesReferent (about this long)
inchin  or  ″the smallest unitthe width of your thumb at the top knuckle
footft  or  ′1 ft = 12 ina school ruler; a sheet of paper is 11 in long
yardyd1 yd = 3 ft = 36 inone long step; a little shorter than a metre stick
milemi1 mi = 1760 yd = 5280 ftabout a 20-minute walk
!
Watch out. Two marks ″ mean inches and one mark ′ means feet: 6′ is 6 feet (a tall person), 6″ is 6 inches (half a ruler). Together, 5′ 9″ means 5 feet 9 inches.

Where Canadians still use imperial units: building (a “2 by 4”, sheets of plywood and drywall are 4 ft by 8 ft, wall studs 16 in apart), people’s heights, TV and phone screens (measured corner to corner in inches), tire and wheel sizes, plumbing pipe, sports (a CFL field is 110 yd long, golf in yards), and road signs in miles when you drive in the United States.

Math History1324

An inch was three grains of barley

Long ago there were no factory-made rulers. In 1324, an English law under King Edward II said an inch is three grains of barley, dry and round, laid end to end.

One grain was called a barleycorn, so a barleycorn is \tfrac13 of an inch. Your shoe size still uses it: each shoe size up is one barleycorn longer.

Today the inch is exact: since 1959, 1 inch = 2.54 cm.

How many barleycorns long is a 1-foot ruler?
1 ft = 12 in, and each inch is 3 barleycorns: 12\times3=36 barleycorns.

How an Inch Is Split Up

An imperial ruler splits each inch into 2 halves, then 4 quarters, then 8 eighths, then 16 sixteenths. The lines have different lengths so you can tell them apart.

Key idea   The lines on a ruler
longest linea whole inch (1, 2, 3, …) next longesta half: \tfrac12 nextquarters: \tfrac14,\ \tfrac34 nexteighths: \tfrac18,\ \tfrac38,\ \tfrac58,\ \tfrac78 shortest linesixteenths: \tfrac1{16},\ \tfrac3{16},\ \tfrac5{16},\ \dots,\ \tfrac{15}{16}
Naming a line
To name any line, count the small spaces from the last whole inch. Each space is \tfrac1{16} in. Then write the fraction in simplest form:
\tfrac8{16}=\tfrac12     \tfrac{12}{16}=\tfrac34     \tfrac6{16}=\tfrac38
The TI-30XIIS simplifies for you: 6 A b/c 16 ENTER shows 3⌟8, which means \tfrac38.

Why does \tfrac{12}{16}=\tfrac34? The lines line up

Put a ruler marked in sixteenths above rulers marked in eighths, quarters and halves. A sixteenths line that sits right on a bigger line has a simpler name.

Think Without counting, how can you tell that a line is not an odd sixteenth?
It is longer than the shortest lines. Only the odd sixteenths (\tfrac1{16},\tfrac3{16},\dots) get the shortest lines.

Reading a Ruler

Key idea   Reading a ruler or tape measure
  • Step 1. The whole inches are the last number you passed.
  • Step 2. The fraction is how far past that number: use the length of the line, or count the \tfrac1{16} spaces.
  • Step 3. Write a mixed number in simplest form, with the unit: 4\tfrac7{16} in (say “four and seven-sixteenths inches”).

Try it: read the ruler

Measuring an object

!
Watch out. Line up the end of the object with the 0 line, not with the end of the ruler. Many rulers have a little blank space before 0. If the object does not start at 0, you must take away the starting mark.
Example 4

How long is each object?

(a) the key
6 spaces past 2: \tfrac6{16}=\tfrac38. The key is 2\tfrac38 in.
(b) the crayon
4 spaces past 5: \tfrac4{16}=\tfrac14. The crayon is 5\tfrac14 in.
(c) The screw starts at the 1 in line. How long is it?
It ends at 4\tfrac58 in. It starts at 1 in. From 1 to 4 is 3 whole inches, plus \tfrac58 more. Or subtract: 4\tfrac58-1=3\tfrac58. The screw is 3\tfrac58 in long.

Measuring to the Nearest \tfrac18 or \tfrac1{16} Inch

An edge often falls between two lines. Then you use the closest line.

Key idea
  • To the nearest \tfrac1{16} in: use all the lines, and pick the closest one.
  • To the nearest \tfrac18 in: skip the shortest (\tfrac1{16}) lines. Pick the closest of the longer lines.

A rough job (framing a wall) is often fine to the nearest \tfrac18 in. Finish work (trim, cabinets) needs the nearest \tfrac1{16} in. Using smaller marks gives a more precise measurement.

Example 5

Nearest sixteenth, nearest eighth

This is the part of a ruler from 2 in to 3 in, made bigger. Measure each arrow.
nearest \tfrac1{16} innearest \tfrac18 in
P2\tfrac5{16} in2\tfrac14 in
Q2\tfrac{13}{16} in2\tfrac78 in
P is just before the 5th sixteenth line: 2\tfrac5{16} in. Skipping the shortest lines, P is between 2\tfrac28\,(=2\tfrac14) and 2\tfrac38, closer to 2\tfrac14 in. Q is just past the 13th sixteenth line: 2\tfrac{13}{16} in. Between 2\tfrac68 and 2\tfrac78, it is closer to 2\tfrac78 in.

Tape Measures and Feet and Inches

A tape measure is a long ruler that rolls up. Its hook at the end is a little loose on purpose. It slides by its own thickness, so the reading is right when you hook it over an edge and when you push it against a wall.

The numbers on a tape keep counting inches past 12: 13, 14, 15, … At each foot many tapes add a small tag: 1F at 12 in, 2F at 24 in, 3F at 36 in. Most tapes also show every 16 in in red (16, 32, 48, …) because wall studs are built 16 in apart.

Key idea   Feet and inches:   2 feet 1\tfrac58 inches = 2'\,1\tfrac58'' = 2 ft 1\tfrac58 in. The inches part is always less than 12.
On a tape: the feet are the last foot tag you passed (1F, 2F, …). The inches are how far past that tag. For example, 25\tfrac58 in is 1\tfrac58 in past 2F (24 in), so it is 2'\,1\tfrac58''. (Lesson 4 shows how to change any number of inches.)

Why is the hook loose?

Pull the tape and the hook slides out. Push it and the hook slides in. It moves by exactly its own thickness.

Key ideas

Units: 1 ft = 12 in · 1 yd = 3 ft = 36 in · 1 mi = 1760 yd = 5280 ft. ″ means inches, ′ means feet. Referents: thumb, ruler, long step, 20-minute walk.
The lines: longest = inch, then \tfrac12, quarters, eighths, and the shortest are the odd sixteenths. Count the \tfrac1{16} spaces and write the fraction in simplest form.
Reading: the last number passed, then the fraction past it. Start at the 0 line, or take away the starting mark. Nearest \tfrac18: skip the shortest lines.
Feet and inches: the last foot tag passed (1F, 2F, …), then the inches past it, less than 12: 25\tfrac58 in =2'\,1\tfrac58''. To order sizes, use sixteenths.
