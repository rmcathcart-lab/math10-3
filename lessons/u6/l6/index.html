One Object, Three Shadows

A mystery object sits in the corner of a room. A flashlight shines on it from above, then from the front, then from the side.

The three shadows are a circle, a square and a triangle.

Can ONE solid object make all three shadows? If you think so, sketch it.

A Yes: one object can make all three
B No: that’s impossible
Yes! Start with a can that is as tall as it is wide. From above its shadow is a circle, and from the front it is a square. Now slice off two pieces, from one line across the top down to the bottom edge on each side. From the front it is still a square, but from the side it is now a triangle. The same object can look very different from different directions. Today we use shadows to measure things we can’t reach.

Indirect Measurement and the Shadow Method

Some lengths are too high or too far away to measure with a tape: a flagpole, a tree, a cell-phone tower, the width of a river. Indirect measurement means finding a length without measuring it directly. You measure lengths you can reach, find two similar triangles (Lesson 5), and solve a proportion.

On a sunny day, everything casts a shadow. The sun is so far away that its rays are parallel: at any moment, they come down at the same angle everywhere nearby.

An object standing straight up, its shadow and the sun ray make a right triangle. Two such triangles have

  • a right angle at the bottom of the object (it stands straight up on level ground), and
  • the same angle at the tip of the shadow (the sun’s angle is the same for both).

Two pairs of equal angles, so the triangles are similar (AA). In general: two right triangles that share the same acute angle are always similar. So the heights and the shadows are in proportion.

The shadow method

Key idea   Measure the two shadows at the same time, then
\dfrac{\text{height of object}}{\text{shadow of object}}=\dfrac{\text{height of stick}}{\text{shadow of stick}}
  1. Step 1. Sketch and label both triangles.
  2. Step 2. Write the word proportion (heights on top, shadows on the bottom).
  3. Step 3. Put in the numbers (same units) and cross-multiply.
  4. Step 4. Check that it makes sense; write a sentence.
!
Watch out. Shadows change all day: long in the morning, short around noon, long again in the evening. Both shadows must be measured within a few minutes of each other. The ground must be level and the objects must stand straight up.
Think Is there ever a day in Calgary when you have no shadow at noon?
No. The sun is only ever straight overhead in places close to the equator (between the tropics, 23.4° north and south). Calgary is about 51° north, so even at noon on the longest day of the year your shadow is about half as long as you are tall.
Example 1

The school flagpole

At 3:00 p.m., an upright metre stick casts a shadow 1.60 m long. At the same time, the school’s flagpole casts a shadow 12.8 m long. How tall is the flagpole?

HeightShadow
Metre stick1.00 m1.60 m
Flagpoleh12.8 m
Find the height of the flagpole.
\dfrac{\text{height}}{\text{shadow}}:\ \dfrac{h}{12.8}=\dfrac{1.00}{1.60}
1.60\times h=12.8\times1.00
h=12.8\div1.60=8
Check with the scale factor: the flagpole’s shadow is 12.8\div1.60=8 times as long, so the flagpole is 8 times as tall: 8\times1.00=8.00 m ✓
The flagpole is 8.0 m tall.

A shortcut: the scale factor

If one shadow is k times as long as the other, the object is k times as tall. This is a quick estimate, and a check on the proportion.

Example 2

The cell-phone tower

Jasmine is 1.62 m tall. Late one afternoon near Airdrie, her shadow is 2.25 m long. At the same time, a cell-phone tower casts a shadow 63.0 m long.

(a) Estimate the height of the tower.
The tower’s shadow is about 60\div2=30 times as long as Jasmine’s. So the tower is about 30\times1.6\approx48 m tall.
(b) Find the height of the tower to the nearest tenth of a metre. Compare with your estimate.
\dfrac{\text{height}}{\text{shadow}}:\ \dfrac{h}{63.0}=\dfrac{1.62}{2.25}
2.25\times h=63.0\times1.62
2.25h=102.06
h=102.06\div2.25=45.36
The tower is about 45.4 m tall. This is close to the estimate of 48 m ✓
(c) A light standard in the same parking lot is 9.0 m tall. How long is its shadow at that moment?
Now the shadow is missing: \dfrac{9.0}{s}=\dfrac{1.62}{2.25}
9.0\times2.25=1.62\times s
20.25=1.62s
s=20.25\div1.62=12.5
The light standard’s shadow is 12.5 m long. (Jasmine’s shadow is longer than she is, so this shadow should be longer than 9.0 m ✓)
You can do the multiply and the divide in one line. For part (b): 63 × 1.62 ÷ 2.25 ENTER gives 45.36. Round only at the end.
Example 3

The grain elevator

On a prairie farm, a fence post sticks up 6 ft out of the level ground. Its shadow is 4 ft 6 in long. At the same time, an old wooden grain elevator casts a shadow 52 ft long.

(a) Sketch the two triangles. How tall is the grain elevator, to the nearest tenth of a foot?
Use the same units: 4 ft 6 in =4\tfrac{6}{12} ft =4.5 ft.
\dfrac{h}{52}=\dfrac{6}{4.5}
4.5\times h=52\times6=312
h=312\div4.5=69.33\ldots
The elevator is about 69.3 ft tall.
(b) About how tall is the elevator in metres? Use 1 ft = 0.3048 m and round to the nearest tenth.
69.33\ldots\times0.3048=21.13\ldots
The elevator is about 21.1 m tall.
(Use the full calculator value, not the rounded 69.3.)
Keep the full value: 52 × 6 ÷ 4.5 ENTER gives 69.333…, then × 0.3048 ENTER gives 21.13…
Math Historyabout 600 BCE

A stick, a shadow, and the Great Pyramid

Thales of Miletus was one of the first Greek mathematicians. Ancient writers say that on a visit to Egypt he found the height of a pyramid from its shadow. One version: he waited for the moment when a person’s shadow is exactly as long as the person is tall. At that moment, the pyramid’s shadow is as long as the pyramid is tall.

The writer Plutarch tells it with a stick stood up at the tip of the pyramid’s shadow: the sun’s rays make two triangles, so pyramid ÷ stick = pyramid’s shadow ÷ stick’s shadow. That is our shadow method, about 2600 years ago. The tricky part: the pyramid’s shadow really starts under its top, in the middle of the pyramid.

Thales’s stick is 2 m tall and casts a 3 m shadow. The pyramid’s shadow sticks out 105 m past its base, and half the base is 115 m. How tall is the pyramid?
The shadow from under the top: 115+105=220 m. \dfrac{h}{220}=\dfrac{2}{3}, so h=220\times2\div3\approx146.7 m. The Great Pyramid was about 147 m tall when it was built.

The Mirror Method

On a cloudy day there are no shadows. You can use a small mirror instead. Lay the mirror flat on level ground between you and the object. Walk backwards (in a straight line away from the object) until you see the top of the object in the centre of the mirror. Then measure along the ground.

Light bounces off a mirror at the same angle it hits it: angle in = angle out. You and the object both stand straight up, so both triangles have a right angle. Two pairs of equal angles: the triangles are similar.

Key idea   The mirror method
\dfrac{\text{height of object}}{\text{object to mirror}}=\dfrac{\text{your eye height}}{\text{you to mirror}}
Measure to the centre of the mirror. Use the height of your eyes, not your full height: the light goes to your eyes, not the top of your head.
Think Why do you measure to the centre of the mirror, not to its edge?
The light bounces at one point, where you see the top of the object: the centre. Both triangles have their corner at that point. Measuring to an edge makes one distance too long and the other too short.

Why the mirror triangles are similar: flip the flagpole

Example 4

Diego’s mirror

Diego wants the height of the flagpole at his school. He puts a mirror on the ground 18.4 m from the base of the pole. He backs up until he sees the top of the pole in the mirror. His eyes are 1.50 m above the ground and he is 2.40 m from the mirror.

(a) How tall is the flagpole?
\dfrac{\text{height}}{\text{to mirror}}:\ \dfrac{h}{18.4}=\dfrac{1.50}{2.40}
2.40\times h=18.4\times1.50=27.6
h=27.6\div2.40=11.5
The flagpole is 11.5 m tall.
(b) Sara’s eyes are 1.70 m above the ground. Diego leaves the mirror in the same spot. How far from the mirror must Sara stand to see the top of the pole? Round to the nearest hundredth of a metre.
\dfrac{11.5}{18.4}=\dfrac{1.70}{d}
11.5\times d=18.4\times1.70=31.28
d=31.28\div11.5=2.72
Sara must stand 2.72 m from the mirror. (Her eyes are higher than Diego’s, so she stands farther back ✓)
Part (a) in one line: 18.4 × 1.5 ÷ 2.4 ENTER gives 11.5. Part (b): 18.4 × 1.7 ÷ 11.5 ENTER gives 2.72.
Extra example

One shadow, one mirror

(a) Downtown, a 2.0 m sign post casts a 2.5 m shadow. At the same time, a hotel’s shadow is 45 m long. How tall is the hotel?
\dfrac{h}{45}=\dfrac{2.0}{2.5}:  2.5h=90,  h=36. The hotel is 36 m tall (about 12 storeys of 3 m ✓).
(b) A mirror lies 10.5 m from a wall that is 7.0 m tall. Kim’s eyes are 1.60 m above the ground. How far from the mirror must she stand to see the top of the wall?
\dfrac{7.0}{10.5}=\dfrac{1.60}{d}:  7.0d=16.8,  d=2.4. Kim stands 2.4 m from the mirror.

Sighting Methods: a Ruler, or Stakes

A ruler at arm’s length. Hold a ruler straight up at arm’s length. Close one eye. Slide the ruler so its 0 lines up with the bottom of the object, and read the ruler where the top of the object lines up. The two sight lines from your eye make two triangles, one inside the other. The ruler and the object are both upright, so they are parallel, and the triangles are similar (like the nested triangles of Lesson 5).

Key idea   The ruler method   \dfrac{\text{height of object}}{\text{distance to object}}=\dfrac{\text{ruler reading}}{\text{eye to ruler}}
The ruler reading and the eye-to-ruler distance must be in the same unit (both cm). Then their ratio has no unit, and the height comes out in the unit of the distance.
Think Paige walks closer to the wind turbine. Will the tower cover more of the ruler, or less?
More. Closer things look bigger. At 200 m away the 120 m tower is 0.6 of the distance, so it covers 0.6\times60=36 cm of the ruler.
Example 5

The wind turbine

Paige is 400 m from a wind turbine in southern Alberta (she found the distance on a map app). She holds a ruler upright 60 cm from her eye. The tower, from the ground to the hub in the centre of the blades, covers 18 cm of the ruler. How tall is the tower?
\dfrac{h}{400}=\dfrac{18}{60}   (18\div60=0.3: the tower is 0.3 of the distance)
60\times h=400\times18=7\,200
h=7\,200\div60=120
The tower is about 120 m tall.

Stakes across a river

You can’t stretch a tape across a river, but you can make a small copy of the triangle on your side. Pick a tree T on the far bank. Put stake A straight across from it. Walk along the bank to stake B, then on to C. At C, turn 90^\circ and walk away from the river until B lines up with the tree: that is point D. The triangles TAB and DCB each have a right angle, and the angles at B are vertically opposite (Unit 5), so they are equal. The triangles are similar.

Example 6

The Bow River

Kenji uses the stake method to find the width w of the Bow River at a fishing spot. He measures AB=30.0 m, BC=10.0 m and CD=7.6 m.

(a) Name the matching sides: TA matches DC, and AB matches CB.
TA matches DC (both are across from the angle at B), and AB matches CB (both run along the bank to B).
(b) How wide is the river?
\triangle TAB\sim\triangle DCB, so \dfrac{TA}{AB}=\dfrac{DC}{CB}:\ \dfrac{w}{30.0}=\dfrac{7.6}{10.0}
10.0\times w=30.0\times7.6=228
w=228\div10.0=22.8
The river is about 22.8 m wide. (Check: AB is 3 times BC, so w is 3 times CD: 3\times7.6=22.8 ✓)

Is the Answer Reasonable?

Indirect measurement is easy to get wrong, and nobody can climb up to check. So always check your answer.

Key idea   Checks for indirect measurement
  • Compare inside one triangle. If the stick’s shadow is longer than the stick, then every shadow is longer than its object at that moment. A tree with a 21 m shadow must then be shorter than 21 m.
  • Estimate with the scale factor. If one shadow (or distance) is about 30 times the other, the heights differ by about 30 times.
  • Compare with something you know. One storey of a building is about 3 m. A school flagpole is often 8 to 12 m. A tree that is 90 m tall is not reasonable in Alberta.

Your answer can also be a little off because of the measurements: shadows measured at different times, ground that is not level, an object that leans, a mirror that is not flat, or using your full height instead of your eye height. Small mistakes in short lengths (the stick, your eye height) grow when you multiply up.

Example 7

Find the mistake

Each student made a mistake. Explain the mistake, and give the correct answer if you can.
(a) Ava: a 2.0 m post has a 3.0 m shadow. A tree’s shadow is 21 m. She writes \dfrac{h}{21}=\dfrac{3.0}{2.0}, so h=31.5 m.
She flipped the stick’s ratio: shadow over height on the right, but height over shadow on the left.
Correct: \dfrac{h}{21}=\dfrac{2.0}{3.0}, so 3.0h=42, h=14 m.

He used his full height. The light goes to his eyes, so use 1.68 m.
h=1.68\times14.7\div2.1=11.76
The tree is about 11.8 m tall.
(c) Mia measured her shadow at 9:00 a.m. and the school’s shadow at 2:00 p.m. She used them in the shadow method.
The sun’s angle changed between 9:00 a.m. and 2:00 p.m., so the triangles are not similar. Her answer is wrong and can’t be fixed with these numbers. She must measure both shadows again at the same time.

Shadows: measure both at the same time. Height ÷ shadow is the same for both objects. A right angle and the same sun angle: similar (AA).
