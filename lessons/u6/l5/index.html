Warm-up

One Large or Two Mediums?

A pizza place sells round pizzas by their size across (the diameter).

You and your friends can get one 16-inch pizza or two 10-inch pizzas. Same toppings, same thickness.

Which choice gives you more pizza to eat?

A two 10-inch pizzas
B one 16-inch pizza
C about the same
B: the one 16-inch pizza, by a lot! Most people pick two pizzas because 10+10=20 is more than 16. But you eat the area, not the width. The 16-inch pizza is about 201 square inches. The two 10-inch pizzas together are about 79+79=157 square inches. The big one has about 44 square inches more: more than half of another 10-inch pizza! When a shape gets wider, its area grows much faster.

Similar Triangles

Two pairs of equal angles are enough to show that two triangles are similar. Then a scale factor or a proportion finds any missing side: on a ramp, under a sign, or across a pond.

I can…
  • find the third angle of a triangle and use the AA test to decide if two triangles are similar
  • name similar triangles in matching order and list the matching angles and sides
  • find a missing side with the scale factor or a proportion, and check that it makes sense
  • spot similar right triangles that share an acute angle, like a ramp and its support post
  • spot nested and crossing triangles made by parallel lines, and find missing sides in them
G3 Demonstrate an understanding of similarity of convex polygons, including regular and irregular polygons.
1

The AA Test for Similar Triangles

Remember: two shapes are similar if they have the same shape. One is an enlargement or a reduction of the other. The parts that match are called corresponding parts: corresponding angles are equal, and corresponding sides are in the same ratio.

For most polygons you must check the angles and the sides. Triangles are easier. The three angles of any triangle add to 180^\circ. So if two angles of one triangle match two angles of another triangle, the third angles must match too. And when all the angles match, the sides are always in the same ratio.

Key idea   AA test (angle–angle): if two angles of one triangle are equal to two angles of another triangle, the triangles are similar.
To find the third angle of a triangle:   third angle =180^\circ-(\text{first angle}+\text{second angle})
!
Watch out. The equal angles do not have to be in the same place on the page. A triangle can be turned or flipped over and still be similar. Match the angles by their size, not by where they are.

Why two angles are enough

Fix two angles on a base. Now make the base longer or shorter. The third angle never changes, and every side grows by the same amount: the triangle keeps its shape.

Think One triangle has angles of 30^\circ and 60^\circ. Another has angles of 60^\circ and 90^\circ. Are they similar?
Yes. The first one's third angle is 180^\circ-(30^\circ+60^\circ)=90^\circ. The second one's is 180^\circ-(60^\circ+90^\circ)=30^\circ. Both are 30^\circ,\ 60^\circ,\ 90^\circ.
Example 1

Find the missing angles. Similar or not?

Find the missing angles in each triangle. Then decide: are the two triangles similar? (In part (c), both triangles are isosceles: two equal sides, so the two angles at the base are equal.)

(a)
Left: 180^\circ-(47^\circ+68^\circ)=65^\circ
Right: 180^\circ-(65^\circ+68^\circ)=47^\circ
Both have 47^\circ,\ 68^\circ,\ 65^\circ, so yes, they are similar.
(b)
Left: 180^\circ-(90^\circ+34^\circ)=56^\circ
Right: 180^\circ-(90^\circ+58^\circ)=32^\circ
90^\circ,\ 34^\circ,\ 56^\circ and 90^\circ,\ 58^\circ,\ 32^\circ do not match, so no.
(c)
Left: base angles (180^\circ-36^\circ)\div2=72^\circ each.
Right: the other base angle is 36^\circ, top =180^\circ-72^\circ=108^\circ.
36^\circ,\ 72^\circ,\ 72^\circ and 36^\circ,\ 36^\circ,\ 108^\circ: no.

Part (c) shows that two isosceles triangles are not always similar, even when both have a 36^\circ angle.

Naming Similar Triangles in Order

We write \triangle ABC\sim\triangle DEF and say “triangle ABC is similar to triangle DEF.” The order of the letters tells you which parts match: the 1st letters match, the 2nd letters match and the 3rd letters match.

Key idea   If \triangle ABC\sim\triangle DEF, then
equal angles:   \angle A=\angle D,\quad \angle B=\angle E,\quad \angle C=\angle F
equal ratios:   \dfrac{DE}{AB}=\dfrac{EF}{BC}=\dfrac{DF}{AC}=k, the scale factor

Matching angles are often marked with the same number of arcs. A side and the angle opposite it (across the triangle) go together: if \angle A matches \angle D, then the side across from A (that is BC) matches the side across from D (that is EF).

Think If \triangle ABC\sim\triangle DEF, is it also true that \triangle BAC\sim\triangle EDF?
Yes. Both names were changed the same way (1st and 2nd letters swapped), so B still goes with E, A with D and C with F. What matters is that matching letters are in the same place in both names.
Example 2

Match the letters

The two triangles are similar. Matching angles have the same number of arcs.

(a) Complete:   \triangle ABC\sim\triangle RPQ
(b) \angle A=\angle R    \angle B=\angle P    \angle C=\angle Q
(c) Complete the equal ratios:   \dfrac{RP}{AB}= PQBC = RQAC
(d) Side AB is across from \angle C. The matching side is across from \angle Q, so it is side RP.
One arc: A and R. Two arcs: B and P. Three arcs: C and Q. So A\to R,\ B\to P,\ C\to Q: \triangle ABC\sim\triangle RPQ. (The order RPQ looks strange, but it is the order that matches.)
3

Finding a Missing Side

Once you know two triangles are similar, you can find a missing side with the scale factor or with a proportion (Unit 1).

Key idea
  • Step 1: Match the vertices (write \triangle\,\_\_\_\sim\triangle\,\_\_\_).
  • Step 2: Find a pair of matching sides where you know both lengths.
  • Step 3: Use the scale factor k=\dfrac{\text{new side}}{\text{matching original side}} (multiply by k to go to the new triangle, divide to come back), or write a proportion with the missing side on top and solve.
  • Step 4: Check: the bigger triangle must have the bigger sides.
Calculator. Do not round k. For a side of 9 cm and k=\frac{10}{6}, key in 9 × 10 ÷ 6 ENTER. It shows 15. Round only the final answer.

Every side times the same k

Grow \triangle ABC into \triangle DEF (Example 3). Every side is multiplied by the same scale factor, so every ratio new ÷ original is the same.

Think k=\frac{10}{6} rounds to 1.67. What do you get for EF if you use 1.67? Why is that not quite right?
9\times1.67=15.03 cm, but the true answer is 15 cm. Rounding k too early adds a small error. Key in 9\times10\div6 instead.
Example 3

Use the scale factor

\triangle ABC\sim\triangle DEF. Find the lengths of EF and DF.
Matching sides with both lengths known: AB=6 cm and DE=10 cm.
Scale factor: k=\dfrac{DE}{AB}=\dfrac{10}{6}\approx1.67 (an enlargement).
EF=BC\times k=9\times10\div6=15 cm
DF=AC\times k=7.5\times10\div6=12.5 cm
Check: \triangle DEF is bigger, and 15 and 12.5 are bigger than 9 and 7.5 \checkmark
Example 4

The second triangle is turned

\triangle LMN\sim\triangle STU. The second triangle is turned. Find each length to the nearest tenth of a centimetre.

(a) Find x (side TU).
TU matches MN. ST matches LM.
\dfrac{x}{4.8}=\dfrac{11.0}{7.2}
x=4.8\times11.0\div7.2=7.333\ldots\approx7.3 cm
Check: \triangle STU is bigger, 7.3>4.8\ \checkmark
(b) Find side LN.
LN matches SU. Put LN on top:
\dfrac{LN}{9.5}=\dfrac{7.2}{11.0}
LN=9.5\times7.2\div11.0=6.218\ldots\approx6.2 cm
Check: \triangle LMN is smaller, 6.2<9.5\ \checkmark
Estimate first: in (a), 11.0 is about 1\tfrac12 times 7.2, so x is about 1\tfrac12\times4.8\approx7. Then 4.8 × 11 ÷ 7.2 ENTER shows 7.333… In (b): 9.5 × 7.2 ÷ 11 ENTER shows 6.218…
More examples

Two Garden Beds

Two triangular garden beds are similar: \triangle PQR\sim\triangle XYZ. PQ=4 m, QR=5 m, PR=6 m and XY=6 m. Find YZ and XZ.
XY matches PQ: k=6\div4=1.5.
YZ=5\times1.5=7.5 m,   XZ=6\times1.5=9 m. Check: \triangle XYZ is bigger \checkmark

Keep the triangles in the same places

!
Watch out. Keep the triangles in the same places in a proportion: the same triangle on top on both sides. \frac{x}{4.8}=\frac{11.0}{7.2} is right (big over small on both sides). \frac{x}{4.8}=\frac{7.2}{11.0} mixes them up and gives 3.1 cm, which is too small for the bigger triangle.

Right Triangles That Share an Acute Angle

Every right triangle has a 90^\circ angle. If two right triangles also have one acute angle (less than 90^\circ) that is the same, then two angles match, so the triangles are similar by the AA test.

This happens a lot in real life. A ramp, a roof or a slide makes a long, thin right triangle with the ground. A vertical post under it cuts off a smaller right triangle. The two triangles share the acute angle where the slope meets the ground.

Key idea   Two right triangles that share an acute angle (or have equal acute angles) are similar: the right angles match, the shared angles match, so by AA the triangles are similar.
Think A ramp rises 1 m for every 12 m along the ground. How high is it 3 m from the start? 6 m?
Height =\text{ground}\div12: 3\div12=0.25 m and 6\div12=0.5 m. Twice as far, twice as high: every post makes a similar triangle.
Example 5

A wheelchair ramp

A wheelchair ramp starts at point A on level ground and rises steadily to a door. A vertical support post DE is 2.16 m from A along the ground, and it is 0.18 m tall. (The diagram is not to scale.)

(a) Complete: \triangle ADE and \triangle ABC are similar because both have a right angle, and they share \angle A.
Both have a right angle (at D and at B), and they share \angle A. Two pairs of equal angles: similar (AA).
(b) The door is 0.46 m above the ground. How far from A, along the ground, must the ramp end (AB)? Round to the nearest hundredth of a metre.
AB matches AD, and BC matches DE.
\dfrac{AB}{2.16}=\dfrac{0.46}{0.18}
AB=2.16\times0.46\div0.18=5.52 m
The ramp must end 5.52 m from A.
(c) A second post will stand 4.00 m from A. How tall must it be, to the nearest hundredth of a metre?
The new post makes a third similar right triangle.
\dfrac{h}{0.18}=\dfrac{4.00}{2.16}
h=0.18\times4.00\div2.16=0.333\ldots
The post must be about 0.33 m (33 cm) tall.
(b) 2.16 × 0.46 ÷ 0.18 ENTER shows 5.52. (c) 0.18 × 4 ÷ 2.16 ENTER shows 0.333…
More examples

A shed roof

A shed roof rises steadily from the low edge. 1.2 m in from the low edge (measured level), the roof is 0.5 m above the low edge. How high above the low edge is the roof 3.0 m in?
Both right triangles share the angle at the low edge, so they are similar (AA).
\dfrac{h}{0.5}=\dfrac{3.0}{1.2}   so   h=0.5\times3.0\div1.2=1.25 m. Check: 3.0 m is farther in than 1.2 m, so the roof is higher \checkmark
5

Nested Triangles and Crossing Triangles

Nested triangles. Draw a line inside a triangle parallel to one side. It cuts off a small triangle at the top. The small and big triangles share the top angle, and the parallel line makes corresponding angles that are equal (Unit 5). So the two triangles are similar by AA.

Crossing triangles. Two line segments cross at a point and their ends are joined by two parallel lines. This makes two triangles point to point, like a bow tie. The angles where the lines cross are vertically opposite (equal), and the parallel lines make alternate interior angles (equal). So these triangles are similar by AA too.

!
Watch out. In nested triangles, the big triangle's side is the whole side, not just the bottom piece. If AD=5 and DB=3, the big side AB=5+3=8. It helps to draw the two triangles apart and label each one.

Why the angles are equal: F, Z and X

The parallel-line angle pairs from Unit 5 give the equal angles. Look for the letter shapes.

Example 6

Nested triangles

In \triangle ABC, DE\parallel BC.   AD=5 cm, DB=3 cm, DE=6 cm and AE=4.5 cm.

(a) Explain why \triangle ADE\sim\triangle ABC.
Both triangles have \angle A (shared). \angle ADE=\angle ABC because they are corresponding angles (DE\parallel BC, with AB as the transversal). Two pairs of equal angles, so they are similar (AA).
(b) Label the two triangles, drawn apart. Put the side lengths you know on them.
Small \triangle ADE: 5, 6, 4.5. Big \triangle ABC: AB=5+3=8, BC and AC unknown.
(c) Find the length of BC.
AB=5+3=8 cm. AB matches AD.
k=\dfrac{AB}{AD}=\dfrac{8}{5}=1.6
BC=DE\times k=6\times1.6=9.6 cm
(d) Find the length of EC.
First find the whole side AC: AC=AE\times k=4.5\times1.6=7.2 cm.
Then EC=AC-AE=7.2-4.5=2.7 cm.
Think In Example 6, compare DB\div AD with EC\div AE. What do you notice?
3\div5=0.6 and 2.7\div4.5=0.6. They are equal! A line parallel to one side cuts the other two sides in the same ratio.
More examples

Going the Other Way

In \triangle ABC, DE\parallel BC (D on AB, E on AC). AD=4 cm, the whole side AB=10 cm and BC=15 cm. Find DE.
\triangle ADE\sim\triangle ABC. Put DE on top: \dfrac{DE}{15}=\dfrac{4}{10}, so DE=15\times4\div10=6 cm. Check: the small triangle has the smaller side, 6<15\ \checkmark

Crossing triangles: turn the bow tie

Give the small triangle of a bow tie a half turn about the crossing point. It lands inside the big triangle, nested, with its side parallel. That is why crossing triangles are similar.

Think In a bow tie, why do we write \triangle ABC\sim\triangle DEC and not \triangle ABC\sim\triangle EDC?
The equal Z angles are at A and D, so A goes with D (and B with E). After the half turn, D lands on the line through A, not on the line through B.
Example 7

How wide is the pond?

Nadia wants to know the width AB of a pond on her farm. She puts a stake at C, so that she can walk in a straight line from A through C to D, and from B through C to E. She places D and E so that DE is parallel to AB. Then she measures: AC=38 m, CD=12 m and DE=15.5 m.

(a) Give the reasons why \triangle ABC\sim\triangle DEC.
\angle ACB=\angle DCE (vertically opposite angles).
\angle CAB=\angle CDE (alternate interior angles, because AB\parallel DE).
Two pairs of equal angles, so the triangles are similar (AA).
(b) Find the width of the pond, AB, to the nearest tenth of a metre.
Match in order: A\to D,\ B\to E,\ C\to C. So AB matches DE, and AC matches DC.
\dfrac{AB}{15.5}=\dfrac{38}{12}\ \Rightarrow\ AB=15.5\times38\div12=49.083\ldots
The pond is about 49.1 m wide. Check: AC is about 3 times CD, and 49.1 is about 3 times 15.5 \checkmark
15.5 × 38 ÷ 12 ENTER shows 49.083…, so about 49.1 m.
More examples

Across a River

To measure across a river from A to B, a surveyor sets up a bow tie: A, C, D in a line, B, C, E in a line and DE\parallel AB. AC=50 m, CD=10 m and DE=8 m. How wide is the river?
\triangle ABC\sim\triangle DEC: \dfrac{AB}{8}=\dfrac{50}{10}, so AB=8\times50\div10=40 m. Check: AC is 5 times CD, and 40 is 5 times 8\ \checkmark
✓

Key ideas

AA test: two pairs of equal angles \Rightarrow similar triangles. Third angle =180^\circ-(\text{the other two}). Match angles by size, not by where they sit.
Name in order: \triangle ABC\sim\triangle DEF means A\to D,\ B\to E,\ C\to F, and \frac{DE}{AB}=\frac{EF}{BC}=\frac{DF}{AC}=k.
Missing side: match the vertices, find a pair you know both of, then \times k or a proportion with the same triangle on top. Check: bigger triangle, bigger sides.
Look for: right triangles that share an acute angle (ramps), nested triangles (use the whole side!) and crossing bow ties with parallel ends.
