The Ant on the Box

An ant sits on a bottom corner of a closed box shaped like a cube. Every edge of the box is 10 cm long.

A grain of sugar sits on the opposite corner: at the top, on the far side. The ant can only walk on the outside of the box.

Which path to the sugar is the shortest?

A along three edges: across, back, then up
B slanting across the front face to its top corner, then along the top edge
C one slanting path across the front and the top, crossing the edge between them at its middle
D they are all the same length
C! Unfold the front and the top of the box so they lie flat. They make one rectangle, 10 cm by 20 cm. On flat paper the shortest path is a straight line, and it crosses the fold right at the middle of the edge. Path A is 30 cm, path B is about 24.1 cm, and path C is only about 22.4 cm. Laying a bumpy world flat so you can measure it with a ruler is exactly what a map does.

Scale Diagrams: Maps and Floor Plans

A road map of Alberta, the floor plan of an apartment, a blueprint for a garage, a model car: all of them are scale diagrams. Today we read the scale three ways, change lengths on a drawing into real lengths (and back), choose a scale so a drawing fits the paper, draw a floor plan, and measure a route on a map.

I can…
  • write a scale in words, as a ratio and as a bar scale, and tell a reduction from an enlargement
  • use the scale to change a diagram length into a real length (multiply, or use a proportion)
  • use the scale to find the length to draw from a real length
  • choose an easy scale so a drawing fits, and draw a floor plan
  • measure a route on a map piece by piece, and compare it with the straight-line distance
G3 Demonstrate an understanding of similarity of convex polygons, including regular and irregular polygons.
Today: a scale diagram (a map, a floor plan, a model) is similar to the real object, so every length changes by the same scale.
1

What Is a Scale Diagram?

A scale diagram is an accurate drawing of a real object in which every length is changed by the same amount. Maps, floor plans, blueprints and model cars are smaller than the real thing: they are reductions. A drawing of an insect or a tiny machine part can be bigger than the real thing: an enlargement. A scale diagram is similar to the real object: it has the same shape and the same angles.

The scale tells you how a length on the diagram compares with the real length. A scale can be written three ways. All three below say the same thing.

In wordsAs a ratioAs a bar scale
1 cm represents 2 m1:200
Key idea   Scale = diagram length : real length   (the diagram comes first)
A ratio scale has no units, because both numbers are in the same unit. 1:200 means 1 cm on the plan is 200 cm in real life (or 1 mm is 200 mm, or 1 in is 200 in).
Units:   1 cm = 10 mm  ·  1 m = 100 cm  ·  1 km = 1000 m = 100 000 cm  ·  1 ft = 12 in
Words to ratio: change the real length to the same unit as the diagram length, then write diagram : real.
Ratio to words: 1:n means 1 cm represents n cm. Change n cm to metres or kilometres so it is easy to picture.
!
Watch out. 1:200 and 200:1 are very different. 1:200 is a reduction (a plan: the real room is bigger). 200:1 is an enlargement (the drawing is bigger). In Lesson 6.1 you met the scale factor: a scale of 1:200 means k=\frac{1}{200}=0.005.
Think A model train box says 1:87. A poster of a flea says 30:1. Which picture is bigger than the real thing? How can you tell from the ratio alone?
The flea poster. The diagram number comes first: in 30:1 the drawing number is bigger, so it is an enlargement. In 1:87 the real number is bigger, so the model is a reduction.

Example 2

A bar scale on a park map

A park map has this bar scale. In your booklet, measure it with a ruler. Here, the ruler is drawn under it.

(a) How long is one block of the bar? Write the scale in words.
One block is 1 cm long and stands for 50 m. The scale is 1 cm represents 50 m.
(b) Write the scale as a ratio.
50\text{ m}=50\times100=5000 cm. The scale is 1:5000.
(c) Ana zooms in on the map on her phone, so everything is twice as long, including the bar. Is the bar scale still correct? Would a printed ratio “1 : 5000” still be correct? Explain.
The bar grows with the map, so it still matches: the bar scale is still correct. A printed “1 : 5000” does not change, but now 2 cm on the screen stands for 50 m, so the real scale is 1:2500. The printed ratio is wrong. This is why phone maps use a bar scale.

Finding Real Lengths from a Diagram

You can find a real length two ways. Both use what you learned about proportions in Unit 1.

Key idea
Method 1: proportion.   \dfrac{\text{diagram}}{\text{real}}=\dfrac{\text{diagram}}{\text{real}}   Put the scale on one side and your length on the other. Units in the same position must match.
Method 2: multiply.   real length = diagram length × (real length for 1 unit of the diagram)
If 1 cm represents 1.5 m, then 4 cm represents 4\times1.5=6 m.  With a ratio 1:n, multiply by n, then change the units.
i
Map shortcut. To change centimetres to kilometres, divide by 100 000 (move the decimal point 5 places left). So 1:2\,000\,000 means 1 cm represents 20 km, and 1:50\,000 means 1 cm represents 0.5 km.

Proportion or multiply: the same answer

Think For the living room, Sam writes \dfrac{1}{1.5}=\dfrac{x}{4.2} and gets x=2.8. What went wrong?
He put the plan length 4.2 cm on the bottom, where the real length goes. Keep plan on top and real on the bottom on both sides: \dfrac{1}{1.5}=\dfrac{4.2}{x} gives x=6.3 m. Also, 2.8 m can’t be right: the real room must be longer than 4.2 m, because each plan cm is 1.5 m.

Example 4

Maps, models and an ant

Find each real length. Say whether you multiplied or divided, and why.
(a) An Alberta road map has a scale of 1:2\,000\,000. Lethbridge and Medicine Hat are 7.9 cm apart on the map. How far apart are they really (in a straight line)?
1 cm represents 2 000 000 cm = 20 km (shortcut).
7.9\times20=158
Multiply: the real distance is bigger. They are about 158 km apart.
(b) A model car is built at a scale of 1:64. The model is 7.5 cm long. How long is the real car, in metres?
1 cm on the model is 64 cm on the car.
7.5\times64=480 cm
480\div100=4.8 m. The real car is 4.8 m long.
(c) A science book shows an ant at a scale of 8:1. The ant in the picture is 6 cm long. How long is the real ant, in millimetres?
\dfrac{\text{picture}}{\text{real}}:\ \dfrac{8}{1}=\dfrac{6}{x}
8x=6, so x=0.75 cm =0.75\times10=7.5 mm
Divide: in an enlargement the real ant is smaller. It is 7.5 mm long.
(a) 7.9 × 20 ENTER gives 158.  (b) 7.5 × 64 ENTER gives 480.  (c) 6 ÷ 8 ENTER gives 0.75.

Multiply or divide? Ask: is the real thing bigger or smaller?

Extra example

More real lengths

(a) A hiking map of Kananaskis has a scale of 1:50\,000. Two campsites are 6.4 cm apart on the map. How far apart are they really?
1 cm represents 0.5 km. 6.4\times0.5=3.2. They are 3.2 km apart.
(b) A dollhouse is built at 1:12. Its front door is 17 cm tall. How tall is the real door, in metres?
17\times12=204 cm =2.04 m. Multiply: the real door is bigger.
(c) A poster shows a flea at 20:1. The flea on the poster is 5 cm long. How long is the real flea, in mm?
5\div20=0.25 cm =2.5 mm. Divide: the real flea is smaller.
3

Finding Diagram Lengths from Real Lengths

Now go the other way: you know the real length and need the length to draw.

Key idea   Diagram length = real length ÷ (real length for 1 unit of the diagram), or use a proportion.
With a ratio 1:n: change the real length to the diagram’s unit (usually cm), then divide by n.
Check: for a reduction, the diagram length must be smaller than the real length.
Example 5

A garage blueprint

Carpenters’ blueprints often use the scale ¼ in represents 1 ft. Liam is drawing the blueprint for a garage that is 24 ft long and 22 ft wide, with a garage door 9 ft wide.

(a) How many feet does 1 in on the blueprint represent?
¼ in represents 1 ft. There are 4 quarters in 1 in. So 1 in represents 4\times1=4 ft.
(b) Find the length, width and door width on the blueprint.
Divide each real length by 4 (or multiply by ¼):
length 24\div4=6 in   width 22\div4=5\tfrac12 in   door 9\div4=2\tfrac14 in
On the blueprint: 6 in by 5½ in, door 2¼ in.
Example 6

At the hobby shop

A hobby shop sells models at different scales. Find each model length to the nearest tenth of a centimetre.

(a) A pickup truck is 5.9 m long. How long is a 1:64 model of it?
Same units: 5.9\text{ m}=590 cm
590\div64=9.218\ldots
The model is about 9.2 cm long.
(b) Model trains in “HO scale” are 1:87. A real grain boxcar is 15.2 m long. How long is the HO model?
15.2\text{ m}=1520 cm
1520\div87=17.47\ldots
The model boxcar is about 17.5 cm long.
(a) 590 ÷ 64 ENTER gives 9.21875.  (b) 1520 ÷ 87 ENTER gives 17.47126437. Then round to one decimal place.
Think Liam’s friend draws the 9 ft garage door 36 in wide on the blueprint. How can you tell right away it is wrong, and what did he do?
36 in is 3 ft: the drawing of the door would be bigger than the whole real door, but a blueprint is a reduction. He multiplied by 4 instead of dividing: 9\div4=2\tfrac14 in.
Extra example

More drawing lengths

(a) On a 1:50 plan, how long should a 6.5 m wall be drawn?
6.5\text{ m}=650 cm; 650\div50=13 cm
(b) On a 1:250\,000 map, two towns are 35 km apart. How far apart are they on the map?
1 cm represents 250 000 cm = 2.5 km. 35\div2.5=14 cm
(c) A 1:18 model is made of a car that is 4.5 m long. How long is the model?
4.5\text{ m}=450 cm; 450\div18=25 cm

Choosing a Scale and Drawing a Floor Plan

Before you draw, choose a scale that makes the drawing fit the paper but is not tiny. Pick an easy number so the dividing is simple.

Key idea   Choosing a scale
  1. Step 1. Find the longest real length (and the longest real width).
  2. Step 2. Measure the space you have on the paper.
  3. Step 3. Divide: real length ÷ space. Do it for the length and the width, and use the bigger answer.
  4. Step 4. Round up to an easy number of real units for each 1 cm (such as 0.5, 1, 2, 2.5, 5 or 10). Rounding down would make the drawing too big.
  5. Step 5. Divide every real length by the scale, draw with a ruler, and write the scale on the drawing.
Think Why do we round the scale up, and not to the nearest easy number?
Rounding down makes each centimetre stand for less, so you need more centimetres: the drawing gets bigger than the space. That is what happens with 1 cm represents 4 m in Example 7.
Example 7

A hockey rink on a handout

Coach Diaz wants to draw a hockey rink, 61 m long and 26 m wide, in a space on a whiteboard handout that is 15 cm long and 10 cm wide.

(a) Choose an easy scale so the rink fits.
Length: 61\div15=4.07 m per cm
Width: 26\div10=2.6 m per cm
Use the bigger, 4.07, and round up: 1 cm represents 5 m (1:500).
(b) Find the length and width of the rink on the drawing. Why would “1 cm represents 4 m” not work?
61\div5=12.2 cm and 26\div5=5.2 cm. Both fit.
With 4 m: 61\div4=15.25 cm, longer than the 15 cm space, so it would not fit.
Extra example

Choosing a scale

(a) A soccer field is 100 m long and 64 m wide. Choose an easy scale to draw it in a space 22 cm by 14 cm, and find its size on the drawing.
100\div22=4.55 and 64\div14=4.57 m per cm. Round up: 1 cm represents 5 m. Drawing: 100\div5=20 cm by 64\div5=12.8 cm.
(b) A classroom is 9.6 m by 7.2 m. Choose an easy scale for a space 20 cm by 15 cm, and find its size on the drawing.
9.6\div20=0.48 and 7.2\div15=0.48 m per cm. Round up: 1 cm represents 0.5 m. Drawing: 19.2 cm by 14.4 cm.

Maps: Distances Along a Route

The straight-line distance between two places is measured with a ruler from one point to the other. People usually travel along roads or trails, so the route distance is longer. For a route, measure each straight piece, add the pieces, then change the total to a real distance once. For a curved trail, lay a piece of string along it, then measure the string.

i
Measuring. The maps and plans in your booklet are drawn to scale. Measure them on the printed page with a ruler, to the nearest millimetre (0.1 cm). Print the booklet at 100 % (actual size, not “fit to page”) so your measurements match the answers. On this screen, the map below has a measuring tape: drag its two round ends.
Think Can a route along roads ever be shorter than the straight-line distance?
No. A straight line is the shortest path between two points. A route can only be equal to it, when the road itself is straight from start to finish.
Example 9

Jada’s walk to school

This map of a small town is drawn to scale. Streets run north–south and avenues run east–west. Drag the ends of the yellow tape to measure (they snap to the places and the corners).

(a) Write the scale in words and as a ratio.
One block of the bar is 1 cm and represents 200 m. 1 cm represents 200 m. 200\text{ m}=20\,000 cm, so 1:20\,000.
(b) Jada walks from home to school along the streets: east on 1 Ave, then north on 3 St. Find her real walking distance.
Measure: 4 cm east + 3 cm north = 7 cm
7\times200=1400 m = 1.4 km. Jada walks 1.4 km.
(c) Find the straight-line distance from home to school.
Measure the straight line: 5 cm. 5\times200=1000 m = 1 km. The straight-line distance is 1 km, shorter than the route.
(d) After school she walks to the rink: south on 3 St, then east on 2 Ave. How far is that?
1.5\text{ cm}+2\text{ cm}=3.5 cm
3.5\times200=700 m. It is 700 m from school to the rink.
(e) Jada walks at about 5 km/h. About how many minutes does her walk from home to school take?
time = distance ÷ speed = 1.4\div5=0.28 h
0.28\times60=16.8 min. Her walk takes about 17 minutes.
(e) In one go: 1.4 ÷ 5 × 60 ENTER gives 16.8 minutes.
Extra example

A drive to the lake

On a map with a scale of 1:500\,000, Sam’s route from Pine Ridge to Silver Lake goes north 3.2 cm, then east 4.6 cm, then north 1.8 cm.

(a) How long is the real route?
1 cm represents 5 km. 3.2+4.6+1.8=9.6 cm; 9.6\times5=48 km
(b) Sam drives at about 80 km/h. About how many minutes does the drive take?
48\div80=0.6 h; 0.6\times60=36 min
(c) A new highway is 5.5 cm long on the map. How many kilometres shorter is it than his route?
5.5\times5=27.5 km; 48-27.5=20.5 km shorter
✓

Key ideas

Scale = diagram : real. Three ways: in words (1 cm represents 2 m), as a ratio with the same units (1:200), as a bar scale. 1:200 is a reduction; 200:1 is an enlargement.
Real length: diagram length × (real length for 1 unit), or a proportion with plan on top on both sides. Then change units (÷ 100 for m, ÷ 100 000 for km). Area: use the real lengths.
Diagram length: real length (in the diagram’s unit) ÷ the scale. Check: a reduction must be smaller than the real thing.
Choosing a scale: real ÷ space for length and width, take the bigger, round up to an easy number. Routes: add the pieces, then change to real once; the straight line is shorter.
