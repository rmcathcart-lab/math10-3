The Magnifying Glass Riddle

Zoe draws a small triangle on paper. One corner of the triangle is 30°.

She looks at the corner through a magnifying glass that makes everything look 3 times as big.

How big does the 30° corner look through the glass?

A 10°
B 30°
C 33°
D 90°
B: still 30°! The glass makes the arms of the corner look 3 times as long, but it does not make the corner open any wider. The amount of turn between the arms stays the same. Try it with your phone camera: zoom in on the corner of a page. It gets bigger, but it stays a square corner. Today we see why: a bigger copy keeps the same shape.

Similar Polygons and Scale Factor

A phone photo and a big poster print of it. A model truck and the real truck. They have the same shape, but not the same size. Today we learn what makes two shapes similar, how to match their corners and sides, and how one number, the scale factor, enlarges, reduces and finds missing lengths.

I can…
  • tell when two polygons are similar: equal matching angles, and every side multiplied by the same number
  • use the naming order (like ABCD\sim PQRS) to match corresponding angles and sides, even when a figure is turned or flipped
  • find the scale factor k=\text{new}\div\text{original} and say if it is an enlargement, a reduction or the same size
  • use k to find a missing side: multiply for the new figure, divide for the original
G3 Demonstrate an understanding of similarity of convex polygons, including regular and irregular polygons.
Today: what similar means, corresponding parts, the scale factor, and finding missing sides.
1

Same Shape, Different Size

A polygon is a closed flat shape with straight sides: a triangle (3 sides), a quadrilateral (4 sides), a pentagon (5 sides), a hexagon (6 sides), and so on.

Two figures are similar if they have exactly the same shape. They can be different sizes. A photo and a bigger print of it are similar. A model car and the real car are similar. But if you stretch a photo in only one direction, everyone in it looks too wide or too thin. The shape has changed, so the stretched photo is not similar to the original.

Key idea   Two polygons are similar when both of these are true:
  1. 1. Corresponding angles are equal. The matching corners are the same size.
  2. 2. Corresponding sides are in the same ratio. Every side of one figure is the matching side of the other figure multiplied by the same number.
The symbol \sim means “is similar to”. Figures with the same shape and the same size are congruent. Congruent figures are similar too.
i
Remember. Turning or flipping a figure does not change its shape. Only stretching or squishing does.
Think A photo is 4 inches by 6 inches. A print of it is 8 inches by 10 inches. Is the print similar to the photo?
No. The short side was multiplied by 8\div4=2, but the long side by only 10\div6=1.67\ldots The sides were not all multiplied by the same number, so part of the photo gets cut off (or squished).
Example 1

Which copies are similar?

Each small square is 1 unit. Which copies are similar to the original?

(a) Which copies are similar to the original?
A, C and D have the same shape as the original. Copy C is flipped, but its shape did not change.
(b) For each similar copy, every length was multiplied by what number?
A: ×2 (2 wide → 4 wide, 3 tall → 6 tall)
C: ×1 (same size: congruent)
D: ×1.5 (2 → 3 and 3 → 4.5)
(c) Explain why copy B is not similar to the original.
The widths were doubled (2 → 4) but the heights stayed the same (3 → 3). The sides were not all multiplied by the same number, so B is stretched sideways. It is a different shape.

Corresponding Parts and Naming Order

Corresponding parts are parts in matching positions: the matching angles and the matching sides. When we write ABCD\sim PQRS, the order of the letters tells you what matches. The 1st letter goes with the 1st letter, the 2nd with the 2nd, and so on.

ABCD↕↕↕↕PQRS
Angles: \angle A and \angle P,  \angle B and \angle Q,  \angle C and \angle R,  \angle D and \angle S
Sides: AB and PQ,  BC and QR,  CD and RS,  DA and SP
Key idea   If ABCD\sim PQRS, then
\angle A=\angle P,\ \ \angle B=\angle Q,\ \ \angle C=\angle R,\ \ \angle D=\angle S and \dfrac{PQ}{AB}=\dfrac{QR}{BC}=\dfrac{RS}{CD}=\dfrac{SP}{DA}
!
Watch out. The second figure is often turned or flipped. Do not match parts by where they sit on the page. Write the two names one above the other and read down the columns.
i
Quick refresher. From Lesson 5.3: the angles in a triangle add up to 180°, in a quadrilateral 360°, and in a pentagon 540°.

Why the letters work: turn (or flip) the copy back

Slide, turn or flip the second figure and shrink it, and it lands right on top of the first one. The corners with matching letters land on each other.

Example 2

Matching parts of turned triangles

\triangle ABC\sim\triangle XYZ. The second triangle is turned around.

(a) Complete the table of corresponding parts.
In \triangle ABC\angle A\angle B\angle C
In \triangle XYZ\angle X\angle Y\angle Z
In \triangle ABCside ABside BCside CA
In \triangle XYZXYYZZX
1st with 1st, 2nd with 2nd, 3rd with 3rd: \angle X,\ \angle Y,\ \angle Z and XY,\ YZ,\ ZX.
(b) \angle A=52^\circ and \angle B=71^\circ. Find \angle C, \angle X, \angle Y and \angle Z.
\angle C=180^\circ-52^\circ-71^\circ=57^\circ (angles in a triangle)
Corresponding angles are equal: \angle X=52^\circ,\ \angle Y=71^\circ,\ \angle Z=57^\circ
(c) Write the three equal ratios of corresponding sides (new over original).
\dfrac{XY}{AB}=\dfrac{YZ}{BC}=\dfrac{ZX}{CA}
Example 3

A flipped quadrilateral

Quadrilateral ABCD\sim quadrilateral EFGH. EFGH is smaller and flipped over. \angle A=78^\circ, \angle B=112^\circ and \angle C=64^\circ.

(a) Find \angle D.
Angles in a quadrilateral add to 360°.
\angle D=360^\circ-78^\circ-112^\circ-64^\circ=106^\circ
(b) Find \angle E, \angle F, \angle G and \angle H.
\angle E=\angle A=78^\circ,\ \angle F=\angle B=112^\circ,
\angle G=\angle C=64^\circ,\ \angle H=\angle D=106^\circ
(c) Which side of EFGH corresponds to BC? FG   Which side of ABCD corresponds to HE? DA
B\leftrightarrow F and C\leftrightarrow G, so BC\leftrightarrow FG. H\leftrightarrow D and E\leftrightarrow A, so HE\leftrightarrow DA.
3

The Scale Factor

When a figure is enlarged or reduced to make a similar figure, every length is multiplied by the same number. This number is called the scale factor, k. The first figure is the original. The figure you make from it is the new figure (also called the image).

Key idea   Scale factor:   k=\dfrac{\text{new length}}{\text{original length}}   (use a pair of corresponding sides)
k>1: an enlargement (the new figure is bigger)
k<1: a reduction (smaller)
k=1: the same size
!
Watch out. Put the new length on top. Both lengths must be in the same unit, so change units first (1 m = 100 cm). Estimate: if the new figure is bigger, k must be more than 1.

Try it: grow and shrink a shape

Drag the k slider. Every side of the new figure is k\times the matching side, and the ratio tags (new ÷ original) all show the same number. Then drag stretch sideways: the tags stop agreeing, and the shape is no longer similar.

Example 4

Find the scale factor

Each row gives a side of an original figure and the matching side of a similar new figure. Find the scale factor. Is it an enlargement, a reduction, or the same size?
OriginalNewScale factor kType
(a)4 cm10 cm10\div4=2.5enlargement
(b)12 m3 m3\div12=0.25reduction
(c)15 cm6 cm6\div15=0.4reduction
(d)7 in7 in7\div7=1same size (congruent)
(e)2.4 ft8.4 ft8.4\div2.4=3.5enlargement
(a) 2.5, enlargement · (b) 0.25, reduction · (c) 0.4, reduction · (d) 1, same size (congruent) · (e) 3.5, enlargement. Estimate first: if the new side is longer, k is more than 1.

A scale factor can be a fraction. In (b), k=0.25=\tfrac{1}{4}: each new side is one quarter of the original side.

New ÷ original, in that order. For (e): 8.4 ÷ 2.4 ENTER gives 3.5.
Example 5

Change the units first

(a) A school logo is 5 cm wide on a letter. On a banner in the gym, the logo is 1.2 m wide. Find the scale factor from the letter to the banner.
Same units first: 1.2 m = 120 cm
k=\dfrac{\text{new}}{\text{original}}=\dfrac{120}{5}=24
The scale factor is 24: the banner logo is 24 times as wide. (An enlargement.)
(b) A model of a railway boxcar is 18 cm long. The real boxcar is 15.66 m long. Find the scale factor from the real boxcar to the model.
15.66 m = 1566 cm. The real boxcar is the original.
k=\dfrac{18}{1566}=0.0114\ldots Flip it to see it better: 1566\div18=87, so k=\tfrac{1}{87}.
The model is \tfrac{1}{87} the size of the real boxcar. (A reduction.)
A tiny decimal is hard to read. Divide the other way: 1566 ÷ 18 ENTER gives 87. So k=\tfrac{1}{87}
i
Model scales. A model labelled 1 : 87 means model length : real length = 1 : 87. Every part of the model is \tfrac{1}{87} of the real size, so k=\tfrac{1}{87} from real to model. Other common scales: 1 : 24 and 1 : 64 for model cars.
Think The scale factor from a real car to a model is \tfrac{1}{64}. What is the scale factor from the model back to the real car?
64. Going back, the new figure is the real car and the original is the model, so k=\text{real}\div\text{model}=64. The real car is 64 times as big.

Using the Scale Factor to Find Missing Sides

Once you know the scale factor, you can find any missing side of two similar figures.

Key idea   Finding a missing side: 4 steps
  1. Step 1. Match the corresponding sides (use the naming order).
  2. Step 2. Find k from a pair of corresponding sides where you know both lengths: k=\text{new}\div\text{original}.
  3. Step 3. A side of the new figure: multiply, \text{new}=k\times\text{original}.
    A side of the original figure: divide, \text{original}=\text{new}\div k.
  4. Step 4. Check: in an enlargement the new sides are longer; in a reduction they are shorter.
i
Another way. You can also use a proportion, as in Lesson 1.4. For a missing side x: \dfrac{x}{5}=\dfrac{12}{8}, so x=12\times5\div8=7.5. Both methods give the same answer.
Example 6

Find the missing sides

ABCD\sim PQRS. The diagrams are not drawn to scale.

(a) Which two sides give the scale factor? Find k from ABCD to PQRS.
AB and PQ: we know both lengths.
k=\dfrac{PQ}{AB}=\dfrac{12}{8}=1.5 (an enlargement)
(b) Find QR and SP.
New sides: multiply by k.
QR=1.5\times BC=1.5\times5=7.5 cm
SP=1.5\times DA=1.5\times6=9 cm
(c) Find CD. Then check one answer with a proportion.
CD is in the original: divide by k.
CD=RS\div1.5=6.6\div1.5=4.4 cm
Check with \frac{QR}{5}=\frac{12}{8}: QR=12\times5\div8=7.5 ✓
A side of the original: divide. 6.6 ÷ 1.5 ENTER gives 4.4.

Another way: the same answers with a proportion

Example 7

A poster and a model truck

(a) Mia has a 10 cm by 15 cm photo of her dog. She orders a similar poster print that is 60 cm on the short side.
(i) Find the scale factor.
k=\dfrac{60}{10}=6
(b) A 1 : 24 model of a pickup truck is sold at a hobby shop in Red Deer.
(i) The real truck is 5.88 m long. How long is the model, in centimetres?
5.88 m = 588 cm, k=\tfrac{1}{24}
Model =588\div24=24.5 cm (multiplying by \tfrac{1}{24} is dividing by 24)
(a)(ii) How long is the long side of the poster?
Long side =6\times15=90 cm
The poster is 60 cm by 90 cm.
(b)(ii) The model is 8.25 cm tall. How tall is the real truck, in metres?
The real truck is 24 times as big: 8.25\times24=198 cm = 1.98 m
The real truck is 1.98 m tall.
Real to model at 1 : 24 is “divide by 24”: 588 ÷ 24 ENTER gives 24.5.

The scale-factor machine: multiply going forward, divide going back

i
Good to know. Areas do not grow by k. If every length is doubled (k=2), the area is 2\times2=4 times as big. The 60 cm by 90 cm poster above has 6\times6=36 times the area of the photo.

Why the area grows by k\times k

Count how many copies of the original fit inside the new figure.

Extra example

A 1 : 50 model house

An architect builds a 1 : 50 model of a new house in Cochrane.

(a) The model is 24 cm wide. How wide is the real house, in metres?
The real house is 50 times as big: 24\times50=1\,200 cm = 12 m
(b) The real front door is 2.1 m tall. How tall is the model door, in centimetres?
2.1 m = 210 cm. 210\div50=4.2 cm
(c) What is the scale factor from the real house to the model? Is it an enlargement or a reduction?
k=\tfrac{1}{50}=0.02, less than 1: a reduction.
✓

Key ideas

Similar (\sim): the same shape. Corresponding angles are equal, and every side is multiplied by the same number. Turning or flipping is fine; stretching one way is not. Same size too = congruent.
Naming order: in ABCD\sim PQRS, 1st letter with 1st, 2nd with 2nd… Write the names one above the other and read down the columns. Don’t match by position on the page.
Scale factor: k=\text{new}\div\text{original}, same units first. k>1 enlargement, k<1 reduction, k=1 same size. A 1 : 87 model has k=\tfrac{1}{87}.
Missing sides: find k from a pair you know. New side: multiply by k. Original side: divide by k. Check: bigger in an enlargement, smaller in a reduction.
