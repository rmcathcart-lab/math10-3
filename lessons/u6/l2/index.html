The Handshake Party

Ten friends meet at Prince’s Island Park. Everyone shakes hands with everyone else, exactly once.

How many handshakes is that?

A 10
B 20
C 45
D 90
C: 45 handshakes. The first friend shakes 9 hands. The second friend already shook with the first, so only 8 are new. Then 7 new, 6, 5 … down to 1: 9+8+7+6+5+4+3+2+1=45. Why not 90? Each of the 10 people shakes 9 hands, and 10\times9=90. But every handshake has two people in it, so it got counted twice: 90\div2=45. Are They Similar? Checking Angles and Side Ratios

A photo shop, a flag maker and a sign shop all ask the same question: is the new one exactly the same shape? Today we learn the two tests that answer it, how to explain when two shapes are not similar, and why some shapes are always similar.

I can…
  • match the corresponding angles and sides of two polygons, using the naming order
  • check Test 1: every pair of corresponding angles is equal
  • check Test 2: every pair of corresponding sides has the same ratio (the scale factor k)
  • explain why two polygons are not similar, naming the pair that fails
  • explain why two regular polygons with the same number of sides are always similar
G3 Demonstrate an understanding of similarity of convex polygons, including regular and irregular polygons.
Today: deciding if two polygons are similar, explaining why or why not, and regular polygons.
1

The Two Tests for Similar Polygons

Two polygons are similar if they have exactly the same shape. One can be bigger or smaller than the other, or turned around, but nothing is stretched or squished. Matching parts are called corresponding angles and corresponding sides.

Key idea   The symbol \sim means “is similar to”. In ABCD\sim PQRS the letters are in matching order: A\leftrightarrow P, B\leftrightarrow Q, C\leftrightarrow R, D\leftrightarrow S. So side AB\leftrightarrow PQ, and so on.

Both tests must pass

Key idea   Two polygons are similar only if BOTH tests pass.
  1. Test 1 (angles): every pair of corresponding angles is equal.
  2. Test 2 (sides): every pair of corresponding sides has the same ratio. Divide (side of the new shape) \div (matching side of the original) for every pair. If you get the same number every time, that number is the scale factor k.
If even one pair fails, the polygons are not similar.
Key idea   Checking, step by step
  1. Step 1. Match the corresponding vertices (use the naming order, or match the angles).
  2. Step 2. Compare each pair of angles.
  3. Step 3. Divide each pair of sides, the same way every time.
  4. Step 4. Decide, and write a sentence: “The polygons are (not) similar because …”
!
Watch out. Divide the same way every time: the same shape’s side always goes on top. If you flip one ratio (6\div9 instead of 9\div6) the numbers will not match, even when the shapes are similar.

What goes wrong if you flip one ratio?

Think Ali divides ABCD sides by PQRS sides every time in Example 1. He gets 0.67 four times. Is he wrong?
No! He divided the same way every time, so all his ratios match: the shapes are similar. His 0.67 (that is 6\div9) is the scale factor going the other way, from PQRS back down to ABCD (a reduction).
2

Checking the Angles

Making a shape bigger or smaller never changes its angles. So in similar polygons, corresponding angles are equal. You can measure the angles with a protractor, read them from the diagram, or work out a missing one.

↺
Quick refresher. The angles inside a polygon with n sides add up to (n-2)\times180^\circ (Unit 5). A quadrilateral (4 sides): 360°. A pentagon (5 sides): 540°. A hexagon (6 sides): 720°.
Example 2

A square and a rhombus

Square ABCD and rhombus PQRS are drawn full size in your booklet: measure them there with a ruler and a protractor. (A rhombus has four equal sides.)

(a) Measure one side of each shape to the nearest tenth of a centimetre. AB= 2.0 cm PQ= 3.0 cm
AB=2.0 cm and PQ=3.0 cm
(b) All the sides of each shape are equal. Find the ratio for every pair of sides.
3.0\div2.0=1.5 for all four pairs: Test 2 passes.
(c) Measure the angles. \angle A= 90° \angle P= 60° \angle Q= 120° \angle R= 60° \angle S= 120°
The square: all 90°. The rhombus: \angle P=60^\circ, \angle Q=120^\circ, \angle R=60^\circ, \angle S=120^\circ.
(d) Are the square and the rhombus similar? Explain.
No. The side ratios are all the same (1.5), but the angles are not equal: \angle A=90^\circ but \angle P=60^\circ. Test 1 fails, so they are not similar.

When an angle is missing, find it first

Use the angle sum to find any missing angle. Then compare the angles in pairs.

Example 3

Do the angles match?

Quadrilaterals EFGH and JKLM have the angles shown. Their side lengths are not given.

(a) Find \angle H and \angle L.
The angles of a quadrilateral add to 360°.
\angle H=360^\circ-75^\circ-105^\circ-80^\circ=100^\circ
\angle L=360^\circ-75^\circ-105^\circ-100^\circ=80^\circ
(b) Do all the corresponding angles match?
\angle E=\angle J=75^\circ, \angle F=\angle K=105^\circ,
\angle G=\angle L=80^\circ, \angle H=\angle M=100^\circ.
Yes: Test 1 passes.
(c) Can you say EFGH\sim JKLM? Explain.
Not yet. The angles match, but we don’t know the side lengths, so we can’t do Test 2. (Look at the pictures: JKLM looks longer and flatter, so it may not be similar.)
(d) Two pentagons have three pairs of equal angles: 100°, 120° and 95°. The other two angles of each pentagon are not given. All the side ratios are 2. Is that enough to say the pentagons are similar? Explain.
No. The angles of a pentagon add to 540°, so in each pentagon the other two angles add to 540^\circ-100^\circ-120^\circ-95^\circ=225^\circ. But they could be split differently, for example 110^\circ+115^\circ in one and 105^\circ+120^\circ in the other. We need to know every pair of angles.
3

Checking the Side Ratios

Equal angles are not enough. The sides must all grow (or shrink) by the same scale factor. Divide each pair of corresponding sides. If all the ratios are the same, Test 2 passes.

Key idea   Rectangles: every rectangle has four 90° angles, so Test 1 always passes. Only the sides decide.
  1. Method 1 (between): long \div long and short \div short. Similar if the two answers are equal (that is k).
  2. Method 2 (within): long side \div short side for each rectangle. Similar if the two answers are equal.
!
Watch out. Match short with short and long with long, even if one rectangle is turned on its side. If the units are different (in and cm, or cm and m), convert first for Method 1. Method 2 works without converting, because both sides of one rectangle have the same units.
If a ratio does not come out even, round it to 2 decimal places. 10 ÷ 7 ENTER gives 1.428571… \approx1.43. If the rounded ratios are different, the shapes are not similar.

Try it: will the photo fit?

3-D objects: boxes and cans

The same side test works for 3-D objects such as boxes and cans: divide every pair of matching measurements (length, width, height, or radius and height).

Watch a box and a can grow

Example 6

Boxes and cans

(a) Two shipping boxes measure 20 cm by 30 cm by 40 cm and 30 cm by 45 cm by 60 cm. Are they similar?
30\div20=1.5, 45\div30=1.5, 60\div40=1.5
All three ratios are 1.5, so the boxes are similar (k=1.5).
(b) One soup can has a radius of 4 cm and a height of 10 cm. A bigger can has a radius of 6 cm and a height of 12 cm. Are the cans similar?
Radius: 6\div4=1.5. Height: 12\div10=1.2.
1.5\ne1.2, so the cans are not similar. (The big can is too short.)

Explaining Why Two Polygons Are Not Similar

To show two polygons are not similar, you only need one pair of angles that are not equal, or one side ratio that does not match. Name it in your answer.

Key idea   Three common traps
A square and a rectangle (not a square)angles all match (90°)but the side ratios do not
A square and a rhombus (not a square)side ratios all matchbut the angles do not
Adding the same amount to every side(a frame, a border)the side ratios change
Sentence frame: The polygons are not similar because is not equal to .
↺
Quick refresher. In a parallelogram, opposite sides are parallel and equal. Opposite angles are equal, and two angles next to each other add to 180° (interior angles on the same side of a transversal, Unit 5).

The similarity tester on two more pairs

Example 7

Two parallelograms

Are parallelograms ABCD and WXYZ similar? (Not drawn to scale.)
(a) Find all four angles of each parallelogram.
\angle A=\angle C=50^\circ (opposite angles)
\angle B=\angle D=180^\circ-50^\circ=130^\circ
In the same way: \angle W=\angle Y=50^\circ, \angle X=\angle Z=130^\circ. All the corresponding angles match.
(b) Find the side ratios.
WX\div AB=15\div10=1.5
XY\div BC=6.5\div4=1.625 (BC=AD=4, XY=WZ=6.5)
The ratios are not equal.
(c) Use the sentence frame to answer the question.
The parallelograms are not similar because the ratio 15\div10=1.5 is not equal to the ratio 6.5\div4=1.625. (The angles match, but the sides do not grow by the same scale factor.)
6.5 ÷ 4 ENTER gives 1.625. Estimate first: 6.5 is a bit more than 1.5\times4=6, so the ratio is a bit more than 1.5.

A mat, and Owen’s mistake

(a) A 20 cm by 30 cm picture is put on a mat with a 5 cm border on every side. Find the outside size of the mat. Is the matted picture similar to the picture alone? Show your work.
Outside: 20+5+5=30 cm by 30+5+5=40 cm.
Short: 30\div20=1.5. Long: 40\div30\approx1.33. The ratios are not equal, so it is not similar. Adding 10 cm to both sides is not the same as multiplying both sides by the same number.
(b) Owen checks if a 24 cm by 40 cm rectangle and a 50 cm by 30 cm rectangle are similar. Find his mistake, and fix it.
Owen’s work:   50\div24\approx2.08   and   30\div40=0.75.   They are not equal, so they are not similar.
Owen matched a short side with a long side (24 with 50). Match short with short and long with long:
Short: 30\div24=1.25. Long: 50\div40=1.25. They are equal, so the rectangles are similar, with k=1.25.
5

Regular Polygons

A regular polygon has all its sides equal and all its angles equal: an equilateral triangle, a square, a regular pentagon, a regular hexagon (6 sides), a regular octagon (8 sides, like a stop sign), and so on.

Take two regular polygons with the same number of sides. Their angles are all the same size: each angle is (n-2)\times180^\circ\div n. And since all the sides of each shape are equal, every side ratio is the same number. Both tests pass every time!

Key idea   Two regular polygons with the same number of sides are always similar.
  1. Always similar: two squares, two equilateral triangles, two regular hexagons, …
  2. Sometimes similar (you must check): two rectangles, two rhombuses, two parallelograms, two pentagons that are not regular.
  3. Never similar: two polygons with a different number of sides (a square and a pentagon).
Example 9

Stop signs and tiles

(a) A stop sign is a regular octagon with sides of 25 cm. A larger stop sign for a highway has sides of 32 cm. Are the signs similar? Find the size of each angle and the scale factor.
Both are regular octagons, so they are similar.
Each angle: (8-2)\times180^\circ\div8=1\,080^\circ\div8=135^\circ (in both signs).
Scale factor: k=32\div25=1.28 (an enlargement).
Each angle in one go: ( 8 − 2 ) × 180 ÷ 8 ENTER gives 135.
(b) A tile is a regular hexagon with sides of 10 cm. Another tile is a regular pentagon with sides of 10 cm. Are they similar? Explain.
No. They have a different number of sides, so the vertices can’t be matched. (Also, a hexagon’s angles are 720^\circ\div6=120^\circ, but a regular pentagon’s are 540^\circ\div5=108^\circ.) Having equal side lengths does not make shapes similar.
(c) Write always, sometimes or never.
Two squares are similar. always
Two rectangles are similar. sometimes
A square and an equilateral triangle are similar. never
Two rhombuses are similar. sometimes
always · sometimes · never · sometimes. Two squares are regular with 4 sides each. Rectangles must be checked (their side ratios can differ), and so must rhombuses (their angles can differ). A square has 4 sides and a triangle has 3, so they can never match.
✓

Key ideas

Similar: same shape, any size, may be turned. Match the vertices by the naming order: in ABCD\sim PQRS, A\leftrightarrow P, side AB\leftrightarrow PQ.
Both tests: (1) every pair of angles equal; (2) every side ratio, new \div original, the same (that is k). Divide the same way every time.
Rectangles: long \div short for each one (no converting needed), or long \div long and short \div short. Boxes and cans: divide every matching measurement.
Not similar? Name the one pair that fails. Traps: square and rectangle, square and rhombus, adding a border. Regular polygons with the same number of sides: always similar.
