Cover the Full Moon

Tonight there is a big, bright full Moon.

You close one eye, stretch your arm out all the way, and hold up a small object in front of the Moon.

What is the smallest thing that can hide the whole Moon?

A a dinner plate
B a CD
C a loonie
D the eraser on a pencil
D: even a pencil’s eraser! At arm’s length, the full Moon looks only about half a centimetre wide. A loonie hides it four times over. The light from the Moon and the light from the eraser make two long, thin triangles with the same shape, both starting at your eye. Try it tonight. (The Moon looks huge near the horizon, but the eraser still covers it!)
Math 10-3 · Unit 6 Similarity and Scale · Lesson 3

Drawing Enlargements and Reductions

A photo blown up into a poster, a bigger birdhouse, a small copy of a detour sign: each one is a similar copy. Today we draw enlargements and reductions three ways: on grid paper, with a ruler and protractor, and by projecting from a point. Then we check our drawings.

I can…
  • make a similar copy: multiply every length by the scale factor k and keep every angle the same
  • draw an enlargement or reduction on grid paper by counting moves and multiplying them by k
  • draw a similar polygon with a ruler and protractor
  • use the projection method from a centre O: OA'=k\times OA
  • fit a photo on a poster, and check a drawing by dividing new sides by old sides
G3 Demonstrate an understanding of similarity of convex polygons, including regular and irregular polygons.
Today: drawing enlargements and reductions that are similar to a given polygon, and checking them.
1

Multiply Every Length, Keep Every Angle

An enlargement is a bigger copy of a figure. A reduction is a smaller copy. For the copy to be similar (the same shape), every length must be multiplied by the same number, the scale factor k, and every angle must stay the same.

Key idea   Drawing a similar figure
New length =k\times original length
Original length = new length \div k
Every angle stays the same size.
k>1: enlargement (bigger)  ·  k<1: reduction (smaller)  ·  k=1: same size

Add or multiply?

!
Watch out. Adding the same amount to every side does not make a similar figure. A 2 cm by 4 cm rectangle with 2 cm added to each side becomes 4 cm by 6 cm. The short side doubled (\times2) but the long side did not (\times1.5), so the shape changed. Always multiply.
Example 1

Copies of a quadrilateral

(a) Quadrilateral ABCD has AB=6 cm, BC=10 cm, CD=8 cm, DA=4 cm and \angle A=75^\circ. Complete the table for each copy.
kEnlargement or reduction?ABBCCDDA\angle A
3enlargement18 cm30 cm24 cm12 cm75°
0.5reduction3 cm5 cm4 cm2 cm75°
1.25enlargement7.5 cm12.5 cm10 cm5 cm75°
Multiply each side by k. For k=1.25: 6\times1.25=7.5, 10\times1.25=12.5, 8\times1.25=10, 4\times1.25=5. The angle does not change: 75° in every copy.
(b) A copy made with k=2.5 has a side of 20 cm. How long was that side on the original?
Original = new \div k=20\div2.5=8 cm.  Check: 8\times2.5=20 ✓
Example 2

Ren’s bigger birdhouse

Ren is building a bigger copy of a birdhouse. The front of the old birdhouse is shown (not to scale). The new front will have a base of 24 cm.

(a) Find the scale factor.
k=\text{new}\div\text{original}=24\div15=1.6 (an enlargement, k>1)
(b) Find the lengths of the new walls and roof edges.
Walls: 12\times1.6=19.2 cm. Roof edges: 10.5\times1.6=16.8 cm.
(c) What is the angle at the peak of the new roof?
Angles don’t change: the peak is still 91°.
(d) The old birdhouse is 14 cm deep (front to back). How deep should the new one be?
Every length is multiplied, including the depth: 14\times1.6=22.4 cm.
The scale factor: 24 ÷ 15 ENTER gives 1.6. Then a roof edge: 10.5 × 1.6 ENTER gives 16.8.
Extra example

Copies of a triangle

A triangle has sides of 6 cm, 9 cm and 12 cm.

(a) Find the sides of a reduction with k=\tfrac23.
6\times\tfrac23=4, 9\times\tfrac23=6, 12\times\tfrac23=8. The sides are 4 cm, 6 cm and 8 cm.
(b) In an enlargement, the side that matches the 12 cm side is 15 cm. Find k and the other two sides.
k=15\div12=1.25. 6\times1.25=7.5 cm and 9\times1.25=11.25 cm.
Think Can a reduction have an angle that is smaller than the matching angle in the original?
No. A reduction is similar, so every angle stays exactly the same. Only the lengths get smaller.
2

The Grid Method

Grid paper makes enlargements easy, because you can count squares instead of measuring. Walk around the shape from corner to corner and write each move: how many squares right or left, and how many up or down. Then multiply every count by k.

Key idea   The grid method
  1. Step 1. Pick a starting corner on the original. Mark a starting dot on the new grid.
  2. Step 2. Count the move to the next corner (right/left and up/down).
  3. Step 3. Multiply each count by k. Make that move on the new grid and mark the corner.
  4. Step 4. Repeat all the way around. You should land back on the starting dot.
  5. Step 5. Join the corners with a ruler.
!
Watch out. Don’t count squares along a slanted side. A slanted side that goes 2 right and 1 up becomes 4 right and 2 up when k=2. Multiply both counts. If k=1.5 and a count is odd, the corner lands in the middle of a square. That’s fine: 3\times1.5=4.5 squares.

Slanted sides and half squares

Think A slanted side goes 3 right and 2 up. Mo enlarges it with k=2 and draws 6 right and 2 up. What went wrong?
He only multiplied one count. It should be 6 right and 4 up. His side has a different slant, so the shape changes.

Try it: draw on the grid

Pick a shape. Tap grid points to draw its image, one corner at a time. Tap your first corner to close it, then press Check. It tells you if you added instead of multiplied, or used the wrong k.

Example 4

Shrink the detour arrow

A road crew’s detour sign is an arrow. Draw a reduction of the arrow with a scale factor of \tfrac12 (that is, k=0.5). Start at the dot.
Moves on the original, starting at the dot and going right: 6 right; 2 down; 4 right and 4 up; 4 left and 4 up; 2 down; 6 left; 4 down.
Halve each one: 3 right; 1 down; 2 right and 2 up; 2 left and 2 up; 1 down; 3 left; 2 down (back to the start ✓).
The arrow is 10 squares long and 8 squares tall. The reduction is 5 squares long and 4 squares tall.
Extra example

Half squares are fine

Enlarge the L-shape with k=1.5. Start at the dot. Some corners land in the middle of a square.
Original moves: 3 right; 1 up; 2 left; 2 up; 1 left; 3 down.  \times1.5: 4.5 right; 1.5 up; 3 left; 3 up; 1.5 left; 4.5 down (back to the start ✓).
3

The Ruler and Protractor Method

When a figure is not on a grid, measure it. Multiply each side length by k, and copy each angle with a protractor (Unit 5). A triangle only needs one side and the two angles at its ends: the other two sides are drawn for you when the arms meet.

Key idea   Ruler and protractor method (a triangle)
  1. Step 1. Measure one side and the two angles at its ends.
  2. Step 2. Multiply the side by k. Draw the new side with a ruler.
  3. Step 3. At each end of the new side, use the protractor to draw an arm at the same angle as the original.
  4. Step 4. Extend the two arms until they meet. That point is the third corner.
  5. Step 5. Check: measure the third angle (it should match) and the other sides (each should be k times the original).
More sides? Draw a side, copy the angle at its end, measure the next side (\times k) along the new arm, and keep going.
Example 5

Enlarge a triangle with a ruler and protractor

(a) Measure side QR, \angle Q and \angle R of \triangle PQR (in your booklet: it is full size there).
QR=4 cm, \angle Q=55^\circ, \angle R=65^\circ
(b) In the space, draw \triangle P'Q'R', an enlargement of \triangle PQR with k=1.5.
Q'R'=4\times1.5=6 cm. Draw 55^\circ at Q' and 65^\circ at R'; the arms meet at P'.
(c) Check: measure \angle P, \angle P', PQ and P'Q'.
\angle P=\angle P'=60^\circ (180-55-65=60). PQ\approx4.2 cm, P'Q'\approx6.3 cm, and 4.2\times1.5=6.3 ✓
Example 6

Theo’s skateboard ramp

Theo sketched the side of a skateboard ramp (not to scale). Angles A and D are right angles. Draw a reduction of ABCD with k=0.5. Then measure B'C' to check your drawing.
New sides: A'B'=12\times0.5=6 cm, A'D'=6\times0.5=3 cm, D'C'=4\times0.5=2 cm. Draw A'B', a right angle at A', then A'D'; a right angle at D', then D'C'. Join C' to B'.
Check: B'C' should be 10\times0.5=5 cm. It measures 5 cm ✓ (and \angle B'\approx37^\circ, the same as \angle B).

4

The Projection Method

The projection method works like a projector, or a flashlight making a shadow on a wall: everything spreads out from one point. Pick a point O, the centre. Draw a ray from O through each corner. Each new corner goes on the same ray, k times as far from O.

Key idea   The projection method
  1. Step 1. Draw a ray from O through each corner (A, B, C, …).
  2. Step 2. Measure OA. Multiply by k. Mark A' on the same ray, that far from O: OA'=k\times OA.
  3. Step 3. Do the same for every corner.
  4. Step 4. Join the new corners in the same order.
If k>1, the new corners are past the old ones. If k<1, they are between O and the old ones. O can be outside or inside the shape.
!
Watch out. Measure from O, not from the old corner. With k=3 and OA=2 cm, A' is 6 cm from O (only 4 cm past A).

Why does projecting make a similar shape?

Think What happens if you use the projection method with k=1?
Every new corner is the same distance from O as the old one, so it lands right on top of it. The copy is the same size, in the same place.

Math History1603

A machine that draws enlargements

In 1603, the German astronomer Christoph Scheiner built the pantograph: four rods joined like a parallelogram. His book about it came out in 1631.

One end is pinned to the table: the centre O. You trace a picture with a pointer, and a pencil copies it, bigger. Pin, pointer and pencil stay on one line, and the pencil is always k times as far from the pin. It is the projection method, done by a machine!

A pantograph is set to k=2. The pointer is 9 cm from the pin. How far from the pin is the pencil?
2\times9=18 cm, on the same line as the pin and the pointer.
5

Photos, Posters and Checking Your Drawing

A photo is a rectangle. A print or poster is similar to the photo only if both sides are multiplied by the same k. Common photo print sizes (in inches) are 4 × 6, 5 × 7, 8 × 10 and 11 × 14, and they are not all similar. If a print is not similar to the photo, part of the picture gets cropped (cut off), or the picture gets stretched.

Key idea   Fitting an enlargement on paper or in a frame
Find k for each direction:  k=\text{space available}\div\text{photo length}.
Use the smaller k. Then the whole picture fits, with some space left over on one side.
Example 9

Photos and a poster

(a) Mei has a 4 in by 6 in photo. She orders an enlargement with k=3. What size is the print?
4\times3=12 and 6\times3=18. The print is 12 in by 18 in.
(b) Is an 8 in by 10 in print similar to Mei’s 4 in by 6 in photo? Explain.
8\div4=2, but 10\div6\approx1.67. The sides were not multiplied by the same number, so it is not similar. Part of the photo would be cropped.
(c) The school’s art club has a 15 cm by 10 cm photo. They want the biggest similar enlargement that fits on a 60 cm by 45 cm sheet of poster paper. Find k, the size of the poster, and how much paper is left over.
Long sides: 60\div15=4. Short sides: 45\div10=4.5. Use the smaller: k=4.
Poster: 15\times4=60 cm by 10\times4=40 cm. (With k=4.5 the long side would be 67.5 cm: too long.)
Left over: 45-40=5 cm across the short direction. The poster is 60 cm by 40 cm, with a 5 cm strip of paper left over.
Each direction: 60 ÷ 15 ENTER gives 4 and 45 ÷ 10 ENTER gives 4.5. Keep the smaller one.
Extra example

The biggest copy that fits

A 12 cm by 8 cm photo of the school team is enlarged for a bulletin board space that is 50 cm by 30 cm. Find the biggest similar enlargement that fits, and how much space is left over.
Long: 50\div12\approx4.17. Short: 30\div8=3.75. Use the smaller: k=3.75.
12\times3.75=45 cm by 8\times3.75=30 cm. Left over: 50-45=5 cm along the long direction.

Checking a drawing

Key idea   Checking a drawing
  1. 1. Measure each pair of matching angles. They should be equal.
  2. 2. Divide each new side by its matching old side. Every answer should be k.
Measuring isn’t perfect: being off by about 1 mm or 1° is fine.
Example 10

Check Jada’s copy

Jada was asked to enlarge quadrilateral WXYZ with k=3. Here are her measurements.

SideWXXYYZZW
Original2.0 cm3.0 cm2.5 cm4.0 cm
Jada’s copy6.0 cm9.0 cm8.5 cm12.0 cm
Copy ÷ original333.43
(a) Is Jada’s copy similar to the original? Which side is wrong, and how long should it be?
No. YZ gives 8.5\div2.5=3.4, not 3. Y'Z' should be 2.5\times3=7.5 cm.
(b) The sides are fixed. What else must Jada check?
Each angle in her copy must be the same as the matching angle in the original. Measure all four pairs.
Copy ÷ original for YZ: 8.5 ÷ 2.5 ENTER gives 3.4, not 3.
✓

Key ideas

Similar copy: new length =k\times original, original = new \div k. Angles stay the same. k>1 enlargement, k<1 reduction. Multiply, never add.
Grid: count each move (right/left, up/down), multiply both counts by k, and land back on the start. Ruler and protractor: side \times k, copy the angles.
Projection: rays from O through each corner; OA'=k\times OA, measured from O. Past the shape if k>1, between if k<1.
Photos: k for each direction, use the smaller. Check: new ÷ old =k for every side, and matching angles are equal.
