The Full-Length Mirror

You want a mirror on your bedroom wall that shows all of you, from the top of your head down to your shoes.

You are 170 cm tall. The mirror hangs flat on the wall, and you stand in front of it.

What is the shortest mirror that can show your whole body?

A 170 cm, as tall as you
B about 120 cm
C 85 cm
D it depends how far back you stand
C: only 85 cm, half your height (if it hangs at the right height). Light from your shoes hits the mirror and bounces up to your eyes. It bounces off at the same angle it came in, so it hits the mirror halfway between your shoes and your eyes. Light from the top of your head works the same way. So the mirror only needs half your height. Stepping back doesn’t help: you see the same part of yourself from anywhere. Try it in a bathroom mirror! The line straight out from the mirror cuts that bounce angle exactly in half: today we cut angles in half.

1

Copying an Angle

To copy (or replicate) an angle means to draw a new angle with exactly the same size. Two angles with the same size are called congruent. Carpenters copy angles all the time: the angle of a stair, a roof or a crooked wall has to be copied onto a board before it is cut.

ToolHow it copies an angle
ProtractorMeasure the angle. Then draw a new angle with the same number of degrees.
Tracing paper or a MiraTrace the angle and move the tracing. A Mira is a see-through red plastic mirror: you look through it, line up the reflection of the angle, and draw it.
Sliding T-bevelA carpenter’s tool with a blade that swings and locks. Set the blade along both arms of the angle, lock it, then move it to the new board and draw.
Carpenter’s squareAn L-shaped steel square. It only copies a right angle (90°). A speed square (a triangle) also marks 45° and has a degree scale.
Compass and straightedgeNo numbers at all: the compass copies distances, and that copies the angle (steps below).
Geometry softwareMany computer drawing programs can measure an angle and copy it exactly.
Think A Mira shows a mirror image of the angle: it opens the other way. Is the copy still the same size?
Yes. Flipping an angle over doesn’t open or close its arms, so the copy is congruent. Only its direction changes.
Key idea   Copy \angle ABC with a compass and straightedge
  • Step 1. Put the compass point on the vertex B. Draw an arc that crosses both arms. Label the crossings P and Q.
  • Step 2. Draw a ray from a new point E. Keep the same compass width. Put the point on E and draw a long arc that crosses the ray at R.
  • Step 3. Set the compass to the distance from Q to P. Put the point on R and draw an arc that crosses the long arc at S.
  • Step 4. Draw the ray from E through S. Now \angle SER=\angle ABC.
!
Watch out. In Steps 1 and 2 the compass width must be the same. Only in Step 3 do you change it, to the distance from Q to P. Hold the compass by the top so the width doesn’t slip.

Why does the compass copy work? It copies a triangle

The compass copies three distances. Those three distances make a triangle, and a triangle with the same three sides has the same angles.

Example 1

Copy with a protractor

Measure \angle KLM with a protractor. Then use the protractor to copy it onto ray ST, with its vertex at S.
Centre on L, 0 line on LM, read the scale that starts at 0 on LM: \angle KLM=64^\circ (acute ✓). Then centre on S, 0 line on ST, mark a dot at 64° on the scale that starts at 0 on ST, and join S to the dot.
Example 2

Copy with a compass and straightedge

Copy \angle DEF onto ray GH using only a compass and a straightedge. Then measure both angles to check.
Arc from E (crossing both arms), the same arc from G, then the distance between the crossings on \angle DEF marked from the crossing on GH. Draw the new arm. Check: both angles measure 112°. (Replay it: Example 2 check in the animation above.)

Bisecting an Angle

Key idea   To bisect means to cut into two equal parts. The angle bisector is the ray from the vertex that cuts an angle into two equal angles.
each half = the angle \div 2the whole angle = the half \times 2

Ways to bisect an angle:

  • Protractor: measure, divide by 2, and mark that many degrees from one arm.
  • Folding: draw or trace the angle on thin paper. Fold through the vertex so one arm lies exactly on top of the other. The crease is the bisector.
  • Mira: stand it on the vertex and turn it until the reflection of one arm lies on the other arm. Draw along its edge.
  • Compass and straightedge: the steps below.
Key idea   Bisect \angle ABC with a compass and straightedge
  • Step 1. Compass point on the vertex B. Draw an arc that crosses both arms at P and Q.
  • Step 2. Compass point on Q. Draw an arc inside the angle.
  • Step 3. Same width. Compass point on P. Draw an arc that crosses the Step 2 arc at D.
  • Step 4. Draw the ray from B through D. Ray BD bisects \angle ABC, so \angle ABD=\angle DBC.
!
Watch out. In Steps 2 and 3, the compass must open to more than half the distance from P to Q, or the two arcs won’t cross. Don’t change the width between Step 2 and Step 3.
Think How could you bisect a reflex angle (more than 180°)?
Bisect the smaller angle on the other side of the arms. Then extend that bisector backward through the vertex. The new ray cuts the reflex angle in half.

Why does the compass bisector work? Two matching triangles

Steps 1 to 3 make a kite shape BPDQ:

  • BP=BQ (one arc in Step 1),
  • PD=QD (the same width in Steps 2 and 3),
  • BD is a side of both triangles.

So triangles BPD and BQD have the same three sides. They are identical, so their angles at B are equal: \angle PBD=\angle QBD.

Think What goes wrong if the compass slips wider between Step 2 and Step 3?
Then PD\ne QD. The crossing moves toward one arm, so the ray is not in the middle: one “half” is bigger than the other.
Example 3

Each half, or the whole angle

Each angle is bisected. Complete the table.
(a)(b)(c)(d)(e)(f)
Whole angle90°128°75°180°68°163°
Each half45°64°37.5°90°34°81.5°
(a) 90\div2=45 · (b) 128\div2=64 · (c) 75\div2=37.5 · (d) 180\div2=90: half a straight angle is a right angle. · (e) 34\times2=68 · (f) 81.5\times2=163
Halves can be decimals: 75 ÷ 2 ENTER shows 37.5. Going back: 81.5 × 2 ENTER shows 163.

Halves and wholes: watch the angle split (and join)

Example 4

Bisect, measure, fold

(a) Bisect \angle JKL with a compass and straightedge.
(b) Measure the two halves with a protractor. What should each one be?
\angle JKL=70^\circ, so each half is 70\div2=35^\circ. Both measure 35° ✓
(c) How could you check your bisector by folding?
Trace the angle on thin paper. Fold through K so arm KJ lies on arm KL. The crease should lie right on the bisector.
Math Historyc. 300 BCE – 1837

Halves are easy. Thirds are impossible.

About 2300 years ago, the Greek mathematician Euclid wrote down today’s steps for bisecting an angle with only a compass and a straightedge.

Then people tried to cut any angle into three equal parts the same way. For over 2000 years, nobody could do it.

In 1837, a 23-year-old French student, Pierre Wantzel, proved it can’t be done. Some angles work, but most don’t: not even 60°.

A 90° angle can be cut into three equal parts with a compass. How big is each part?
90^\circ\div3=30^\circ. (A 30° angle is easy to make with a compass: bisect the 60° corner of a triangle with three equal sides.)

Perpendicular Lines and the Perpendicular Bisector

Two lines are perpendicular if they meet at a right angle (90°). We mark the right angle with a small square, and write AB\perp CD (“AB is perpendicular to CD”). When two lines are perpendicular, all four angles where they cross are 90°.

A straight angle is 180°, and 180^\circ\div2=90^\circ. So drawing a perpendicular line is the same as bisecting a straight angle. The midpoint of a segment cuts it into two equal parts. The perpendicular bisector of a segment goes through its midpoint at 90°.

Perpendicular through a point P on a line
  1. Compass point on P. Draw arcs that cross the line on both sides, at X and Y.
  2. Open the compass wider. Point on X: draw an arc above P.
  3. Same width, point on Y: draw an arc that crosses it at Q.
  4. Draw line PQ. It is perpendicular to the first line.
Perpendicular bisector of segment AB
  1. Open the compass to more than half of AB. Point on A: draw arcs above and below AB.
  2. Same width, point on B: draw arcs that cross the first two at C and D.
  3. Draw line CD. It crosses AB at its midpoint M, at 90°. So AM=MB.
⚒
On the job. A carpenter’s (framing) square has a perfect 90° corner. Hold one edge along a board and draw along the other edge: the line is perpendicular to the board’s edge. You can also fold a paper line onto itself: the crease is perpendicular to the line.
Think Every point on the perpendicular bisector of AB is the same distance from A and from B. Where could that be useful?
Anywhere you want a spot that is fair to two places: a mailbox or a bus stop the same distance from two houses, or a hook centred between two nails.
Example 5

Construct perpendiculars

(a) Construct the perpendicular bisector of segment AB. Label the midpoint M. Measure AM and MB.
AB=6 cm, so AM=MB=3 cm, and the angle at M is 90°.
(b) Construct a line through P that is perpendicular to line n.
The angle between the new line and n measures 90°.

Perpendicular to a flat surface

A post, a pole or a table leg meets a flat surface (a plane), not just a line. It is perpendicular to the surface only if it makes 90° with every line on the surface through its foot. In practice, a builder checks with a square or a level in two directions: front-to-back and side-to-side.

Example 6

Mateo’s table leg

Mateo is building a table. He checks one leg and finds that it makes an angle of 92.5° with the underside of the tabletop.

(a) Is the leg perpendicular to the tabletop? Explain.
No. Perpendicular means exactly 90°, and 92.5° is not 90°.
(b) By how many degrees must he move the leg?
92.5^\circ-90^\circ=2.5^\circ. He must move it 2.5°.
(c) The leg and the tabletop make two angles side by side, on a straight line. What is the other angle?
A straight angle is 180°. 180^\circ-92.5^\circ=87.5^\circ
(d) The leg is now 90° from front to back. Why should Mateo also check it from side to side?
The tabletop is a flat surface. The leg could still lean sideways. It must be 90° in both directions.
Estimate first: 92.5 is a bit more than 90, so (b) is small and (c) is a bit less than 90. Then 180 − 92.5 ENTER shows 87.5.
4

Parallel, Perpendicular or Neither?

Parallel lines lie on the same flat surface and never meet. They stay the same distance apart, like the two rails of a railway track. We mark parallel lines with matching arrows (>) and write AB\parallel CD.

Key idea   Sorting two lines
  1. Do the lines meet, or would they meet if you made them longer? If no, they are parallel. (Check: the distance between them, measured straight across, is the same in two places.)
  2. If yes, measure the angle where they meet. 90° → perpendicular. Any other angle → neither.
!
Watch out. A line goes on forever. Two lines that don’t touch on the page may still meet if you extend them with a ruler. Then they are not parallel.
Think Are the two long edges of a ruler parallel? How could you check?
Yes. Measure the width of the ruler straight across in two places, near each end. If both widths are the same, the edges are parallel.
Example 7

Sort the lines

Use a ruler and protractor. Sort each pair of lines as parallel, perpendicular or neither, and give a reason.

On the Job: Mitre Cuts and Braces

A mitre joint joins two boards at a corner, like the corner of a picture frame. Each board end is cut on an angle. For a tight corner, the joint line must bisect the corner angle, so each cut is half the corner angle.

Key idea   Mitre cut angle = corner angle \div 2
Square or rectangular frame: 90^\circ\div2=45^\circ.
For a frame shaped like a regular polygon with n sides, find one corner first (Lesson 3): corner =(n-2)\times180^\circ\div n.
⚒
On the job. A mitre saw’s scale shows how far the blade is turned away from a square (90°) cut. So the saw setting =90^\circ- the cut angle. For a 45° cut, the setting is also 45°.

A brace is a board nailed across a corner to keep a gate or shelf from sagging. On a square gate, a brace from corner to corner bisects the 90° corners.

Think A frame with very many sides looks almost like a circle. What happens to the cut angle and the saw setting as the number of sides grows?
The corners get closer to 180°, so the cut gets closer to 180^\circ\div2=90^\circ (almost a square cut) and the saw setting gets closer to 0°.
Example 8

Frames and a gate

(a) Ava is making a rectangular picture frame. At what angle does she cut the end of each piece?
Corner =90^\circ. 90^\circ\div2=45^\circ for each cut.
(b) Jonah is making a frame for a mirror shaped like a regular hexagon (6 sides). Find the corner angle, the cut angle, and the mitre saw setting.
Angle sum =(6-2)\times180^\circ=720^\circ. Corner =720^\circ\div6=120^\circ. Cut =120^\circ\div2=60^\circ. Saw setting =90^\circ-60^\circ=30^\circ.
(c) A square garden gate has a brace from the bottom hinge corner to the top latch corner. What angle does the brace make with the top rail? Why?
The brace goes corner to corner of a square, so it bisects the 90° corner. 90^\circ\div2=45^\circ. The brace makes 45° with the top rail.
One corner of a regular hexagon in one go: ( 6 − 2 ) × 180 ÷ 6 ENTER shows 120.
Extra Example

A corner that isn’t square

Real walls are rarely perfect. Priya is putting baseboard in an inside corner. She copies the corner with a sliding T-bevel and measures it with a protractor: 94°.

(a) At what angle should she cut the end of each baseboard?
The joint bisects the corner: 94^\circ\div2=47^\circ.
(b) What is the mitre saw setting?
90^\circ-47^\circ=43^\circ
(c) Why not just cut 45° like a square corner?
Two 45° cuts make a 90° corner, but the wall is 94°. A gap would open at the joint.
✓

Key ideas

Copy: protractor (measure, then draw the same degrees), T-bevel, tracing or Mira, or compass: arc at B, the same arc at E, then the width QP from R. Same size = congruent.
Bisect: each half = angle ÷ 2; whole = half × 2. Fold arm onto arm, use a Mira, or compass: arc at the vertex, then two equal arcs from the arms. Ray from the vertex through the crossing.
Perpendicular: meet at 90° (\perp, a small square). The perpendicular bisector goes through the midpoint at 90°. A post must be 90° in two directions.
Sort lines: never meet → parallel (\parallel, same gap); meet at 90° → perpendicular; any other angle → neither. Extend lines first! Mitre cut = corner ÷ 2; saw setting = 90° − cut.
