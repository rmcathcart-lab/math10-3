The Coin That Rolls Around a Coin

Put two loonies flat on a desk, touching. Hold one still.

Roll the other loonie all the way around the edge of the still one, with no slipping, until it is back on top.

How many full turns does the rolling loonie make?

A 1 turn
B 1\tfrac12 turns
C 2 turns
D 3 turns
C: 2 full turns! Most people say 1. The coin turns once from rolling along the edge, and once more because it travels all the way around the other coin. That is 2\times360^\circ=720^\circ. Watch the red arrow: at the bottom it already points up again. Grab two coins and try it!

Angle Partners: Complementary, Supplementary and Opposite Angles

Angles that sit next to each other, angles that add to 90^\circ or 180^\circ, the equal angles made by two crossing lines, and finding missing angles one step at a time, with a reason for each step.

I can…
  • tell when two angles are adjacent, and add adjacent angles
  • find the complement (90^\circ- the angle) and the supplement (180^\circ- the angle) of an angle
  • decide if two angles are complementary, supplementary or neither, and find a missing angle in a corner or on a straight line
  • use vertically opposite angles (equal) and angles around a point (360^\circ)
  • find missing angles one at a time and give a reason for each
G5 Solve problems that involve parallel, perpendicular and transversal lines, and pairs of angles formed between them.
1

Adjacent Angles

An angle has two arms that meet at a point called the vertex. You can name an angle three ways:

  • with three letters, the vertex in the middle: \angle PQR
  • with the vertex letter alone: \angle Q
  • with a small number or letter inside the corner: \angle 1 or \angle a
Key idea   Two angles are adjacent (“next to each other”) when they
  • have the same vertex,
  • share one arm, and
  • do not overlap (one angle is not inside the other).
Adjacent angles add up. If \angle PQS and \angle SQR are adjacent, then \angle PQS+\angle SQR=\angle PQR (the big angle made by the two outside arms).
Think Can two adjacent angles both be obtuse? Sketch it.
Yes. For example 100^\circ and 120^\circ side by side, sharing one arm. Together they make 220^\circ, more than a straight line, but they are still adjacent.
Example 1

Adjacent or not?

In each picture, are \angle 1 and \angle 2 adjacent? Write yes or no, and give a reason.

(a)
Yes. Same vertex Q, they share arm QS, and they do not overlap.
(b)
No. Same vertex, but they share no arm.
(c)
No. They share an arm, but the vertices are different.
(d)
No. Same vertex and a shared arm, but \angle 1 is inside \angle 2 (they overlap).
(e) In picture (a), \angle PQS=35^\circ and \angle SQR=50^\circ. Find \angle PQR.
The angles are adjacent, so they add: \angle PQR=35^\circ+50^\circ=85^\circ.
2

Complementary and Supplementary Angles

Two angles are complementary if they add up to 90^\circ. Each angle is the complement of the other.
Two angles are supplementary if they add up to 180^\circ. Each angle is the supplement of the other.

Key idea
Complement =90^\circ-\text{the angle}Supplement =180^\circ-\text{the angle}
Only an acute angle (less than 90^\circ) has a complement. Only an angle less than 180^\circ has a supplement.
i
Remember. Complementary goes with a Corner (90^\circ). Supplementary goes with a Straight line (180^\circ). The two angles do not have to be next to each other: a 30^\circ angle in one corner of the page and a 60^\circ angle in another corner are still complementary.

Partners don’t have to touch

Two angles far apart can still be complementary or supplementary. Slide one over to the other and see.

Think What are the complement and the supplement of a tiny 1^\circ angle?
Complement 90^\circ-1^\circ=89^\circ, supplement 180^\circ-1^\circ=179^\circ. The supplement is always 90^\circ more than the complement.

Finding the complement and the supplement

The complement is what fills up the corner. The supplement is what fills up the straight line.

Example 3

Work backwards, and partners on the job

(a) The complement of an angle is 31^\circ. What is the angle? What is its supplement?
Angle: 90^\circ-31^\circ=59^\circ
Supplement: 180^\circ-59^\circ=121^\circ
(b) The supplement of an angle is 142^\circ. What is the angle? What is its complement?
Angle: 180^\circ-142^\circ=38^\circ
Complement: 90^\circ-38^\circ=52^\circ
(c) A straight bike path ends at a straight sidewalk. The angle on one side of the path is 73^\circ. What is the angle x on the other side?
The sidewalk is a straight line, so the angles are supplementary.
x=180^\circ-73^\circ=107^\circ
(d) A flagpole holder is screwed to a straight-up (vertical) wall. The pole makes a 34^\circ angle with a level line out from the wall. What angle y does the pole make with the wall above it?
The wall and the level line make a right angle, so the angles are complementary.
y=90^\circ-34^\circ=56^\circ

Partners on the job

(a) A wheelchair ramp makes a 12^\circ angle with the flat ground. What angle does it make with a straight-up post?
The ground and the post make a right angle: complementary. 90^\circ-12^\circ=78^\circ
(b) A door on a straight wall is opened 65^\circ. What angle does the door make with the wall on its other side?
The wall is a straight line: supplementary. 180^\circ-65^\circ=115^\circ
(c) A ladder leans on a straight-up wall. It makes 70^\circ with the flat floor. What angle does it make with the wall?
The wall and the floor make a right angle: complementary. 90^\circ-70^\circ=20^\circ

Complementary, Supplementary or Neither?

Adjacent angles are not always complementary or supplementary. Add them to find out:

Key idea
  • Sum is 90^\circ \rightarrow complementary (the outside arms make a square corner).
  • Sum is 180^\circ \rightarrow supplementary (the outside arms make a straight line).
  • Any other sum \rightarrow neither.
Example 4

Find the sum, then decide

Each picture shows two adjacent angles. Find the sum. Then write complementary, supplementary or neither.

(a)
Sum: 90^\circ
complementary
42+48=90^\circ: complementary
(b)
Sum: 180^\circ
supplementary
125+55=180^\circ: supplementary
(c)
Sum: 105^\circ
neither
35+70=105^\circ: neither
(d)
Sum: 160^\circ
neither
100+60=160^\circ: neither

(c) and (d) are adjacent (same vertex, a shared arm, no overlap), but their sums are not 90^\circ or 180^\circ.

(e) Can two angles be supplementary if they are not adjacent? Give an example.
Yes. Supplementary only means the sum is 180^\circ. For example, a 130^\circ angle on one side of a room and a 50^\circ angle on the other side are supplementary.

Spot the mistake: right or wrong?

(a) “The complement of 40^\circ is 140^\circ.”
Wrong. 140^\circ is the supplement. The complement is 90^\circ-40^\circ=50^\circ.
(b) “A 95^\circ angle has a supplement of 85^\circ.”
Right. 95^\circ+85^\circ=180^\circ.
(c) “A 200^\circ reflex angle and a 160^\circ angle make a full turn.”
Right. 200^\circ+160^\circ=360^\circ.

Finding a missing angle in a corner or on a straight line

Key idea
  • Step 1: Decide: is it a right angle (90^\circ), a straight line (180^\circ), or neither?
  • Step 2: Write an equation: (missing angle) + (the angles you know) =90^\circ or 180^\circ.
  • Step 3: Subtract to find the missing angle.
  • Step 4: Check by adding.
!
Watch out. Don’t guess from how a picture looks. A corner is 90^\circ only if it has the small square mark (or the question says so).
Example 5

Find the missing angle

Find each missing angle x. Write the equation first.

(a)
Right angle:
x+33^\circ=90^\circ
x=90^\circ-33^\circ=57^\circ
Check: 57+33=90\ \checkmark
(b)
Straight line:
x+141^\circ=180^\circ
x=180^\circ-141^\circ=39^\circ
Check: 39+141=180\ \checkmark
(c)
Straight line:
46^\circ+x+58^\circ=180^\circ
x=180^\circ-46^\circ-58^\circ=76^\circ
Check: 46+76+58=180\ \checkmark
Estimate first: in (c), 46+58 is about 100, so x is about 80^\circ. Then subtract both angles in one go: 180 − 46 − 58 ENTER shows 76.
Think In (c), why must you subtract both known angles from 180^\circ?
All three angles together make the straight line. So x is what is left of 180^\circ after the 46^\circ and the 58^\circ.

Vertically Opposite Angles and Angles Around a Point

When two straight lines cross, they make four angles. The two angles across from each other are called vertically opposite angles.

(“Vertically” means they share a vertex. It does not mean up and down.)

Key idea   Vertically opposite angles are always equal: \angle 1=\angle 3 and \angle 2=\angle 4.
Think Two lines cross, and one of the four angles is 90^\circ. What are the other three?
All 90^\circ: the angle across is equal (90^\circ), and the two beside it are 180^\circ-90^\circ=90^\circ. Lines that cross at right angles are perpendicular.

Why are vertically opposite angles equal?

\angle 1+\angle 2=180^\circ (they make a straight line)
\angle 3+\angle 2=180^\circ (they also make a straight line)
So \angle 1 and \angle 3 are both “180^\circ minus \angle 2”. That means \angle 1=\angle 3. In the same way, \angle 2=\angle 4.

Angles around a point

A full turn is 360^\circ, so all the angles around a point add up to 360^\circ. Each angle needs to be next to the one before it, with no gaps and no overlaps.

Key idea   The four angle facts (use these words as your reasons)
Complementary anglesadd up to 90^\circ (a right angle)
Supplementary angles (angles on a straight line)add up to 180^\circ
Vertically opposite anglesare equal
Angles around a pointadd up to 360^\circ
Example 6

Measure the four angles

(a) Measure each angle with a protractor.
\angle a= 36^\circ\angle b= 144^\circ\angle c= 36^\circ\angle d= 144^\circ
\angle a=36^\circ,\ \angle b=144^\circ,\ \angle c=36^\circ,\ \angle d=144^\circ
(b) Which angles are equal? Why?
\angle a=\angle c and \angle b=\angle d: vertically opposite angles
(c) Add all four. What do you get? Why?
36+144+36+144=360^\circ: angles around a point
(d) Suppose you only knew \angle a=36^\circ. How could you find the other three without a protractor?
\angle c=36^\circ (vertically opposite \angle a)
\angle b=180^\circ-36^\circ=144^\circ (supplementary: angles on a straight line)
\angle d=144^\circ (vertically opposite \angle b)
Example 7

All the way around

(a) Find x.
Angles around a point:
95^\circ+110^\circ+78^\circ+x=360^\circ
283^\circ+x=360^\circ
x=77^\circ
(b) A ceiling fan has 5 blades, evenly spaced. What angle is between two blades that are next to each other?
The 5 equal angles go all the way around:
360^\circ\div5=72^\circ
There are 72^\circ between blades.
(c) \angle AOB=130^\circ. The reflex angle AOB goes the long way around. How big is it?
The two angles go all the way around:
360^\circ-130^\circ=230^\circ
The reflex angle is 230^\circ.
In (a), take all three angles away from 360: 360 − 95 − 110 − 78 ENTER shows 77. In (b): 360 ÷ 5 ENTER shows 72.

Finding Missing Angles with Reasons

In a bigger diagram, you find the angles one at a time. Each new angle you find helps you find the next one.

Key idea
  • Step 1: Look for a missing angle that is next to or across from an angle you know.
  • Step 2: Find it, and write the reason (one of the four angle facts).
  • Step 3: Repeat with the new angle.
  • Check: the angles around the point add up to 360^\circ.
Example 8

Three lines through one point

Three straight lines cross at one point. Find angles a, b, c and d. Give a reason for each.

AngleWorkReason
a180-48-74=58^\circangles on a straight line
b48^\circvertically opposite the 48^\circ
c74^\circvertically opposite the 74^\circ
d58^\circvertically opposite a
a=58^\circ,\ b=48^\circ,\ c=74^\circ,\ d=58^\circ. Check: 48+74+58+48+74+58=360^\circ\ \checkmark (angles around a point)
Think Could you find b and c before a? How?
Yes. b is across from the 48^\circ and c is across from the 74^\circ (vertically opposite). Any order works, as long as each step uses an angle you already know.
Example 9

Scissors and a pool table

(a) A pair of scissors is open. The angle between the blades is 26^\circ. What is the angle between the handles? Why?
The blades and the handles are vertically opposite angles, so they are equal.
The handles are open 26^\circ.
(b) In a pool game, a ball hits the straight cushion (edge) of the table and bounces off. It comes in at 40^\circ to the cushion and leaves at 40^\circ to the cushion. Find the angle p between its two paths.
The cushion is a straight line (angles on a straight line):
40^\circ+p+40^\circ=180^\circ
p=180^\circ-80^\circ=100^\circ
More examples

Crossing train tracks

Two straight train tracks cross. One of the angles between them is 38^\circ. Find the other three angles. Give a reason for each.
Across from the 38^\circ: 38^\circ (vertically opposite). The two beside it: 180^\circ-38^\circ=142^\circ each (angles on a straight line). Check: 38+142+38+142=360\ \checkmark
More examples

Around the clock

(a) A clock shows 2:00. The 12 numbers are evenly spaced. What is the angle between the hands? What is the reflex angle?
Each hour mark is 360^\circ\div12=30^\circ apart. From 12 to 2 is 2 marks: 2\times30^\circ=60^\circ. Reflex angle: 360^\circ-60^\circ=300^\circ.
(b) A pizza is cut into 8 equal slices from the centre. One slice is eaten. What angle is the gap? What angle do the slices that are left make?
One slice: 360^\circ\div8=45^\circ. The slices that are left: 360^\circ-45^\circ=315^\circ (or 7\times45^\circ=315^\circ).
✓

Key ideas

Adjacent: same vertex, one shared arm, no overlap. Adjacent angles add: \angle PQS+\angle SQR=\angle PQR.
Partners: complementary add to 90^\circ (a Corner), supplementary add to 180^\circ (a Straight line). Complement =90^\circ- angle, supplement =180^\circ- angle.
Crossing lines: vertically opposite angles are equal. Angles around a point add to 360^\circ. A reflex angle =360^\circ- the angle.
Missing angles: right angle, straight line or around a point? Write the equation, subtract, check by adding. Find angles one at a time, with a reason for each.
