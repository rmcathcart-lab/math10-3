Nine dots make a square: 3 dots by 3 dots.

Draw 4 straight lines that go through all 9 dots. Your pencil may never leave the paper, and you can’t go over a line twice.

Take-Away Games: Work Backward

A strategy is a plan for solving a puzzle or winning a game. Good players do not just hope. They use problem-solving strategies like the ones in this toolbox. You will use all of them in this lesson.

Key idea   Strategy toolbox
1Guess and check: try something, see what happens, then adjust.
2Look for a pattern: solve small cases and see what repeats.
3Make a systematic list: list every possibility in an order, so none is missed or counted twice.
4Draw or model: use a sketch, coins, counters or a real board.
5Eliminate possibilities: cross out what cannot work.
6Simplify the problem: try a smaller version first.
7Work backward: start at the end (the win) and go back one step at a time.
8Try another approach: if one way gets stuck, use a different strategy.
The take-away game. Put a pile of counters (coins, paper clips, beans) on the table. Two players take turns. On your turn you must take 1, 2 or 3 counters. The player who takes the last counter wins.

Key idea   Winning strategy (take 1, 2 or 3; last counter wins): always leave a multiple of 4 (4, 8, 12, 16, 20, …) for the other player.
How many to take: count down from the pile to the next multiple of 4 below it. (21 counters: the next multiple of 4 below is 20, so take 1.)
If the pile is already a multiple of 4 on your turn, you cannot force a win. Take 1 and hope the other player makes a mistake.

Why do the losing numbers repeat every 4?

Put every pile size on a number line. A move is a hop down of 1, 2 or 3. A good player always hops onto an L.

Think You and a friend play with 100 counters (take 1, 2 or 3, last counter wins). Should you go first or second?
100 is a multiple of 4 (4\times25), so the first player is stuck. Go second, and always leave 96, 92, 88, …

Example 3

Change a rule, and fix a strategy

(a) Variation 1: you may take 1, 2, 3 or 4 counters. Last counter still wins. Which numbers should you leave now? How many should you take first from a pile of 23?
With 5 counters on your turn you lose: you leave 1, 2, 3 or 4, and the other player takes them all. So leave multiples of 5 (5, 10, 15, 20, …). From 23, the next multiple of 5 below is 20. Take 3.
(b) Variation 2: take 1, 2 or 3, but the player who takes the last counter loses. Work backward. Which numbers should you leave? How many should you take first from 15?
With 1 counter on your turn you lose (you must take it). So leave 1. From 2, 3 or 4 you can leave 1. From 5 you cannot. So leave 1, 5, 9, 13, … (one more than a multiple of 4). From 15, leave 13: take 2.
(c) Jordan says, “In the 1, 2 or 3 game (last counter wins), always take 3. It makes the pile go down fastest.” Show that Jordan’s strategy can lose when there are 6 counters on his turn. What should he do instead?
With 6 counters, Jordan takes 3 and leaves 3. The other player takes all 3 and wins. Correct strategy: take enough to leave a multiple of 4. From 6, take 2 and leave 4. Then Jordan wins.

Tic-Tac-Toe: Win, Block, Fork

Two players take turns on a 3\times3 grid. X goes first. Each player puts a mark in an empty square. Three of your marks in a row (across, up and down, or diagonally) wins. If the grid fills up with no three in a row, the game is a tie (a draw). In this lesson the squares are numbered 1 to 9, like this:

A fork is a move that makes two threats (two in a row with the third square empty) at the same time. The other player can block only one of them, so you win next turn.

Key idea   Tic-tac-toe checklist (check every empty square, in this order, before you move):
  1. Win. If you have two in a row and the third square is empty, take it.
  2. Block. If the other player has two in a row and the third square is empty, take it.
  3. Fork. If one move gives you two threats at once, take it.
  4. Otherwise take the centre; if it is taken, take a corner. (One exception: if you are O and X holds two opposite corners, take an edge, or X can fork.)
Example 4

Use the checklist

Use the checklist. Where should the player whose turn it is play? Give the square number and the reason.

(a) X to play.
Square 6. Step 1: X has 4 and 5, so 6 makes three in a row across the middle. X wins.
(b) O to play.
Square 2. O cannot win yet. Step 2: X has 1 and 3, so O must block the top row at 2.
(c) X to play.
Square 7. X cannot win yet, and O has 3 and 5, so X must block at 7. This block is also a fork: X now threatens 4 (1-4-7) and 8 (7-8-9). O can stop only one.
Example 5

Why good players always tie

Mateo (X) plays the centre. Sofia (O) answers on an edge, square 2. Mateo plays 1.

(a) Why must Sofia play 9 now?
Mateo has 1 and 5. He threatens to win at 9 (the diagonal 1-5-9), so she must block.
(b) Mateo then plays 7. Show that he will win on his next turn.
Mateo has 1, 5, 7. He threatens 4 (1-4-7) and 3 (3-5-7): a fork. Sofia has no threat of her own and can block only one square, so Mateo wins with the other.
(c) Sofia’s first move was the mistake. People have checked every possible game (a systematic list): after X takes the centre, O must answer in a corner. Then, if both players use the checklist, the game always ends in a tie. Priya says, “If I go first and take the centre, I will always win.” Correct her statement.
Priya will win only if the other player makes a mistake (like Sofia’s edge move). If O answers the centre with a corner and then always blocks, nobody can win: the game is a tie. A good player never loses at tic-tac-toe, but cannot force a win against another good player.

Play the computer: tic-tac-toe

Tap a square to play. Hint shows what the checklist says. The Checklist computer follows the four steps exactly. The Perfect computer has checked every possible game. Can you ever beat it?

Think Play X against the Checklist computer. Start in corner 1, then take the opposite corner, 9. What happens? Is the checklist perfect?
The computer takes the centre, then (step 4) a corner, and you block its threat with a fork: you win! After X 1, O 5, X 9, the safe move for O is an edge. The checklist is a great start, but it isn’t perfect.

Dots and Boxes: Don’t Draw the Third Side

Start with a grid of dots. Players take turns drawing one short line that joins two dots next to each other (across or up and down). If your line finishes the fourth side of a box, write your initial in the box and take another turn. When every line is drawn, the player with more boxes wins.

Key idea   Dots and boxes strategy
  1. If a box has 3 sides, take it (draw the fourth side).
  2. Never draw the third side of a box if you can help it: the other player will take that box and move again.
  3. Near the end, every line may give boxes away. Then give away the smallest group of boxes.
Example 6

Take a box, then play safe

It is your turn. The boxes are named A to I. The dashed lines q, r and s have not been drawn yet.

(a) Which box can you take right now? Which line do you draw?
Box E has 3 sides (top, bottom, left). Draw its right side, the line between E and F. You take E and get another turn.
(b) Now you must draw one more line. For each of q, r and s, say whether it is safe or which boxes it gives away.
q: box A would have 3 sides, so it gives away A. r: box C would have only 2 sides. Safe. s: after (a), F and I each have 2 sides, so s gives away F and I. Draw r.
(c) Leo says, “I like to draw a side on a box that already has two sides. Then I’m closer to finishing it.” What is wrong with Leo’s strategy?
His line is the box’s third side. The other player draws the fourth side and takes the box, then gets another turn. Leo should draw sides on boxes with 0 or 1 sides instead.
Think How many lines are drawn in a whole game on this grid (4 dots by 4 dots)? How did you count them?
24: 4 rows of 3 lines across (12) and 4 columns of 3 lines up and down (12). A systematic list, row by row.

Play the computer: dots and boxes

Tap between two dots to draw a line. Your lines are blue, the computer’s are red. Turn on Count the sides to see how many sides each box has, so you never draw a third side.

The Tower Puzzle: Simplify and Look for a Pattern

The tower puzzle has three pegs, A, B and C, and some discs of different sizes stacked on peg A, biggest at the bottom. The goal is to move the whole tower to peg C.

Rules
  1. Move one disc at a time: the top disc of a peg.
  2. Never put a disc on top of a smaller disc.
Model it with coins: a toonie, a loonie, a quarter, a nickel and a dime are five different sizes (biggest to smallest).

Try it: the tower puzzle

Tap a peg to pick up its top disc, then tap the peg to put it on. Start with 1 disc, then 2, then 3: simplify the problem. Count your moves. Solve it for me shows the fewest moves.

Each new number is double the one before, plus 1: 2\times15+1=31, 2\times31+1=63. Also, each number is one less than a power of 2: 2-1,\ 4-1,\ 8-1,\ 16-1,\ 32-1,\ 64-1.
Key idea   Tower puzzle:   fewest moves for n discs =2\times(\text{moves for }n-1\text{ discs})+1\ =\ 2^n-1
Why: move the smaller discs out of the way, move the biggest disc once, then move the smaller discs back on top of it.
For 8 discs: 2 ^ 8 − 1 ENTER shows 255.
Think When can the biggest disc move? Why does it move only once in the fewest moves?
Only when every other disc is stacked on the third peg (nothing on top of it, nothing on its target). Moving it again would mean moving all the others out of the way again: wasted moves.
Example 8

Big towers, a wrong pattern, and a new rule

(a) A science centre has a tower puzzle with 8 discs. A visitor makes 1 move every second and never makes a mistake. How long does the puzzle take? Give minutes and seconds.
2^8-1=256-1=255 moves, so 255 seconds. 255\div60=4 remainder 15 (4\times60=240). It takes 4 min 15 s.
(b) Kai says, “1 disc takes 1 move and 2 discs take 3 moves, so each disc adds 2 moves. 5 discs take 9 moves.” Find his error and correct it.
Kai looked at only two cases. With 3 discs the count is 7, not 5, so “add 2” is wrong. The moves double and add 1: 1, 3, 7, 15, 31. 5 discs take 31 moves.
(c) Variation: a disc may move only to a peg next to its peg (A to B, B to C, C to B, B to A, but never straight between A and C). Model the puzzle. How many moves do 1 disc and 2 discs take? Does the rule 2^n-1 still work?
1 disc: A→B, B→C: 2 moves. 2 discs: S A→B, S B→C, L A→B, S C→B, S B→A, L B→C, S A→B, S B→C: 8 moves. No. 2^1-1=1 and 2^2-1=3, but the counts are 2 and 8. Changing a rule changes the pattern.
Math History1883

A tower to end the world

In 1883, the French mathematician Édouard Lucas sold a new puzzle in Paris: our tower puzzle. He used a made-up name, “N. Claus de Siam”. Its letters are a scramble of “Lucas d’Amiens”.

The puzzle came with a legend. The story goes that in a temple, priests move a tower of 64 gold discs, one disc at a time, never a bigger disc on a smaller one. When they finish, the world will end.

Should we worry? 64 discs take 2^{64}-1 moves (the picture counts them). At 1 move per second, about how many years is that? A year is about 31.6 million seconds.
2^{64}-1=18\,446\,744\,073\,709\,551\,615 seconds \div\ 31\,600\,000\approx584\,000\,000\,000: about 584 billion years, about 40 times as long as the universe has existed. We’re safe!

A Hidden-Ships Grid Game: Eliminate Possibilities

Each player has a secret 6\times6 grid, with columns A to F across and rows 1 to 6 up. A square is named by its column, then its row, like a map: C4. Each player hides a 2-ship (2 squares in a line) and a 3-ship (3 squares in a line), across or up and down, never diagonal. Players take turns calling a square. The other player answers “hit” or “miss”, and “sunk” when every square of a ship has been hit. The first player to sink all the other player’s ships wins.

Key idea   Hidden-ships strategy
  • Hunt: call only the squares of one colour in a checkerboard pattern. Two squares side by side always have different colours, so every ship covers at least one shaded square. You need at most half the squares (18 of the 36).
  • Target: after a hit, call the squares next to it (up, down, left, right). After two hits in a line, keep going along that line.
  • Eliminate: cross off any position that would use a square you know is a miss.
Think In how many different places can a 2-ship hide on the 6\times6 grid?
Across: 5 places in each of the 6 rows, 30. Up and down: 5 in each of the 6 columns, 30. In all, 60 places. That is why a good hunting plan matters!

Why does the checkerboard always find every ship?

Example 9

Where is the 3-ship?

X = hit, ○ = miss. The 2-ship at E5–E6 is sunk.

You hit the 3-ship at C3. Your next two calls, C4 and D3, were misses.

(a) Make a systematic list of every place a 3-ship that covers C3 could be: 3 across, then 3 up and down. Then cross out the ones that use a miss.
Across: A3–C3, B3–D3 (D3 miss), C3–E3 (D3 miss). Up and down: C1–C3, C2–C4 (C4 miss), C3–C5 (C4 miss). Only A3–C3 and C1–C3 are left.
(b) Which square should you call next? What will each answer tell you?
Call B3 (or C2). If B3 is a hit, the ship is A3–C3; call A3 to sink it. If B3 is a miss, the ship must be C1–C3; call C2 and C1.
(c) Mia says, “The ship could still be C3, D3 and E3.” What is her error?
D3 was a miss, so no ship can cover D3. Any position that uses D3 is eliminated.

Key ideas

Take-away (1, 2 or 3; last wins): work backward from small piles. Leave a multiple of 4. Change a rule and the numbers change: take 1 to 4 → leave multiples of 5; last loses → leave 1, 5, 9, 13, …
Tic-tac-toe: check in order: win, block, fork, then centre, then a corner. A fork makes two threats at once. Two good players always tie.
Dots and boxes: take any box with 3 sides. Never draw a box’s third side. If you must give boxes away, give the smallest group.
Tower puzzle: simplify and look for a pattern: 1, 3, 7, 15, … double and add 1, so 2^n-1. Hidden ships: hunt on a checkerboard, target next to a hit, eliminate positions that use a miss.
