The Leaning Book Tower

You have a pile of identical books. You stack them at the edge of a table, one book on each layer. Each book can stick out past the one under it.

No glue, no tape, nothing holding them.

Can the top book stick out so that none of it is over the table? What is the fewest books you need?

A It can’t be done
B 3 books
C 4 books
D 10 books
C: 4 books, just barely! Build it from the top down. The top book can stick out half a book past the one under it. The next one out a quarter, then a sixth, then an eighth past the table. \tfrac12+\tfrac14+\tfrac16+\tfrac18=\tfrac{25}{24}: a little more than one whole book. With 3 books you only get \tfrac{11}{12} of a book. Try it with 4 (or 5) math textbooks!

Transversals: Naming the Angle Pairs

When one line crosses two other lines, it makes eight angles. Today we number them, learn the names of the angle pairs (they make the letters F, Z and C), find them in real pictures like ladders, rails and streets, and see why the lines must be parallel before the pairs are equal.

I can…
  • tell whether a line is a transversal
  • number the eight angles, and say which are interior or exterior, and on the left or right of the transversal
  • name corresponding (F), alternate interior (Z), alternate exterior and same-side (C or U) angle pairs
  • find the transversal and name the pairs in real pictures and busy diagrams
  • explain why the pairs are equal only when the lines are parallel
G5 Solve problems that involve parallel, perpendicular and transversal lines, and pairs of angles formed between them.
1

Transversals and the Eight Angles

Transversal   A transversal is a line that crosses two (or more) other lines at two different points. In our diagrams, line t is the transversal, and \ell_1 and \ell_2 (say “line one” and “line two”) are the two lines it crosses. A line goes on forever: we only draw part of it.
Example 1

Transversal or not?

Is line t a transversal of lines m and n? Write yes or no, and give a reason.

(a)
Yes. t crosses m and n at two different points.
(b)
No. t goes through the point where m and n cross. It meets them at only one point.
(c)
No. t is parallel to n, so it never crosses n. It crosses only m.
(d)
Yes. t crosses both lines at two different points. (Parallel lines can have a transversal.)
Think Line t goes through the point where \ell_1 and \ell_2 cross, like in Example 1(b). How many angles do the three lines make there?
6 angles, all at one point (three lines make 6 “slices” around the point). A transversal makes 8 angles, 4 at each of its two crossings.

Numbering the eight angles

A transversal crossing two lines makes eight angles, four at each crossing.

Key idea   How we number the eight angles (every diagram in this unit)
  • At the top crossing, angles 1, 2, 3, 4 go clockwise, starting at the upper left.
  • At the bottom crossing, angles 5, 6, 7, 8 go clockwise, starting at the upper left.

Interior angles are between the two lines (the shaded band): \angle 3,\ \angle 4,\ \angle 5,\ \angle 6.
Exterior angles are outside the two lines: \angle 1,\ \angle 2,\ \angle 7,\ \angle 8.
Each angle is also on the left or the right side of the transversal.

i
Good to know. The two lines do not have to be parallel. Here \ell_1 and \ell_2 are not parallel, but the names in this lesson still work. When lines are parallel, we mark them with matching arrows (>), as in Example 1(c) and (d).

Corresponding and Vertically Opposite Angles

Corresponding angles are in the same position at the two crossings: both upper left, both upper right, both lower right or both lower left. They are on the same side of the transversal, and one is interior while the other is exterior.
They make an F shape. The F can be backwards, upside down or tilted.
In our numbering the four pairs are easy to remember: add 4.
\angle 1 and \angle 5,   \angle 2 and \angle 6,   \angle 3 and \angle 7,   \angle 4 and \angle 8.
\angle 3 and \angle 7 are corresponding.
Vertically opposite angles (Lesson 6) are across from each other at the same crossing, like the opposite parts of an X. They are always equal. The pairs are \angle 1 and \angle 3,  \angle 2 and \angle 4,  \angle 5 and \angle 7,  \angle 6 and \angle 8.
!
Watch out. Corresponding angles are at different crossings. Vertically opposite angles are at the same crossing. Two angles that sit next to each other at one crossing (like \angle 1 and \angle 2) make a straight line, so they are supplementary (they add to 180^\circ).

Why does “add 4” work?

Slide the whole top crossing down the transversal. Every angle lands on the angle in the same position at the bottom crossing.

Think Which angle corresponds to \angle 5? Why do you subtract 4 this time?
\angle 1. \angle 5 is at the bottom crossing, so its partner is at the top crossing, where the numbers are 4 smaller: 5-4=1.

3

Alternate Angles and Same-Side Angles

Alternate means “one on each side”. Alternate angles are at different crossings and on opposite sides of the transversal.

  • Alternate interior angles are both between the lines, on opposite sides of t. They make a Z shape.  Pairs: \angle 3 and \angle 5,  \angle 4 and \angle 6.
  • Alternate exterior angles are both outside the lines, on opposite sides of t.  Pairs: \angle 1 and \angle 7,  \angle 2 and \angle 8.

Two more pairs are at different crossings and on the same side of the transversal.

  • Interior angles on the same side of the transversal are both between the lines, on the same side of t. They make a C or U shape.  Pairs: \angle 3 and \angle 6,  \angle 4 and \angle 5.
  • Exterior angles on the same side of the transversal are both outside the lines, on the same side of t.  Pairs: \angle 1 and \angle 8,  \angle 2 and \angle 7.
alternate interior: \angle 4 and \angle 6
interior, same side: \angle 3 and \angle 6
Key idea   Naming a pair of angles: ask three questions.
1. Are the angles at the same crossing? If yes: across from each other → vertically opposite; next to each other → they make a straight line (180^\circ).
If they are at different crossings, ask:  2. Interior or exterior?   3. Same side of t or opposite sides?

Real Pictures and Busy Diagrams

Real pictures are turned and tilted, and the angles may be numbered or lettered in any order. Look at where each angle is, not at its number.

i
How to find the transversal. The transversal is the line that goes through the vertices of both angles. The other line at each vertex is one of “the two lines”. It can help to turn your page so the transversal goes up and down.

\angle p and \angle r: corresponding

Name each pair of angles.
(i) \angle a and \angle g: corresponding
(ii) \angle f and \angle g: alternate interior
(iii) \angle e and \angle h: alternate exterior

When the Lines Are Not Parallel

The names tell you where two angles are. They do not tell you that the angles are the same size. Measure to see what happens when the lines are parallel, and when they are not.

When the lines are parallel, corresponding angles are equal, alternate interior angles are equal, and interior angles on the same side add to 180^\circ. When the lines are not parallel, these pairs are not equal and the same-side pair does not add to 180^\circ.
A: 120^\circ,\ 120^\circ, yes; 60^\circ,\ 60^\circ; 180^\circ. B: 120^\circ,\ 105^\circ, no; 60^\circ,\ 75^\circ; 165^\circ. Equal, equal, 180^\circ, not parallel. (Measurements within 1^\circ or 2^\circ are fine.)
Key idea   The names work for any two lines. The sizes depend on whether the lines are parallel.
  • Always true: vertically opposite angles are equal; two angles next to each other on a straight line add to 180^\circ.
  • Only when the two lines are parallel (Lesson 8): corresponding angles, alternate interior angles and alternate exterior angles are equal; interior angles on the same side and exterior angles on the same side add to 180^\circ.
!
Watch out. Never use the parallel-line rules unless you know the lines are parallel: the question says so, or the lines have arrow marks. Lines that only look parallel might not be.

Math Historyc. 300 BCE · 1820s

The rule nobody could prove

About 300 BCE, the Greek mathematician Euclid wrote the Elements, the most famous geometry book ever. It starts with five rules he took as true. The fifth one is about today’s angles:

If the interior angles on the same side of a transversal add to less than 180^\circ, the two lines meet on that side.

For about 2000 years, people tried to prove it from the other four rules, and failed. In the 1820s, János Bolyai and Nikolai Lobachevsky found out why: there are other geometries where the rule is false.

Interior angles on the same side are 88^\circ and 89^\circ, on the right of t. Do the lines meet? On which side?
88^\circ+89^\circ=177^\circ, less than 180^\circ. Yes, they meet on the right side of t (far away).

Why are vertically opposite angles always equal?

They are at one crossing, so we only need straight lines. Nothing about the other crossing matters.

Think In Diagram A, \angle 1=\angle 5=120^\circ. Suppose instead \angle 1=120^\circ and \angle 5=121^\circ. Are the lines parallel?
No. Corresponding angles are only equal when the lines are parallel. Off by 1^\circ, the lines look parallel but meet far away.

Finding angles when the lines are not parallel

Only the always true rules work. They stay at one crossing.

✓

Key ideas

Transversal: a line that crosses two lines at two different points. It makes 8 angles: 1–4 at the top crossing and 5–8 at the bottom, each clockwise from the upper left. Interior = between the lines.
Same crossing: across = vertically opposite (always equal); next to each other = a straight line (always 180^\circ). Corresponding = same position (F): add 4.
Three questions: same crossing? interior or exterior? same side of t? Alternate interior (Z), alternate exterior, interior/exterior on the same side (C or U).
Real pictures: the transversal goes through both vertices. Not parallel? The names still work, but the pairs are not equal. Only use the parallel rules with arrows or when told.
