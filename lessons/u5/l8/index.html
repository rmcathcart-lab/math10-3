Which Cup Fills First?

Juice is poured into the top cup. It flows out through the tubes into the cups below.

The red plug blocks one tube. Nothing can get past it.

Which bottom cup fills up first: A, B, C or D?

A cup A
B cup B
C cup C
D cup D
Cup C! Follow the juice one step at a time. The top cup’s right tube is lower, so the juice leaves there first and runs into the right cup. That cup’s low tube is plugged, so the juice rises until it reaches the side tube, which leads to C. Cups A, B and D get nothing (unless you pour really fast). Parallel Lines and Transversals: Finding Missing Angles

When two lines are parallel, the angles a transversal makes follow rules. Today we use those rules to find every missing angle, give a reason for each step, and check whether two shelves, joists or stripes really are parallel.

I can…
  • tell which angle pairs are equal and which add to 180° when the lines are parallel
  • find all 8 angles from just one, and give a reason for each one
  • find angles in steps, write an equation for a letter like x, and use a perpendicular transversal
  • use the angles to decide if two lines are parallel
G5 Solve problems that involve parallel, perpendicular and transversal lines, and pairs of angles formed between them.
1

The Parallel-Line Rules

A transversal is a line that crosses two other lines. It makes 8 angles. As in Lesson 7, angles 1 to 4 are at the top crossing and angles 5 to 8 are at the bottom crossing. Each set is numbered clockwise, starting at the upper-left.

Two lines are parallel if they are always the same distance apart and never meet. Matching arrow marks (>) on two lines mean they are parallel. We write \ell_1\parallel\ell_2 (say “\ell_1 is parallel to \ell_2”).

Key idea   If \ell_1\parallel\ell_2, these pairs are equal:
corresponding (F shape)\angle1=\angle5,\ \angle2=\angle6,\ \angle3=\angle7,\ \angle4=\angle8
alternate interior (Z shape)\angle3=\angle5,\ \angle4=\angle6
alternate exterior\angle1=\angle7,\ \angle2=\angle8
and these pairs are supplementary (add to 180°):
interior, same side (U shape)\angle3+\angle6=180^\circ,\ \angle4+\angle5=180^\circ
exterior, same side\angle1+\angle8=180^\circ,\ \angle2+\angle7=180^\circ
Always true, parallel or not: vertically opposite angles are equal (\angle1=\angle3, \angle2=\angle4, \angle5=\angle7, \angle6=\angle8), and two angles side by side on a straight line add to 180° (for example \angle1+\angle2=180^\circ).
✓
Shortcut to check. With parallel lines there are only two sizes of angle. All the small (acute) angles are equal, all the big (obtuse) angles are equal, and small + big = 180°. Use this to check, but still write the name of the rule as your reason.
!
Watch out. Use the parallel-line rules only when you know the lines are parallel: arrow marks, or the question says so. Lesson 7 showed that the pairs are not equal when the lines are not parallel.

Why are corresponding angles equal?

Slide the top crossing down the transversal. If the lines are parallel, it lands exactly on the bottom crossing, so every angle lands on its partner.

More practice

All 8 from one angle

\ell_1\parallel\ell_2 and \angle4=57^\circ. Write the size of every other angle, with a reason.

Small angles, all 57°: \angle2 (vertically opposite \angle4), \angle8 (corresponding to \angle4), \angle6 (alternate interior with \angle4).
Big angles, all 180^\circ-57^\circ=123^\circ: \angle1 and \angle3 (straight line with \angle4), \angle5 and \angle7 (corresponding to \angle1 and \angle3).

Think it through

Think \ell_1\parallel\ell_2. Can \angle2 be 80° and \angle7 be 80° at the same time?
No. \angle2 and \angle7 are exterior angles on the same side, so they add to 180°. If \angle2=80^\circ, then \angle7=100^\circ. (Both would be 90° only for a perpendicular transversal.)
Think The lines are not parallel and \angle1=110^\circ. Which other angles do you still know for sure?
Only the ones at the same crossing: \angle3=110^\circ (vertically opposite), and \angle2=\angle4=70^\circ (straight line). Angles 5 to 8 could be anything.
2

Finding Angles in Steps, With Reasons

Many diagrams need two or three steps. Look for an angle you can find, write its size on the diagram, then use it to find the next one. The order matters, so write your steps in a table: the angle, its size and the reason.

Key idea   When an angle is named with a letter, such as x, write an equation and solve it. If x and 47° are on a straight line, then x+47^\circ=180^\circ, so x=180^\circ-47^\circ=133^\circ.

\ell_1\parallel\ell_2. Two straight roads meet at point A on \ell_1 and make a triangle with \ell_2. Find a, b and c, in that order. Give a reason for each.

Example 4

A parking lot

In a parking lot, the stall lines are parallel. The curb is straight, and the painted line at the front of the stalls is parallel to the curb. Each stall line meets the curb at 62°.

(a) Write an equation for a. Solve it.
a and 62° are on a straight line (the curb).
a+62^\circ=180^\circ
a=180^\circ-62^\circ=118^\circ
(b) Find b. Give your reason.
b=62^\circ. The stall lines are parallel and the curb is a transversal, so b and 62° are corresponding angles.
(c) Find c. Give your reason.
The curb and the front line are parallel, and the stall line is a transversal. b and c are interior angles on the same side.
c=180^\circ-62^\circ=118^\circ
So each stall is a parallelogram with angles 62°, 118°, 62° and 118°.

Think it through

Think In Example 3, Mia says “c looks like about 70°, so c = 70°.” What is wrong with that?
A diagram may not be drawn to scale, and a guess is not a reason. Use the rules: c=180^\circ-48^\circ-57^\circ=75^\circ.
Think Can a parallelogram have angles 70°, 110°, 80° and 100°? Why or why not?
No. Both pairs of sides are parallel, so there are only two sizes of angle: if one is 70°, the angles are 70°, 110°, 70° and 110°.
More practice

Two more step-by-step angles

(a) A gate has two parallel rails and a diagonal brace. The brace makes 38° with the bottom rail. Find the angle y between the brace and the top rail, on the other side of the brace (a Z shape).
The rails are parallel and the brace is a transversal. y and 38° are alternate interior angles, so y=38^\circ.
(b) \ell_1\parallel\ell_2 and \angle2=64^\circ. Find \angle7 in two steps, with a reason for each step.
\angle6=64^\circ (corresponding to \angle2). \angle7=180^\circ-64^\circ=116^\circ (on a straight line with \angle6). Or in one step: exterior angles on the same side.

Perpendicular Transversals

Two lines are perpendicular if they meet at a right angle (90°). A small square in the corner shows a right angle. We write t\perp\ell_1.

If a transversal is perpendicular to one of two parallel lines, it is perpendicular to the other one too. The corresponding angles are equal, so they are both 90°. In fact, all 8 angles are 90°.

Key idea   If \ell_1\parallel\ell_2 and t\perp\ell_1, then t\perp\ell_2. All 8 angles are 90°.
Two angles that make a right angle together are complementary: they add to 90°.

Builders use this. The wall studs in a house are vertical, so they are parallel to each other and perpendicular to the level floor.

Example 5

Balusters on a staircase

A carpenter is putting the balusters (the upright posts) on a staircase. The balusters are vertical, so they are parallel to each other and perpendicular to the level floor and stair treads. The handrail rises at 36° from the horizontal.

(a) What is angle a, where a baluster meets a tread? Why?
a=90^\circ. The baluster is perpendicular to the level tread.
(b) Find b and c, the angles where a baluster meets the handrail.
Draw a horizontal line through the top of the baluster. It is parallel to the dashed line, so the rail makes 36° with it too (alternate interior angles).
The baluster is perpendicular to it, so b+36^\circ=90^\circ (complementary).
b=90^\circ-36^\circ=54^\circ
c=180^\circ-54^\circ=126^\circ (straight line along the rail)
(c) Why can the carpenter cut the tops of all the balusters at the same angle?
The balusters are parallel and the handrail is a transversal. So every baluster meets the rail at corresponding angles, which are equal (54° and 126° at every baluster).

Think it through

Think The rungs of a ladder are parallel. One rung is perpendicular to the left rail. Is every rung perpendicular to the left rail? How do you know?
Yes. The left rail is a transversal of the parallel rungs. Corresponding angles are equal, so every rung meets it at 90°.
Think Two complementary angles are equal. How big is each one?
They add to 90°, so each one is 90^\circ\div2=45^\circ. (A mitre cut on a picture frame!)
4

Are the Lines Parallel?

The rules also work backwards. Measure a pair of angles made by a transversal. If the pair follows a parallel-line rule, the lines are parallel. If it does not, the lines are not parallel: if you made them longer, they would meet.

Key idea   Two lines cut by a transversal are parallel if
  • a pair of corresponding angles is equal, or
  • a pair of alternate interior (or alternate exterior) angles is equal, or
  • a pair of interior (or exterior) angles on the same side adds to 180°.
If the pair does not follow the rule, even by 1°, the lines are not parallel.
!
Watch out. Vertically opposite angles are always equal, so they tell you nothing about whether the lines are parallel. Also, don’t trust your eyes: a diagram may not be drawn to scale. Use the angle sizes.
Example 6

Leah’s leaning bookshelf

Leah is building a leaning bookshelf. The shelves are \ell_1 and \ell_2 and the slanted leg is the transversal t. She measures two angles. Are the shelves parallel? Give the rule you used. (The diagrams are not drawn to scale.)

(a)
The two 70° angles are corresponding angles. They are equal, so yes, the shelves are parallel.
(b)
These are interior angles on the same side. 112^\circ+66^\circ=178^\circ, not 180°, so no, the shelves are not parallel.
(c)
The two 75° angles are alternate interior angles. They are equal, so yes, the shelves are parallel.
Example 7

Owen’s deck joists

Owen is building a deck at an angle. Two floor joists are nailed across a beam, and they must be parallel. He measures the angles shown.

(a) Are the joists parallel? Explain.
Find the upper-right angle at joist 2: 180^\circ-117^\circ=63^\circ (straight line).
It corresponds to the 60° angle at joist 1. 63^\circ\ne60^\circ, so no, the joists are not parallel.
(b) What should the 117° angle be, so that the joists are parallel?
At joist 1 the upper-left angle is 180^\circ-60^\circ=120^\circ.
The matching (corresponding) angle at joist 2 must also be 120°.
(c) By how many degrees must Owen turn joist 2? Should the 117° angle get bigger or smaller?
120^\circ-117^\circ=3^\circ.
He must turn joist 2 by 3°, so that the 117° angle gets bigger (to 120°).

Think it through

Think Sam measures \angle5=101^\circ and \angle7=101^\circ. He says the lines must be parallel. Is he right?
No. \angle5 and \angle7 are vertically opposite. They are always equal, parallel or not, so they prove nothing.
Think Two interior angles on the same side add to 183°. Do the lines meet on that side of the transversal, or on the other side?
On the other side. The two interior angles on the other side add to 360^\circ-183^\circ=177^\circ, less than 180°, and the lines lean toward each other on the side with the smaller sum.

Which pairs can prove the lines are parallel?

Turn the bottom line and watch three angles. Vertically opposite angles always match, so they prove nothing. A corresponding pair matches only when the lines are parallel.

More practice

Fence posts and ladder rungs

(a) Two fence posts stand on straight, sloping ground. Between the posts, the downhill post makes 88° with the ground and the uphill post makes 92°. Are the posts parallel?
Both angles are between the posts, on the same side of the ground line: interior angles on the same side. 88^\circ+92^\circ=180^\circ, so yes, the posts are parallel.
(b) Two ladder rungs meet the left rail at 81° and 79°, both measured above the rung, on the right of the rail. Are the rungs parallel? By how many degrees is one off?
Same corner at each crossing: corresponding angles. 81^\circ\ne79^\circ, so no. One rung is off by 81^\circ-79^\circ=2^\circ.
Math Historyabout 300 BCE · 1829

The rule nobody could prove

About 2300 years ago, the Greek mathematician Euclid wrote down the basic rules of geometry. His fifth rule is today’s idea: if a transversal makes two interior angles on the same side that add to less than 180°, the two lines will meet on that side if you make them long enough.

For about 2000 years, mathematicians tried to prove this rule from Euclid’s other rules, and failed. In 1829 and 1831, Lobachevsky (Russia) and Bolyai (Hungary) showed why: a whole different geometry works without it.

Owen’s joists: the two angles between them, above the beam, are 60° and 117°. Do the joists meet above the beam or below it?
60^\circ+117^\circ=177^\circ, less than 180°, so by Euclid’s rule they meet above the beam: very far up (about 15 times the gap between the joists), but they do meet.
✓

Key ideas

If \ell_1\parallel\ell_2: corresponding (F), alternate interior (Z) and alternate exterior angles are equal; angles on the same side (interior U, or exterior) add to 180°.
Always: vertically opposite angles are equal, and angles on a straight line add to 180°. With parallel lines there are only two sizes: small + big = 180°.
Find angles in steps: angle, size, reason. Letters: write an equation (x+47^\circ=180^\circ). A transversal ⊥ one parallel line is ⊥ the other: all 8 angles are 90°.
Parallel? Check a pair: corresponding or alternate angles equal, or same-side angles add to 180°. Off by even 1°: not parallel. Vertically opposite angles prove nothing.
