What Colour Is the Bear?

A hiker walks 1 km south. Then she walks 1 km east. Then she walks 1 km north.

She is right back where she started!

Then she sees a bear.

What colour is the bear?

A brown
B black
C white
D you can’t tell
C: white! On flat paper the walk never closes up. But the Earth is round. Start at the North Pole: 1 km south, then 1 km east goes around the pole in a circle, and 1 km north takes you straight back to the pole. The only bears there are polar bears. Bonus: there are also spots near the South Pole where this walk works, but there are no bears in Antarctica!

Estimating Angles with Referents, and True Bearings

Carpenters, pilots and hunters often picture an angle instead of grabbing a protractor. Today we learn a few angles you can always see in your head, use them to estimate and sketch any angle, and then use angles measured from north to give directions on a map.

I can…
  • make the referent angles 22.5°, 30°, 45°, 60°, 90° and 180° with a sheet of paper or a clock
  • build an angle from referents and name its type
  • estimate an angle before I measure it, and use my estimate to pick the right protractor scale
  • sketch an angle without a protractor
  • write a direction as a true bearing, draw and measure bearings on a map, and find the bearing back
G6 Demonstrate an understanding of angles, including acute, right, obtuse, straight and reflex, by: drawing; replicating and constructing; bisecting; solving problems.
Today: estimating and sketching angles with referents, and angles as true bearings.
1

Referent Angles

A referent angle is an angle you can picture easily because you know something that has that size. You can make every referent with a sheet of paper or a clock.

Key idea   Referent angles
90°the corner of a sheet of paper
45°fold the corner in half
22.5°fold it in half again
30°one hour mark on a clock
60°two hour marks on a clock
180°a straight line

A clock has 12 hour marks around a full turn of 360°, so each hour mark is 360^\circ\div12=30^\circ.

One hour mark: 360 ÷ 12 ENTER gives 30. Four hour marks: 4 × 30 ENTER gives 120.
Think There are 30° between two hour marks. How many degrees are between two minute marks?
A clock has 60 minute marks around 360°: 360^\circ\div60=6^\circ. So 5 minute marks make one hour mark: 5\times6^\circ=30^\circ.

Building bigger angles from referents

Bigger angles are built from referents. Add pieces, or take a piece away from a straight line (180°) or a full turn (360°):

135^\circ=90^\circ+45^\circ      330^\circ=360^\circ-30^\circ
Think Build 330° a second way, using a different referent.
For example 330^\circ=270^\circ+60^\circ, where 270^\circ=180^\circ+90^\circ. Or 330^\circ=180^\circ+90^\circ+60^\circ. Many answers are right.

2

Estimating the Size of an Angle

To estimate means to make a good guess using what you know. Estimate an angle before you measure it. Then you can tell right away if your protractor reading is wrong.

Key idea   Estimating an angle
  • Step 1. Decide the type: less than 90° (acute), between 90° and 180° (obtuse), or more than 180° (reflex).
  • Step 2. Find the two referents it is between, and decide which one it is closer to.
  • Step 3. Write an estimate, such as “a little more than 60°, about 70°”.

A good estimate is usually within about 10° of the measured angle. Turning the page does not change the size of an angle, so tilt your head or the page if it helps.

Think Mo estimates an angle as “about 80°”. It measures 87°. Is that a good estimate?
Yes. 87^\circ-80^\circ=7^\circ, which is within about 10°.

Your estimate picks the protractor scale

A protractor has two scales, so an arm crosses two numbers. The two numbers always add to 180°. Your estimate tells you which number to read.

Think Why do the two numbers an arm crosses always add to 180°?
The base line is a straight angle, 180°. The arm splits it into two parts. The outer scale counts one part from the left end, and the inner scale counts the other part from the right end. Together the two parts make the whole 180°.
Example 3

Which number is the angle?

Estimate each angle first. Then read the protractor. The arm crosses two numbers: which one is the angle?

(a)
Estimate: acute, about 65°
The two numbers: 65 and 115
The angle is 65°.
The angle is acute, so it is 65°, not 115°. The arm on the left sits on the outer 0.
(b)
Estimate: obtuse, about 135°
The two numbers: 40 and 140
The angle is 140°.
The angle is obtuse, so it is 140°, not 40°. The arm on the right sits on the inner 0.

Try it: estimate first, then measure

The reading starts hidden. Have the class estimate the angle. Then drag the protractor onto the vertex, turn it with the round handles, and tap Hide the reading to check. New angle draws another one.

Example 4

Is the ladder safe?

A safety rule for an extension ladder is that it should make an angle of about 75° with the ground. Too steep and it can tip back; too flat and the bottom can slide out.

(a) Estimate the angle between each ladder and the ground.
(b) Which ladder is set up safely? Explain.

(a) Ladder P: between 60° and 90°, about halfway, so about 75°. Ladder Q: a little less than 60°, about 55°.
(b) Ladder P is safe: it is close to 75°. Ladder Q is too flat, about 20° less than the rule, so its bottom could slide out.
3

Sketching an Angle

A sketch is a quick drawing without a protractor. Sketch an angle by building it from referents.

Key idea   Sketching an angle
  • Step 1. Draw one arm and mark the vertex.
  • Step 2. Picture the referents nearby (90° is an L, 45° is half of that, 180° is a straight line).
  • Step 3. Draw the second arm where you think the angle is. Mark the angle with an arc.
  • Step 4. Check with a protractor. Were you within about 10°?
Example 5

Sketch without a protractor

Sketch each angle without a protractor. Write the referents you used. Then check with a protractor.

(a) 45°
Half of a right angle.
(b) 150°
A straight line less 30° (one hour mark).
(c) 20°
A bit less than 22.5°.
(d) 240°
A straight line plus 60° (a reflex angle).
Extra Example

Pizza angles

(a) A pizza is cut into 8 equal slices. What is the angle at the tip of one slice?
The slices share a full turn: 360^\circ\div8=45^\circ, the same as one fold of a paper corner.
(b) A bigger pizza is cut into 12 equal slices. What is the angle of one slice?
360^\circ\div12=30^\circ, like one hour mark on a clock.
(c) Which referents would you use to sketch 165°?
A straight line less half an hour mark: 180^\circ-15^\circ=165^\circ. It is obtuse, just short of a straight line.

True Bearings

A true bearing gives a direction as an angle. It is measured clockwise from north. Pilots, ship captains, hunters, surveyors and orienteers use bearings because a number is more exact than “northeast”.

Key idea   True bearing: the angle measured clockwise from north, written with 3 digits.
N = 000°E = 090°S = 180°W = 270°
NE = 045°SE = 135°SW = 225°NW = 315°
Bearings go from 000° up to (but not including) 360°.

The 16-point compass splits each 45° in half again, so the points are 22.5° apart. NNE (north-northeast) is halfway between N and NE: 022.5°.

!
Watch out. Always start at north and turn clockwise (the way clock hands move). Write 3 digits: east is 090°, not 90°.

A direction is often described as “20° south of east”. That means: face east, then turn 20° toward south. Make a quick sketch, start from the bearing of the main direction (east is 090°), and add if the turn is clockwise, subtract if it is counterclockwise.

Think Why is there no bearing of 360°?
Turning 360° is a full turn: you face north again. North is already 000°, so bearings stop just before 360°.
Example 6

(a) Write the true bearing of each compass direction.

(b) Write each direction as a true bearing.
(i) 30° east of north 030°
(ii) 20° south of east 110°
(iii) 40° west of south 220°
(iv) 15° west of north 345°
(i) From N (000°) turn 30° clockwise: 000^\circ+30^\circ=030^\circ. (ii) From E (090°) turn clockwise toward S: 090^\circ+20^\circ=110^\circ. (iii) From S (180°) turn clockwise toward W: 180^\circ+40^\circ=220^\circ. (iv) From N turn 15° counterclockwise toward W: 360^\circ-15^\circ=345^\circ.

Try it: point the compass

Drag the red arrow around the compass. The bearing counts clockwise from north. Tap Hide the bearing, point the arrow somewhere, and let the class say the bearing first.

5

Bearings on a Map, and the Bearing Back

A school protractor only goes to 180°. For a bearing more than 180°, measure the angle on the west side of the north line and subtract it from 360°.

Key idea   Drawing or measuring a bearing from a point
  • Step 1. Draw a north line through the point (straight up, the same way as the map’s north arrow).
  • Step 2. Put the protractor’s centre on the point and its 0 line on the north line.
  • Step 3. If the path goes to the east (right) side: bearing = the angle you measure.
    If it goes to the west (left) side: bearing = 360° − the angle you measure.
(To draw 250°: 360^\circ-250^\circ=110^\circ, so mark 110° on the west side of north.)
West side: you measure 105°, so the bearing is 360 − 105 ENTER = 255.
Example 7

Draw the bearing

Draw a line from each point P in the direction of the bearing.
(a), (b): measure 60° and 135° clockwise (east side). (c) 360^\circ-250^\circ=110^\circ on the west side. (d) 360^\circ-320^\circ=40^\circ on the west side.
Example 8

A hiking map in Kananaskis

A hiking map in Kananaskis shows a parking lot (P), a bridge (B), a lookout (L) and a lake (K). Use a protractor.

(a) Find the bearing from P to B. 050°
(b) Find the bearing from B to L. 135°
(c) Find the bearing from L to K. 255°
(d) The hikers walk from the lake straight back to the parking lot. What bearing do they follow? about 350°
(a) B is on the east side of P’s north line: measure 50°, bearing 050°. (b) 135°, east side. (c) K is on the west side of L’s north line: measure 105°, so 360^\circ-105^\circ=255^\circ. (d) Draw a north line at K. P is just west of it: measure about 10°, so 360^\circ-10^\circ=350^\circ (within 2° is fine).

The bearing back

The bearing back (also called the back bearing) is the direction for the return trip. It points exactly the opposite way, so it differs by 180°.

Key idea   Bearing back:
bearing less than 180°:  add 180°
bearing 180° or more:  subtract 180°
Check: the answer must be between 000° and 359°.
335° is more than 180°, so subtract: 335 − 180 ENTER gives 155.

Why is the bearing back always 180° different?

Every point on a map has its own north line, and all the north lines are parallel. The way back runs along the same path, pointing the other way: half a turn.

Think In Example 8, the bearing from B to L is 135°. Without a protractor, find the bearing from L back to B.
135° is less than 180°, so add: 135^\circ+180^\circ=315^\circ.
Example 9

Find the bearing back

(a) Find the bearing back for each bearing.
Bearing070°145°200°315°180°
Back250°325°020°135°000°
070+180=250;  145+180=325;  200-180=020;  315-180=135;  180-180=000 (south and north are opposite).
(b) A bush pilot flies from Fort McMurray to a fishing lodge on a bearing of 335°. On what bearing should she fly to get home?
335° is more than 180°, so subtract: 335^\circ-180^\circ=155^\circ. She should fly home on a bearing of 155°.
Extra Example

A snowmobile trip

A snowmobile trail leaves a cabin on a bearing of 240°.

(a) Is the trail closer to SW, WSW or W?
SW = 225°, WSW = 247.5°, W = 270°. 240° is 7.5° from WSW and 15° from SW: closest to WSW.
(b) On what bearing do they ride back to the cabin?
240° is more than 180°: 240^\circ-180^\circ=060^\circ.
(c) They leave at 5:00. What angle do the clock hands make?
5 hour marks: 5\times30^\circ=150^\circ, an obtuse angle.
✓

Key ideas

Referents: paper corner 90°, fold it 45°, fold again 22.5°; one hour mark 30°, two 60°; a straight line 180°. Build others: 135^\circ=90^\circ+45^\circ.
Estimate first: the type, the two referents it is between, then a number. Within about 10° is good. The estimate picks the protractor scale.
True bearings: clockwise from north, 3 digits. N 000°, E 090°, S 180°, W 270°. “20° south of east”: from 090° turn clockwise, 110°.
On a map: north line, protractor 0 on it. West side: 360^\circ- the angle. Bearing back: under 180°, add 180°; 180° or more, subtract 180°.
