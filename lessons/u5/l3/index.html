The Sliding Ladder

A ladder leans against a wall. There is a dot of paint at the exact middle of the ladder.

The bottom of the ladder slips. It slides out along the floor, and the top slides down the wall, until the ladder lies flat.

What path does the dot of paint follow?

A a straight line, slanting down
B a curve that bulges out, away from the corner
C a curve that dips in, toward the corner
D straight down, then straight across
B: part of a circle! The middle of the ladder always stays the same distance from the corner where the wall meets the floor: half the ladder’s length. Points that are all the same distance from one centre make a circle, so the dot draws a quarter circle. Try it: slide a ruler down a book standing on your desk and watch its middle. On the way down, the ladder makes every angle with the floor, from almost 90° to 0°.

Measuring, Drawing and Classifying Angles

Angles are everywhere: a laptop screen, a ladder on the floor, a slice of pizza, the corners of a stop sign. Today we name angles, sort them by size, measure and draw them with a protractor (even when they are turned), find reflex angles, and discover the rule for the angles inside any polygon.

I can…
  • name the vertex and arms of an angle, and name an angle three ways (\angle ABC, \angle B, \angle 1)
  • sort angles by size: acute, right, obtuse, straight and reflex
  • measure an angle with a protractor, using the scale that has its 0 on an arm
  • draw an angle of a given size, and find or draw a reflex angle: 360^\circ- the smaller angle
  • use inductive reasoning to find the sum of the angles in a polygon: (n-2)\times180^\circ
G6 Demonstrate an understanding of angles, including acute, right, obtuse, straight and reflex, by: drawing; replicating and constructing; bisecting; solving problems.
Today: measuring, drawing and classifying angles, and solving problems with them.
1

The Parts of an Angle, and Its Name

An angle is made by two arms (rays) that start at the same point. That point is the vertex. The size of an angle is the amount of turn from one arm to the other. We measure it in degrees (°). One full turn is 360°, a half turn is 180° and a quarter turn is 90°.

Key idea   Three ways to name an angle
  1. 1. Three letters, with the vertex in the middle: \angle ABC or \angle CBA.
  2. 2. The vertex letter alone: \angle B. Use this only when there is just one angle at that vertex.
  3. 3. A small number or letter written inside the angle: \angle 1.
The symbol \angle means “angle”.
!
Watch out. The length of the arms does not change the size of an angle. Long arms or short arms, the turn between them is the same. If the arms are too short to measure, make them longer with a ruler.
Example 1

Name the angles

(a) Name \angle 1 in two other ways.
\angle PQR or \angle RQP (the vertex Q in the middle)
(b) Name the vertex and the two arms of \angle 2.
vertex Q; arms QR and QS
(c) Why is it not clear to call \angle 1 “\angle Q”?
Three angles have their vertex at Q: \angle 1, \angle 2, and the big angle \angle PQS. The name \angle Q could mean any of them.
2

Types of Angles

We sort angles into types by their size. Every type has a name you need to know.

Acute
less than 90°a sharp corner
Right
exactly 90°a square corner (a small square marks 90°)
Obtuse
between 90° and 180°wider than a square corner
Straight
exactly 180°a straight line
Reflex
between 180° and 360°more than a half turn

Every pair of arms really makes two angles: the small one inside, and the big one going the long way around. Together they make a full turn, 360°. When we say “the angle” we mean the smaller one, unless it is marked the long way around. Then it is a reflex angle.

Angles are all around us: the corner of a page, a door swinging open, a car’s seat back, the slope of a roof. An angle can also be made where a line meets a flat surface (a plane), like a fence post standing in the ground or a ladder leaning on the floor. A fence post that is straight up meets the ground at 90°. A leaning ladder makes an acute angle with the floor.

Measuring an Angle with a Protractor

A protractor measures angles. It has two scales of numbers that run in opposite directions. The outer (black) scale starts at 0 on the left. The inner (grey) scale starts at 0 on the right. The centre mark goes on the vertex, and the base line (the 0 line) goes along one arm.

Key idea   Measuring an angle: 3 steps
  1. Step 1. Put the centre mark exactly on the vertex.
  2. Step 2. Turn the protractor so the base line lies along one arm.
  3. Step 3. Find the 0 that sits on that arm. Follow that scale around to the other arm and read the number.
Check: does the angle look acute or obtuse? Every mark has two numbers (like 50 and 130). Pick the one that matches.
!
Watch out. The angle does not have to point to the right, and the protractor does not have to be level. Turn the protractor (or the page) so the base line lies on an arm. The 0 you start from can be on the left or on the right.

Try it: drag and turn the protractor

Drag the protractor to move it. Turn it with the round handles. It snaps onto the vertex and onto an arm, and the box under it tells you each step. Tap Hide the reading, then New angle (it can point any way), and let the class read it first.

Why does every mark have two numbers that add to 180?

The two scales count the same straight base line from opposite ends.

Think A classmate reads 155° for an angle that is clearly a sharp corner. What did they most likely do, and what is the right reading?
They read the wrong scale (the one whose 0 is not on an arm). The two numbers at a mark add to 180, so the right reading is 180^\circ-155^\circ=25^\circ, which is acute.
Example 4

Read the protractor

Read each protractor. Give the size of the angle and its type.

The arm on the right is on the inner 0. Inner scale: 62°. (Not 118: the angle is acute.)
(b) Size 145°   Type obtuse
The arm on the left is on the outer 0. Outer scale: 145°. (Not 35: the angle is obtuse.)
(c) Size 108°   Type obtuse
The protractor is turned, but one arm is still on the inner 0. Inner scale: 108°.
(d) Size 35°   Type acute
One arm is on the outer 0. Count on the outer scale: 35°. (Not 145: the angle is acute.)

4

Drawing Angles, and Reflex Angles

Key idea   Drawing an angle of a given size (for example, 70°)
  1. Step 1. Use a ruler to draw one arm. Put a dot at one end for the vertex.
  2. Step 2. Put the centre mark on the vertex and the base line along the arm.
  3. Step 3. Start at the 0 on your arm. Follow that scale to 70 and make a small dot at the edge.
  4. Step 4. Take the protractor away. Join the vertex to the dot with a ruler. Label the angle 70°.
Example 6

Draw the angle

Draw each angle on the arm given. Mark and label the angle.
(a) \angle ABC=50^\circ
Inner scale: the 0 is on the right, on arm BA.
(b) \angle DEF=135^\circ
Use the outer scale: the 0 is on the left, on arm ED. The new arm can go up (as shown) or down; both make 135°.
(c) \angle GHI=90^\circ
A right angle: mark it with a small square instead of an arc.

Reflex angles

A protractor only goes up to 180°. To find a reflex angle, measure the small angle that goes with it, then subtract from 360°.

Key idea   Reflex angle =360^\circ- the smaller angle    Check: the answer must be between 180° and 360°.
To draw a reflex angle, first find the smaller angle (360^\circ- reflex), draw it, and then mark the reflex angle with an arc going the long way around.
A smaller angle of 125°: 360 − 125 ENTER gives 235.
Example 7

Reflex angles

(a) The smaller angle between two arms is 125°. What is the reflex angle?
360^\circ-125^\circ=235^\circ. The reflex angle is 235°.
(b) Measure the marked reflex angle.
Small angle: 64°. Reflex: 360^\circ-64^\circ=296^\circ.
(c) Draw a reflex angle of 250° on the arm given.
360^\circ-250^\circ=110^\circ. Draw 110°, then mark the outside with an arc.
Extra example

More reflex angles

(a) The smaller angle is 40°. Find the reflex angle.
360^\circ-40^\circ=320^\circ
(b) The smaller angle is a right angle. Find the reflex angle.
360^\circ-90^\circ=270^\circ
(c) The smaller angle is 172°. Find the reflex angle.
360^\circ-172^\circ=188^\circ, just over 180°
(d) A reflex angle is 205°. What is the smaller angle?
360^\circ-205^\circ=155^\circ (obtuse)
Think Why is a reflex angle always more than 180°?
The smaller angle is at most 180°, so 360^\circ- the smaller angle is at least 180°. The two angles share the full turn, and the reflex one is the bigger share.

Angles Inside a Polygon

A polygon is a closed shape made of straight sides. It is named by its number of sides, n: triangle (3), quadrilateral (4), pentagon (5), hexagon (6), heptagon (7), octagon (8), nonagon (9), decagon (10), dodecagon (12). The angles inside a polygon, at its corners, are its interior angles. A regular polygon has all sides equal and all angles equal.

Triangles first. Cut out any paper triangle and tear off its three corners. Put the corners side by side: they always make a straight line. So the angles in any triangle add up to 180°.

Inductive reasoning means looking at several examples, finding a pattern, and using the pattern to make a general rule. We use it now to find a rule for any polygon.

Example 8

Find the pattern

In each polygon, lines are drawn from one corner to the other corners. This splits the polygon into triangles.

(a) Complete the table. For 7 and 10 sides, use the pattern instead of drawing.
Number of sides, n3456710n
Number of triangles123458n-2
Sum of the angles180°360°540°720°900°1 440°(n-2)\times180^\circ
Triangles: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 8, n-2. Sums: 180°, 360°, 540°, 720°, 900°, 1 440°, (n-2)\times180^\circ.
(b) Describe the pattern in words.
The number of triangles is always 2 less than the number of sides. Each triangle adds 180°, so the sum of the angles is (sides − 2) × 180°.

Watch the polygons split into triangles

Key idea   Sum of the interior angles of a polygon with n sides:   S=(n-2)\times180^\circ
Each angle of a regular polygon:   S\div n
Use brackets so the subtracting is done first. For 10 sides: ( 10 − 2 ) × 180 ENTER gives 1 440.

Why n-2? Another way: triangles from a point inside

Think Without the brackets, the calculator does 10-2\times180. What does it show, and why is that wrong?
It multiplies first: 2\times180=360, then 10-360=-350. A sum of angles can’t be negative. The brackets make it subtract first: (10-2)\times180=1\,440.
Example 9

The stop sign

A stop sign is a regular octagon. (a) Find the sum of its interior angles.
An octagon has n=8 sides. S=(8-2)\times180^\circ=6\times180^\circ=1\,080^\circ
(b) A sign maker cuts the corners of a stop sign from sheet metal. What angle is each corner?
The octagon is regular, so all 8 angles are equal. 1\,080^\circ\div8=135^\circ. Each corner is 135°.
(c) What type of angle is each corner? obtuse
Obtuse: 135° is between 90° and 180°.
All in one: ( 8 − 2 ) × 180 ÷ 8 ENTER gives 135.

Finding a missing angle

Find the sum of all the angles first. Then take away the angles you know. What is left is the missing angle.

Example 10

Find the missing angle

Find the missing angle x in each shape.
(a) A quadrilateral.
n=4:\ S=(4-2)\times180^\circ=360^\circ
Known angles: 85+110+72=267^\circ
x=360^\circ-267^\circ=93^\circ
(b) The end wall of a garden shed is a pentagon. Find the angle at the peak of the roof.
n=5:\ S=(5-2)\times180^\circ=540^\circ
Known angles: 90+90+125+125=430^\circ
x=540^\circ-430^\circ=110^\circ. The peak is 110°.
Extra example

More polygons

(a) A regular nonagon (9 sides). Find the sum of its angles and the size of each angle.
S=(9-2)\times180^\circ=1\,260^\circ
1\,260^\circ\div9=140^\circ
(b) The angles of a polygon add up to 1 620°. How many sides does it have? (Work backward.)
1\,620^\circ\div180^\circ=9 triangles. Sides = triangles + 2 = 11.
(c) A hexagon has angles 130°, 110°, 125°, 140°, 95° and x. Find x.
S=(6-2)\times180^\circ=720^\circ
130+110+125+140+95=600^\circ
x=720^\circ-600^\circ=120^\circ
✓

Key ideas

Names: two arms from a vertex. \angle ABC has the vertex in the middle; \angle B only when one angle is at B; or \angle 1. Arm length does not change the size.
Types: acute <90^\circ · right =90^\circ · obtuse between 90° and 180° · straight =180^\circ · reflex between 180° and 360°.
Protractor: centre mark on the vertex, base line on one arm, read the scale whose 0 is on that arm. Check acute or obtuse. Reflex =360^\circ- the smaller angle.
Polygons: n-2 triangles from one corner, so S=(n-2)\times180^\circ. Regular: each angle is S\div n. Missing angle: S- the known angles.
