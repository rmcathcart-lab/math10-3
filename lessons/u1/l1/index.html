Seven Coins, One Dollar

You have a big pile of nickels, dimes and quarters.

You must use exactly 7 coins.

How can you make exactly $1.00?

?
Is there more than one way?
Two ways! 2 quarters + 5 dimes (50¢ + 50¢), or 3 quarters + 1 dime + 3 nickels (75¢ + 10¢ + 15¢). Both use 7 coins and make $1.00. And the fewest coins for $1.00? Just one: a loonie!

Money Sense: Rounding, Estimating and Using Your Calculator

Pay cheques, grocery bills, gas, splitting a pizza: this whole unit is about money. Today we warm up the skills it runs on: reading money amounts, rounding them, estimating a bill in your head, and using the TI-30XIIS for exact totals and change.

I can…
  • read and write money using place value (dollars, dimes, cents, tenths of a cent)
  • round money to the nearest dollar, ten cents or cent
  • estimate a bill in my head, and tell if an answer is reasonable
  • use BEDMAS and brackets on my calculator
  • find change by counting up, and know when cash is rounded to 5¢

1

Place Value with Money

Money is written with a decimal point. The digits to the left of the point are whole dollars. The digits to the right are parts of a dollar.

Tenths the first place after the point: dimes. $0.30 is 3 dimes, or 30 cents.
Hundredths the second place: cents. $0.07 is 7 cents.
Thousandths the third place: tenths of a cent. You can’t pay it, but you see it on gas prices ($1.479 per litre) and shelf tags.

Cents to dollars

Key idea   $1 = 100 cents  ·  1 dime = $0.10  ·  1 cent = $0.01  ·  a tenth of a cent = $0.001
Cents to dollars: divide by 100 (move the decimal point 2 places left).   85¢ = $0.85  ·  149.9¢ = $1.499
!
Watch out. A calculator shows $4.50 as 4.5. That is four dollars and fifty cents, not $4.05. Always write money with two decimal places: $4.50.
Think Is $0.5 the same as 5¢?
No. $0.5 = $0.50 = 50¢ (5 dimes). Five cents is $0.05.

Putting prices in order

To compare prices, give them all two decimals. Then compare the dollars, then the dimes, then the cents.

2

Rounding Money

A calculator often shows more decimal places than you can pay. Then you round: you replace the number with a nearby number that is easier to use.

To round
1Find the place you are rounding to.
2Look at the digit just to its right.
35 or more: round up (add 1 to your place). 4 or less: keep your place the same. Drop the digits after it.
To the nearest dollar:look at the tenths (dimes) digit
To the nearest ten cents:look at the hundredths (cents) digit
To the nearest cent:look at the thousandths digit
!
Watch out. When you round up a 9, it becomes 10 and you carry 1 to the next place: $6.97 to the nearest ten cents is $7.00, not “$6.10”. Also, round only at the end of a calculation. Rounding in the middle can make your final answer a few cents off.
Think Why not just chop off the extra digits? Try $8.278 to the nearest cent.
Chopping gives $8.27, but $8.278 is closer to $8.28. Rounding picks the closer one.

(b) Kim puts 42 L of gas in her car. Gas costs $1.479 per litre. Her calculator shows 62.118. How much does she pay?
Round to the nearest cent: the thousandths digit is 8 (5 or more), so round up. Kim pays $62.12.
Kim’s gas on the calculator: 42×1.479ENTER

Why round only at the end?

Every rounding moves the number a little. Do it once, at the end, and the answer is off by less than half a cent. Round in the middle, and the error gets multiplied.

3

Estimating a Bill

An estimate is an answer that is close to the exact answer and that you can find in your head. Estimating tells you if you have enough money, and if a calculator or receipt answer makes sense.

Rounding estimate: round each price to the nearest dollar, then add.
Front-end estimate: add the dollar parts first (the “front” digits). Then look at the cents and group them into amounts of about $1. Add those dollars on.
✓
Tip. Need to be sure you have enough money? Round every price up to the next dollar ($3.20 becomes $4). That estimate is a bit too high, so if you have that much, you have enough.

Is the answer reasonable?

Key idea   Estimate first, then calculate. If the two answers are far apart, check what you typed. A decimal point in the wrong place makes an answer 10 or 100 times too big or too small.
Example 4

Does it make sense?

(a) Emma and a friend each order a burger at $16.95 and a milkshake at $6.25. The bill says $56.40. Estimate the bill. Does it make sense?
Estimate: 2\times\$17+2\times\$6=\$34+\$12=\$46. $56.40 is about $10 more, so it does not make sense.
Exact: 2\times16.95+2\times6.25=33.90+12.50=\$46.40. The bill is $10.00 too high. Emma should ask the server to fix it.
(b) Tyler buys 5 bags of chips at $3.89 each. His calculator shows 194.5. Is this reasonable? What did he probably do wrong?
Estimate: 5\times\$4=\$20. $194.50 is about 10 times too big, so it is not reasonable.
He probably typed 38.9 instead of 3.89. Correct: 5\times3.89=\boldsymbol{\$19.45}.

Order of Operations on the Calculator

When a calculation has more than one operation, you must do them in a set order, called the order of operations. Remember it with BEDMAS.

Brackets first
→
Exponents
→
Division and Multiplicationleft to right
→
Addition and Subtractionleft to right

The TI-30XIIS follows BEDMAS by itself, so you can type a whole line and it multiplies before it adds. When you need to add first and then divide or multiply, put the adding in brackets.

Calculator. Type the whole calculation on one line, then press ENTER once at the end. Use ( and ) for brackets. The top line of the screen shows what you typed: check it before you press ENTER. To start over, press CLEAR.

Example 6

Sharing a lunch order

!
Watch out. To share a bill, you need the total first. Put the adding in brackets: (a+b+c)\div4. Without brackets, the calculator divides only the last number.

Four coworkers share a lunch order: a pizza for $28.99, wings for $16.49 and drinks for $6.52. They split the cost equally.

(a) Write one calculation with brackets and find each person’s share.
Type: ( 28.99 + 16.49 + 6.52 ) ÷ 4 ENTER
(28.99+16.49+6.52)\div4=52.00\div4=13.00. Each person pays $13.00.
(b) Sam types 28.99 + 16.49 + 6.52 ÷ 4 ENTER and gets 47.11. Explain what went wrong.
With no brackets, the calculator divides only 6.52 by 4 (that is 1.63), then adds: 28.99+16.49+1.63=47.11. That is almost the whole bill, not one person’s share. Sam needs brackets around the adding.
Try both ways and compare the answers.
5

Multiplying, Dividing and Making Change

Multiply to find the cost of many of the same item, or your pay for many hours (wage × hours).
Divide to share a cost, or to find the price of one item in a pack.

Estimate first, use the calculator, then round to the nearest cent at the end.

Example 7

Pay, packs and sharing

(a) Diego works at a garden centre for $16.25 per hour. Last week he worked 18.5 hours. How much did he earn?
Estimate: \$16\times20=\$320; he worked a bit less than 20 h, so about $300.
16.25\times18.5=300.625. Round to the nearest cent: $300.63.
(b) A case of 24 bottles of water costs $5.99. What does one bottle cost?
Estimate: \$6\div24=\$0.25.
5.99\div24=0.249583\ldots, which rounds to $0.25. One bottle costs about $0.25.
(c) Three friends share a $25.00 taxi ride. The calculator shows 8.333333333. How much should each friend pay?
To the nearest cent, $8.33. But 3\times8.33=24.99, one cent short of $25.00. So two friends pay $8.33 and one pays $8.34. (Check: 8.33+8.33+8.34=25.00.)
The calculator gives every digit; you round to the cent at the end.
Think Diego worked 18.5 hours. Is that 18 hours and 50 minutes?
No. 0.5 of an hour is half an hour: 18 hours and 30 minutes.

Making change

When you pay with cash, the cashier gives you change: the amount you paid minus the price. Cashiers often find change by counting up from the price.

Counting up: start at the price. Add small coins to reach a friendly amount (one that ends in 00, 25, 50 or 75 cents), then the next dollar, then add bills up to the amount paid. The change is everything you added.
Check: amount paid − price = change.

No pennies: paying cash

No pennies. Canada stopped making pennies in 2013. When you pay with cash, the total is rounded to the nearest 5 cents. A total ending in 1 or 2 cents rounds down to 0; 3 or 4 rounds up to 5; 6 or 7 rounds down to 5; 8 or 9 rounds up to the next 10. Card and debit payments are not rounded.

Math History1858 – 2013

The coin that cost more than it was worth

Canada’s first cents were made in 1858. For more than 150 years, the penny was in every pocket and every till.

But by the end, making one penny cost about 1.6¢, more than the coin was worth! The Royal Canadian Mint in Winnipeg made its last penny in 2012, and stopped handing pennies out in 2013. That is why cash totals now round to the nearest 5¢.

Making one penny cost 1.6¢. What did it cost to make 100 pennies (worth $1.00)?
100\times1.6\text{¢}=160\text{¢}=\$1.60. It cost $1.60 to make $1.00 of pennies: 60¢ lost on every dollar.
✓

Key ideas

Place value: tenths are dimes, hundredths are cents, thousandths are tenths of a cent. Cents ÷ 100 = dollars. Write money with two decimals: $4.50.
Rounding: look one place to the right. 5 or more, round up; 4 or less, keep. Round only at the end.
Estimate first (rounding or front-end). If the estimate and the answer are far apart, check what you typed.
Calculator: it follows BEDMAS; put adding in brackets before you share. Change: count up from the price. Cash rounds to the nearest 5¢; debit doesn’t.
