Snap the Chocolate Bar

A chocolate bar is 6 squares long and 4 squares wide. That is 24 squares.

You snap it along the lines, one piece at a time. No stacking pieces!

How many snaps does it take to get all 24 squares apart?

?
Does it matter which way you snap first?
23 snaps, every time. Each snap turns one piece into two, so it adds exactly one piece. You start with 1 piece and need 24, so you need 24-1=23 snaps. The order does not matter!

Fractions Refresher: Equivalent Fractions and Simplest Form

Fractions are everywhere at work: half a tank, three-quarters of your hours, a five-eighths wrench. Today we review what a fraction means, how two fractions can be the same amount, and how to find a fraction of an amount. Ratios and rates (next lessons) are built on these skills.

I can…
  • say what the numerator and the denominator of a fraction mean
  • make equivalent fractions and find a missing number
  • use the GCF to write a fraction in simplest form
  • compare fractions, and change between fractions and decimals on my calculator
  • find a fraction of an amount, like \dfrac34 of 24
Refresher Skills from earlier grades that this unit uses (not marked as a unit outcome).
1

What a Fraction Means

A fraction names part of something. It has two numbers.

Denominator (bottom number): how many equal parts make one whole.
Numerator (top number): how many of those parts you have.

A fraction can be part of a whole (3 of the 8 slices of one pizza) or part of a set (3 of the 8 people on a work crew).

\text{fraction}=\dfrac{\text{numerator}}{\text{denominator}}=\dfrac{\text{number of parts you have}}{\text{number of equal parts in one whole (or items in the set)}}

!
The parts of a whole must all be the same size. A pizza cut into 4 uneven pieces is not cut into quarters.
Example 1

Shaded and not shaded

Write the fraction of each picture that is shaded, and the fraction that is not shaded.

(a)
Shaded: \dfrac{3}{8}
Not shaded: \dfrac{5}{8}
8 equal parts, 3 shaded: \dfrac38 shaded, \dfrac58 not shaded
(b)
Shaded: \dfrac{5}{12}
Not shaded: \dfrac{7}{12}
12 equal squares, 5 shaded: \dfrac{5}{12} shaded, \dfrac{7}{12} not shaded
(c)
Shaded: \dfrac{4}{6}
Not shaded: \dfrac{2}{6}
6 equal slices, 4 shaded: \dfrac46 shaded, \dfrac26 not shaded
(d)
Filled: \dfrac{3}{7}
Not filled: \dfrac{4}{7}
A set of 7 counters, 3 filled: \dfrac37 filled, \dfrac47 not filled

The two fractions always add to one whole: \dfrac38+\dfrac58=\dfrac88=1. In (d), the 7 counters are a set, so the denominator is 7.

2

Equivalent Fractions

Equivalent fractions name the same amount, even though the numbers are different. Each bar has the same amount shaded, so \dfrac12=\dfrac24=\dfrac48.

To make an equivalent fraction, multiply or divide the numerator and the denominator by the same number.
\dfrac{2}{3}=\dfrac{2\times4}{3\times4}=\dfrac{8}{12}\dfrac{15}{20}=\dfrac{15\div5}{20\div5}=\dfrac{3}{4}
Why it works: multiplying the top and the bottom by 4 is the same as multiplying by \dfrac44, and \dfrac44 is one whole. Multiplying by 1 never changes an amount. It only cuts the pieces smaller.
!
Adding the same number to the top and bottom does not work: \dfrac12 is not equal to \dfrac{1+1}{2+1}=\dfrac23. Only multiply or divide.

Finding a missing number

Ask: “What was the denominator multiplied (or divided) by?” Then do the same thing to the numerator. Sometimes the jump is not a whole number. Then simplify first (Section 3), and scale from there.

Example 2

Equivalent fractions and missing numbers

(a) Write three fractions equivalent to \dfrac{3}{5}.
Multiply top and bottom by 2, 3 and 4:
\dfrac{6}{10},\ \dfrac{9}{15},\ \dfrac{12}{20}
(many answers are possible)
(b) Find the missing number: \dfrac{2}{3}=\dfrac{\square}{18}
3\times6=18, so multiply the top by 6 too:
2\times6=12, so \dfrac23=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{12}{18}}
(c) Find the missing number: \dfrac{20}{32}=\dfrac{5}{\square}
20\div4=5, so divide the bottom by 4 too:
32\div4=8, so \dfrac{20}{32}=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{5}{8}}
(d) Find the missing number: \dfrac{4}{7}=\dfrac{36}{\square}
4\times9=36, so multiply the bottom by 9 too:
7\times9=63, so \dfrac47=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{36}{63}}
Example 3

Kenji’s practice tests

On a 20-question practice test for his learner’s licence, Kenji got 15 questions right. If he does equally well on a 28-question practice test, how many will he get right?

We need \dfrac{15}{20}=\dfrac{\square}{28}. 20\times ?=28 has no whole-number answer, so simplify first.
Step 1: Simplify. \dfrac{15}{20}=\dfrac{15\div5}{20\div5}=\dfrac34
Step 2: Scale up. 4\times7=28, so \dfrac34=\dfrac{3\times7}{4\times7}=\dfrac{21}{28}
Kenji would get 21 of the 28 questions right.

Think: would Kenji get more or fewer than 21 right on a 40-question test? (Same skill: \dfrac34=\dfrac{30}{40}, so 30.)

3

Factors, the GCF and Simplest Form

A factor of a number divides into it evenly, with no remainder. Find factors in pairs that multiply to the number. For 12: 1\times12,\ 2\times6,\ 3\times4, so the factors of 12 are 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12.

A common factor of two numbers is a factor of both. The greatest common factor (GCF) is the largest one. A fraction is in simplest form (also called lowest terms) when the only common factor of its numerator and denominator is 1.

To write a fraction in simplest form
1Find the GCF of the numerator and the denominator.
2Divide the numerator and the denominator by the GCF.
?
Can’t find the GCF? Divide by any common factor you see (2, 3, 5, 10, …), then look again. Keep going until 1 is the only common factor. You get the same answer; it just takes more steps.
The TI-30XIIS simplifies fractions for you. Enter \dfrac{18}{24} as 18A b/c24ENTER. The screen shows 3┘4, which means \dfrac34. Use this to check your work, but learn to do it by hand too.
Example 4

The GCF and simplest form

(a) List the factors of 24 and 40. Find the GCF, then write \dfrac{24}{40} in simplest form.
Factors of 24: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24 · Factors of 40: 1, 2, 4, 5, 8, 10, 20, 40
The common factors are 1, 2, 4 and 8, so the GCF is 8. \dfrac{24}{40}=\dfrac{24\div8}{40\div8}=\boldsymbol{\dfrac35}

Write each fraction in simplest form.

(b) \dfrac{18}{45}
GCF is 9
\dfrac{18\div9}{45\div9}=\boldsymbol{\dfrac25}
(c) \dfrac{35}{56}
GCF is 7
\dfrac{35\div7}{56\div7}=\boldsymbol{\dfrac58}
(d) \dfrac{40}{100}
GCF is 20 (or \div10, then \div2)
\dfrac{40\div20}{100\div20}=\boldsymbol{\dfrac25}
Example 5

Rosa’s week

Last week, Rosa, an apprentice electrician, worked 36 hours. She spent 27 of those hours on a job site and the rest in class.

\dfrac{27}{36}; the GCF of 27 and 36 is 9.
\dfrac{27\div9}{36\div9}=\dfrac34
\dfrac34 of her hours were on the job site.
(b) What fraction of her hours was in class? Check it on your calculator.

Fractions, Decimals and Comparing Fractions

The fraction bar means divide: \dfrac38=3\div8=0.375. Some fractions make a decimal that repeats forever, like \dfrac23=0.666\ldots; round these (to the nearest thousandth, 0.667, is usually enough).

!
Divide the top by the bottom. \dfrac38 is 3\div8=0.375, not 8\div3. A fraction less than one whole always gives a decimal less than 1.
Fraction to decimal: enter the fraction, 3A b/c8ENTER, then press 2ndF◂▸D to see 0.375. Press 2ndF◂▸D again to go back. (Or just key 3÷8ENTER.)
Decimal to fraction: key 0.35ENTER, then 2ndF◂▸D shows 7┘20, which is \dfrac{7}{20}.

Math Historyc. 1200 and 1659

The bar that means “divide”

Long ago, mathematicians in India wrote a fraction as one number on top of another, with no line between them.

Around the year 1200, al-Hassar, a mathematician in Fez, Morocco, is the first person we know of who drew the bar between them. Soon after, Fibonacci used it in Italy, and it spread across Europe.

The ÷ sign came much later. It was first used for division in a German algebra book in 1659, by Johann Rahn. Look at it: a little bar with a dot above and a dot below, like a tiny fraction!

Which is bigger: 5\div8 or 8\div5? Write each one as a fraction first.
5\div8=\dfrac58=0.625 and 8\div5=\dfrac85=1.6. 8\div5 is bigger. The order matters: top ÷ bottom.

Comparing fractions

To compare two fractions, you need to see which is bigger. Use one of two methods.

Method 1: common denominator. Write both fractions with the same denominator. Then the one with the bigger numerator is bigger.
Method 2: decimals. Change both fractions to decimals and compare the decimals.

Use < (“is less than”) and > (“is greater than”). The point of the sign faces the smaller number: \dfrac12<\dfrac34.

!
A bigger denominator means smaller pieces. \dfrac18 of a pizza is less than \dfrac15 of the same pizza.
Example 7

Comparing and ordering

(a) Use a common denominator to compare \dfrac{3}{4} and \dfrac{5}{6}. Write < or >.
12 is a multiple of 4 and of 6.
\dfrac34=\dfrac{9}{12} and \dfrac56=\dfrac{10}{12}
9<10, so \boldsymbol{\dfrac34<\dfrac56}.
(b) Use decimals to compare \dfrac{5}{8} and \dfrac{7}{11}. Write < or >.
\dfrac58=0.625 and \dfrac{7}{11}=0.6363\ldots\approx0.636
0.625<0.636, so \boldsymbol{\dfrac58<\dfrac{7}{11}}.
(c) A mechanic’s wrenches are marked in inches: \dfrac58,\ \dfrac12,\ \dfrac{9}{16},\ \dfrac34. Put the sizes in order from smallest to largest.
All of them can be written in sixteenths: \dfrac58=\dfrac{10}{16},\ \dfrac12=\dfrac{8}{16},\ \dfrac{9}{16},\ \dfrac34=\dfrac{12}{16}
Order: \boldsymbol{\dfrac12,\ \dfrac{9}{16},\ \dfrac58,\ \dfrac34} in. (Decimals check: 0.5, 0.5625, 0.625, 0.75.)
Example 8

Free throws

In basketball practice, Ava made 13 of her 20 free throws. Noor made 16 of her 25 free throws.

(a) Who has the better shooting record? Show how you know.
Ava: \dfrac{13}{20}=0.65
Noor: \dfrac{16}{25}=0.64
0.65>0.64, so Ava has the better shooting record.
(b) Noor says she is better because she made more shots. Is she right? Explain.
No. Noor also took more shots. To be fair, compare the fraction of shots made, not the number made. Ava made a bigger fraction of her shots.

Think: out of 100 shots, Ava’s record is like making 65 and Noor’s like making 64 (\dfrac{13}{20}=\dfrac{65}{100}, \dfrac{16}{25}=\dfrac{64}{100}). That is Method 1 with a denominator of 100.

A quick estimate: more or less than one half?

A fraction is more than \dfrac12 when its top is more than half of its bottom. This is a fast way to compare, and to check an answer. Decide first, then tap a card.

\dfrac{5}{12}
Less. Half of 12 is 6, and 5 < 6.
\dfrac{7}{12}
More. Half of 12 is 6, and 7 > 6.
\dfrac{4}{9}
Less. Half of 9 is 4.5, and 4 < 4.5.
\dfrac{6}{11}
More. Half of 11 is 5.5, and 6 > 5.5.

Think: so \dfrac{5}{12}<\dfrac{6}{11} without any work. One is below one half and the other is above it.

5

A Fraction of an Amount

In math, “of” usually means multiply. To find \dfrac34 of 24, split 24 into 4 equal groups, then take 3 of them.

Fraction of an amount
1Divide the amount by the denominator.
2Multiply by the numerator.

\dfrac34 \text{ of } 24=24\div4\times3=18

On the calculator: 24×3÷4ENTER or 3A b/c4×24ENTER. Both give 18.
✓
Is it reasonable? A fraction less than 1 of an amount is always less than the amount. \dfrac34 is more than half, so \dfrac34 of 24 should be more than 12 and less than 24. For money, round to the nearest cent at the end.

Think: a quick way to find \dfrac14 of an amount is to halve it twice: 52 → 26 → 13. And \dfrac34 is what is left: 52-13=39.

Example 10

Dana’s gas tank

Dana’s car has a 52 L gas tank. The gas gauge shows that the tank is \dfrac14 full.

(a) How many litres of gas are in the tank?
52\div4\times1=13 L
There are 13 L of gas in the tank.
(b) How many litres will it take to fill the tank?
52-13=39 L
(or \dfrac34 of 52 = 39 L)
It takes 39 L to fill it.
(c) Gas costs $1.45/L. How much will it cost to fill the tank?
39\times1.45=56.55
It costs $56.55 to fill the tank.
(Estimate: 40\times1.5=60.)
✓

Key ideas

A fraction is part of a whole or a set. Bottom: how many equal parts. Top: how many you have. Equivalent fractions: multiply or divide top and bottom by the same number (never add).
Simplest form: divide top and bottom by the GCF (or by any common factor, again and again, until only 1 is left). Check with A b/c.
The bar means divide: \dfrac38=3\div8=0.375. Compare with a common denominator or with decimals. Bigger denominator, smaller pieces.
A fraction of an amount: divide by the bottom, multiply by the top. \dfrac34 of 24 is 18. Round money at the end.
