Warm-up

The Missing Dollar

Three friends rent a bowling lane for $30. Each friend pays $10.

The lane is on sale today for $25, so the manager sends $5 back. The worker gives each friend $1 and keeps $2.

Now each friend paid $9: 3 × $9 = $27. Add the worker’s $2: $27 + $2 = $29.

Where did the missing dollar go?

?
Got an idea? Ask: what does each number count?
No dollar is missing. The adding is the trick! The friends paid $27 in all: $25 is in the till and $2 is with the worker (\$25+\$2=\$27). Adding the worker’s $2 to the $27 counts it twice. The right check: \$27 paid +\ \$3 given back =\$30 ✓. Today, watch what every number in a sale really counts.

On Sale! Discounts and Sale Prices

“30% off!” “$25 off!” “Buy 2, get 1 free!” Today we find the discount and the sale price, the percent off, the tax on a sale price, and which sale is really the best deal. We also learn to spot a fake deal.

I can…
  • find the discount and the sale price, and use the shortcut: multiply by the percent you pay
  • find the percent decrease (the percent off) from the regular price and the sale price
  • find the total cost of a sale item with tax
  • compare promotions like 30% off, $25 off and “buy 2, get 1 free”
  • explain why two discounts do not add, and check if a sale is really a deal
N1 Solve problems that involve unit pricing and currency exchange, using proportional reasoning.

Discount and Sale Price

Regular price(or original price) the price before the sale
Discountthe amount of money you save
Discount ratethe percent off
Sale pricewhat you pay

Stores also call a discount a markdown (the opposite of a markup).

Key idea   Discount = regular price × discount rate   ·   Sale price = regular price − discount
!
Watch out. Change the percent to a decimal first: 30% = 0.30, not 30.
0.30 × 139.99 ENTER shows 41.997. Round to the cent: $42.00.
Example 1

A winter jacket

A winter jacket at a Calgary mall is regularly $139.99. It is on sale for 30% off.

(a) Estimate the discount and the sale price.
$139.99 is about $140.
10% of $140 is $14, so 30% is about 3\times14=\$42.
Sale price about 140-42=\$98.
(b) Calculate the discount.
30\%=0.30
0.30\times139.99=41.997
Discount = $42.00
(c) Calculate the sale price.
139.99-42.00=\$97.99
The jacket costs $97.99 on sale. (Close to the estimate of $98.)

The shortcut: multiply by the percent you pay

If you save 30%, you pay 100\%-30\%=70\% of the price. So you can find the sale price in one step.

Shortcut   Sale price = regular price × (1 − discount rate)
30% off: you pay 70%, so multiply by 0.70.
!
Watch out. Multiply by the part you pay: 30% off means × 0.70, not × 0.30. (× 0.30 gives the discount.)
For 40% off $124.50: 124.50 × 0.6 ENTER. Round to the cent only at the end.
Think A $100 item is 30% off. Find the sale price both ways (discount first, then the shortcut). Why do you get the same answer?
Discount: 0.30\times100=\$30, sale price 100-30=\$70. Shortcut: 100\times0.70=\$70. Taking away 30 of every 100 leaves 70 of every 100: that is 70%.

Example 3

Discounts on a bill

(a) A carpet cleaning company charges $89.00 per room. If you book 4 or more rooms, you get 12% off the whole bill. What do 5 rooms cost (before tax)?
Regular bill: 5\times89.00=\$445.00
Pay 88%: 445.00\times0.88=\$391.60
5 rooms cost $391.60.
(b) During “Tool Days”, a hardware store has hand tools 20% off and power tools 15% off. Ali buys a hammer ($24.99) and a drill ($149.00). What is his total before tax?
Hammer (pay 80%): 24.99\times0.80=19.992
Drill (pay 85%): 149.00\times0.85=126.65
Total: 19.992+126.65=146.642
Ali pays $146.64 before tax.

Percent Decrease: How Much Off Was It?

Sometimes you know the regular price and the sale price, and you want the percent off. This is a percent decrease. It works just like percent increase from Lesson 8, but the price goes down.

Key idea   \text{Percent decrease}=\dfrac{\text{amount of decrease}}{\text{original price}}\times100
where amount of decrease = original price − new price
!
Watch out. Always divide by the original (regular) price, not the sale price. The percent is “out of” what it used to cost.
2600 ÷ 18500 × 100 ENTER shows 14.054…, so about 14.1%.

Why divide by the original price?

The percent off is a percent of the regular price. Dividing by the sale price gives a percent that doesn’t check out.

Think A price drops from $50 to $25. Later it goes back up from $25 to $50. Is the percent the same both times?
No. Down: \dfrac{25}{50}\times100=50\%. Up: \dfrac{25}{25}\times100=100\%. Each time you divide by the price you started from.
Example 4

Find the percent decrease

(a) A gaming headset goes from $80.00 to $60.00.
Step 1: decrease =80.00-60.00=\$20.00
Step 2: \dfrac{20.00}{80.00}\times100=25\%
The price dropped by 25%.
(b) A used truck is listed for $18 500 but sells for $15 900. (Round to the nearest tenth of a percent.)
Step 1: decrease =18\,500-15\,900=\$2600
Step 2: \dfrac{2600}{18\,500}\times100=14.05\ldots
The price dropped by about 14.1%.

Sale Price, Then Tax

Sales tax is charged on the sale price, the price you actually pay, not the regular price. So take the discount off first, then add the tax. In Alberta that is 5% GST, so multiply by 1.05. In other provinces use the rate from Lesson 8 (BC: 5% GST + 7% PST = 12%; Ontario: 13% HST; Nova Scotia: 14% HST; rates can change).

Key idea   Total cost = regular price × (1 − discount rate) × (1 + tax rate)
Example: 35% off, then 5% GST:   regular price × 0.65 × 1.05
You can do it in one line: 84 × 0.65 × 1.05 ENTER. Then round to the cent.
Example 5

A blender in Calgary

In Calgary, a blender is regularly $84.00. It is on sale for 35% off. GST is 5%.

(a) Find the sale price.
84.00\times0.65=\$54.60
(b) Find the GST.
54.60\times0.05=\$2.73
(c) Find the total cost.
54.60+2.73=\$57.33
(Check: 84\times0.65\times1.05=57.33)
Example 6

A tent in Kelowna

On a trip to Kelowna, BC, Priya buys a tent that is regularly $260.00. It is 25% off. BC charges 5% GST and 7% PST.

(a) What is the sale price?
260.00\times0.75=\$195.00
(b) What is her total cost with taxes?
Tax: 5% + 7% = 12%
195.00\times1.12=\$218.40
(c) What would she pay with taxes at the regular price? How much does she save in total?
260.00\times1.12=\$291.20
Savings: 291.20-218.40=\$72.80
(d) What percent of the regular total does she save?
\dfrac{72.80}{291.20}\times100=25\%
She saves 25%, the same as the discount rate. Tax does not change the percent saved.
Think Why is the percent saved in (d) exactly the same as the discount rate?
Both totals are multiplied by the same 1.12. The sale total is 75% of $260 × 1.12, so it is 75% of the regular total. She saves the other 25%.

Comparing Promotions

A promotion is anything a store does to get you to buy: a percent off, dollars off, “buy one, get one” (BOGO), “buy 2, get 1 free”, and so on. To compare promotions, work out what you would really pay, or the percent you would save.

Key idea   \text{Percent saved}=\dfrac{\text{amount saved}}{\text{regular price of everything you take home}}\times100
Example 7

30% off or $25 off?

One store offers 30% OFF and another offers $25 OFF any item. Which promotion gives the lower price?

(a) Jeans, regularly $60.00
30%: 0.30\times60=\$18 off, pay $42.00
$25 off: pay $35.00
$25 off is better.
(b) A jacket, regularly $120.00
30%: 0.30\times120=\$36 off, pay $84.00
$25 off: pay $95.00
30% off is better.
(c) Boots, regularly $85.00
30%: 0.30\times85=\$25.50 off, pay $59.50
$25 off: pay $60.00
30% off is better, by $0.50.
When are the two deals equal?
When 30% of the price is $25: 25\div0.30\approx\$83.33. Below that, take $25 off; above it, take 30% off.

Try any price: 30% off or $25 off?

Slide the regular price. Watch which sign wins, and where they swap.

Buy one, get one: what do you take home?

For a multi-buy deal, the percent saved is out of the regular price of everything you take home, the free shirt too.

?
Is it a deal for you? The biggest percent is not always the best choice. Ask: Do I need that many? Will it spoil or go out of style? Do I have room to store it? Is the store close, with a good return policy?
Example 8

T-shirt deals

T-shirts are regularly $22.00 each at two stores.

Store A: Buy one, get the second half price
Store B: Buy 2, get 1 free
(a) At Store A, find the cost of 2 shirts and the percent saved.
Pay: 22.00+11.00=\$33.00
Regular: 2\times22.00=\$44.00; saved $11.00
\dfrac{11.00}{44.00}\times100=25\%
(b) At Store B, find the cost of 3 shirts and the percent saved.
Pay for 2: 2\times22.00=\$44.00
Regular: 3\times22.00=\$66.00; saved $22.00
\dfrac{22.00}{66.00}\times100\approx33.3\%
(c) Which is the better deal? Give a reason that is about more than the price.
Store B saves a bigger percent (33.3% vs 25%), so it is the better deal if you want 3 shirts. If you only need 2, Store A is better: you spend $33.00 instead of $44.00, and you don’t buy a shirt you don’t need.

Discount on a Discount

Some sales take an extra percent off a price that is already on sale. The second discount is taken from the lower sale price, so the two percents do not add. Multiply by each “percent you pay”, one after the other.

Two discounts   final price = regular price × (1 − first rate) × (1 − second rate)
20% off, then an extra 10% off: 0.80\times0.90=0.72. You pay 72%, so you save 28%, not 30%.
Both discounts in one line: 150 × 0.8 × 0.9 ENTER shows 108.
Think Is “20% off, then an extra 10% off” the same as “10% off, then an extra 20% off”?
Yes. 0.80\times0.90=0.72 and 0.90\times0.80=0.72. Either way you pay 72% and save 28%.
Example 9

Hiking boots: an extra 10% off

Hiking boots are regularly $150.00. They are 20% off, and on Saturday you get an extra 10% off at the till.

(a) Find the final price.
After 20% off: 150.00\times0.80=\$120.00
After the extra 10% off: 120.00\times0.90=\$108.00
(b) What is the total percent off? Is it the same as 30% off?
Saved: 150.00-108.00=\$42.00
\dfrac{42.00}{150.00}\times100=28\%
No. 30% off would be 150\times0.70=\$105.00.

“Is it really a deal?”

A big “% off” sign does not always mean a good price. Some stores raise the price before a sale, so the sale price is close to, or even more than, the old price. Compare the sale price with what the item used to cost.

Example 10

The Black Friday TV

A TV was $500.00 in October. The week before a “Black Friday” sale, the store raised its price by 20%. Then it advertised 15% OFF!

(a) What was the raised price?
500.00\times1.20=\$600.00
(b) What is the sale price?
600.00\times0.85=\$510.00
(c) Is it really a deal? Find the percent change from the October price.
510.00-500.00=\$10.00 more
\dfrac{10.00}{500.00}\times100=2\%
No. The “sale” price is 2% higher than in October.
✓

Key ideas

Discount = regular price × rate. Sale price = regular price − discount. Shortcut: multiply by the percent you pay (30% off → × 0.70).
Percent decrease = decrease ÷ original price × 100. Always divide by what it used to cost.
Sale, then tax: regular price × (1 − discount rate) × (1 + tax rate). In Alberta: × 1.05 for GST.
Compare deals by what you really pay or the percent saved. Two discounts multiply, they don’t add. Check the old price: is it really a deal?
